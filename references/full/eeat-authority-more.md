### 1. A knowledge panel reflects a confidence score, not trust  `09.1`
*useful · content insights · source 09*

A common misconception is that a knowledge panel represents some special "trust" Google has extended to a person or brand — e.g. the idea that publishing one article can make Google instantly "recognize" someone. The mechanism described instead is a "confidence score": a measure of how confident Google is that the specific person you searched for is the same entity represented in a given knowledge panel, i.e. an identity-matching score, not a quality or trust rating. This is contrasted against a secondhand anecdote (someone claiming Google "recognized" their article right away) that the speaker explicitly doubts applies.

> "confidence score means how much Google thinks"

**Evidence:** Contrasted against a secondhand anecdote of someone claiming instant Google "recognition" after publishing an article, which the speaker says he doesn't believe applies.

**Apply at Pabau:** When pursuing a knowledge panel for a Pabau executive or the brand itself, focus on strengthening identity-matching signals (consistent naming, sameAs links, schema) rather than assuming that publishing more "trustworthy" content alone will trigger panel creation.

**Apply anywhere:** When pursuing a knowledge panel for an executive or the brand itself, focus on strengthening identity-matching signals (consistent naming, sameAs links, schema) rather than assuming that publishing more "trustworthy" content alone will trigger panel creation.

### 2. A specific condition page can outrank the CDC and WebMD  `128.20`
*useful · general insights · source 128*

Grow and Convert rank first, with the featured snippet, for the term "multiple concussions" for their client Cognitive FX, competing against the CDC, WebMD, Brainline and other government sites. This is worth recording because health is the most authority-weighted vertical there is, and the received wisdom is that a small clinic cannot compete with government and major medical publishers. The mechanism is the rest of their process rather than anything special: a specific rather than general angle, an hour with a genuine subject-matter expert who guides the piece, detail behind every claim, and links built once the page proves it can rank unaided. Large institutional sites publish broad overview pages on conditions. A specialist practice can publish the specific, detailed answer to the specific query, and win it.

> "Competing against sites like the CDC, WebMD"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX ranks number one with the featured snippet for "multiple concussions" against the CDC, WebMD, Brainline and government sites.

**How to do it**

1. List the specific conditions, complications and procedures your practice genuinely specializes in.
2. Check each SERP for whether the ranking pages are broad institutional overviews rather than specific answers.
3. Pick terms where the query is specific and the ranking answers are generic, which is where institutional authority is beatable.
4. Interview the practitioner who treats that condition for a full hour and build the page from their framing.
5. Answer the exact query in the first sentence or two, since the featured snippet is available on these terms.
6. Back every clinical claim with a citation and name the credentialed author on the page.
7. Add the keyword to a rank tracker at publish and wait a few weeks for the position to settle.
8. Build links only once the page is already inside the top 30 unaided.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Picking broad condition head terms. Institutional sites genuinely do win those on authority, so the beatable set is narrower and more specific queries where the ranking pages are generic overviews.

**Apply at Pabau:** This is direct evidence Pabau's practice customers can rank for specific treatment and complication queries against large medical publishers, which makes specificity plus a named practitioner author the pitch for Pabau's own condition and treatment content.

**Apply anywhere:** Specific, expert-led condition pages can outrank government and major medical publishers, because those sites publish broad overviews while the query is specific. Pick specific queries where the ranking answers are generic, build the page from an hour with the treating practitioner, answer in the first sentence, and cite every claim.

### 3. Audit the About page because raters are told to check it  `69.11`
*useful · general insights · source 69*

Smarty gives the origin of the About page's status in SEO. About pages were not something SEOs paid attention to until Google released quality rater guidelines instructing their raters to check the pages when assessing the trustworthiness of websites found in organic search. Since then, the About page has become one of the most common checks in SEO audits. The point for practice is that the page now carries two independent audiences with the same requirement. Human raters check it for trustworthiness evidence, and generative AI platforms check it to decide whether a brand is worth recommending. Both want checkable facts, so one rewrite serves both.

> "instructing their raters to check the pages"

**Evidence:** Smarty traces the About page's rise in SEO audits directly to Google's quality rater guidelines instructing raters to check them for trustworthiness.

**How to do it**

1. Add an About page check to your standard site audit template, alongside titles and internal links.
2. Score it on four things: named people, verifiable claims, contact details, and a visible last-updated date.
3. Flag any About page that is a single paragraph of mission copy as a fail.
4. Check the same facts appear on the team, contact and privacy pages so the site tells one story.
5. Re-audit whenever the company's size, funding, leadership or certifications change.
6. Treat the same rewrite as serving both quality raters and AI recommendation checks rather than running two workstreams.

**Pitfall:** Auditing the About page once at launch and never again. Stale customer counts and departed leadership on the page are worse than no numbers, because they are checkable and wrong.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should add an About page check to the standard site audit, scoring named leadership, dated claims, contact details and certification currency, and re-run it whenever the company changes.

**Apply anywhere:** Put the About page in your standard audit template and score it on named people, verifiable dated claims, contact details and freshness, since both quality raters and AI systems check it for trustworthiness.

### 4. Authority reverses recruiting so experts apply to you  `171.12`
*useful · general insights · source 171*

Sol Orwell describes the endgame he had in mind the whole time. Once Examine was the authority, people wanted to work with them rather than needing to be recruited. He gives the number: a hiring call for site contributors produced over 500 PhDs willing to work with them, against an audience of roughly 2M monthly visitors and an email list in the low hundreds of thousands. His phrasing is that the mindset is to create authority so that people cannot escape you, and then they want to work with you. The point is that brand authority is not only a demand-side asset. It compounds on the supply side by cutting the cost and raising the quality of the expertise you can put into the content.

> "we got over 500 PhDs to work with us"

**Evidence:** Examine's contributor hiring call drew over 500 PhDs; the site had roughly 2M monthly visitors and an email list of a couple of hundred thousand.

**How to do it**

1. Treat expert contributor supply as an output of brand-building, not a separate recruiting problem.
2. Publish an open call for contributors on your own site and list once the audience is large enough to notice it.
3. State what contributors get: audience size, byline, and the standing of the publication.
4. Select on credentials and writing quality, since a large applicant pool lets you be strict.
5. Attribute every contribution to a named person with their qualifications, which feeds the authority that produced the pool.
6. Re-run the call periodically rather than treating it as a one-time hire.

**Pitfall:** Running the open call before the audience exists. With no visible authority the call attracts content mills rather than qualified experts, and publishing their work damages the authority you were trying to build.

**Apply at Pabau:** As Pabau's blog authority grows, David can run an open call for practicing clinicians and practice managers to contribute, which fixes the named-author and first-hand-experience requirement on medical and aesthetic topics more cheaply than commissioning.

**Apply anywhere:** Once your publication has real audience, run an open call for expert contributors. Authority reverses recruiting, and a large applicant pool lets you insist on named, credentialed authors, which in turn feeds the authority.

### 5. Back every SEO page claim with high-DR sources and short video  `13.8`
*useful · best practices · source 13*

Irwin's trust checklist for SEO and landing pages: every claim needs proof (facts, figures, stats), not assertion, and any sourcing or citing should point to high-domain-rating, niche-relevant, authoritative sites, since linking to an authoritative external source lends the page credibility by association in his framing of how Google evaluates it. Clickable, genuinely useful tool links also register as positive user-engagement signals. Separately, short videos kept to one to two minutes are described as a deliberate lever for dwell time — a visitor staying on-page two minutes to watch a video signals to Google or Analytics that the content is satisfying, making brief embedded video a repeatable tactic for pages needing a dwell-time or trust boost rather than just a content-format choice.

> "correct sourcing from high-DR places usually will not just help"

**How to do it**

1. Before publishing, list every factual claim or statistic in the draft article and confirm each has a citation, not just an assertion.
2. For each citation, check the source domain's authority (inferred: use Ahrefs/Semrush Domain Rating or DataForSEO domain metrics) and confirm it is niche-relevant to the topic, not just any convenient link.
3. Replace any low-authority or off-topic citation with a high-DR, niche-relevant source before the article ships.
4. Where the page links to a tool or interactive resource, confirm the link is genuinely clickable and functional, since working tool links register as engagement signals.
5. On pages with weak dwell time or trust (inferred: check GSC average time-on-page or Analytics engagement rate), add one short video of one to two minutes summarizing or demonstrating the article's content.
6. Re-check the published page after edits to confirm every claim has a visible source and the video loads and plays correctly on mobile.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Google Search Console, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Citing a source just because it is convenient rather than high-DR and niche-relevant undercuts the credibility-by-association effect Irwin describes — a weak or off-topic source can do more harm than no source.

### 6. Between same-name entities Google weighs fame, relevance then confidence  `73.19`
*useful · content insights · source 73*

Asked whether same-name competition is a click-through-rate problem, Barnard says zero. He gives three factors instead. First, famousness: if someone is famous, like Robert Smith of The Cure, his panel always shows and as another Robert Smith you would struggle to beat him. Second, when two entities are roughly equal in famousness and both have panels, relevance and personalization decide - if the searcher is obviously interested in circuses, Google shows Jason Barnard the circus clown rather than Jason Barnard the digital brand engineer. Third, at equal notability, confidence wins: if Google is more confident in the information it holds about one entity, it shows that one. This makes confidence the lever you control. Barnard also frames disambiguation as two jobs the machine must do at once - separate every person sharing the name, and merge the multiple facets that belong to one of them, since all of us have several.

> "it's famousness on a much lower level"

**Evidence:** Barnard's worked examples: Robert Smith of The Cure as the unbeatable famous case, and Jason Barnard the circus clown being served to a searcher interested in circuses.

**How to do it**

1. Search your own name or brand and list every other entity sharing it, plus the facets each is known for.
2. Judge whether any of them is famous enough that you cannot displace them, and plan around that rather than fighting it.
3. For rivals at similar notability, raise confidence rather than chasing clicks: consistent facts, consistent descriptions, corroborating links.
4. Bind each of your own facets explicitly to your name in text, so the machine merges them into one entity rather than splitting them.
5. State the distinctions between you and the namesakes in indexable copy, naming the other entity's field.
6. Test the brand query logged in and logged out to see how personalization changes which entity is served.
7. Track the KGMID and confidence score over time as the measure of progress.

**Tools:** Kalicube Pro

**Pitfall:** Trying to win a same-name battle by driving clicks. Barnard rates click-through rate at zero for this; the deciding signals are fame, personalized relevance and Google's confidence in your facts.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's name is close to unique, so the same-name risk is low. The transferable action is the facet-merging half: make sure Pabau's blog, template and code-reference sections are all bound to the same brand entity in text.

**Apply anywhere:** When a namesake outranks you, stop chasing clicks. Fame you cannot change, relevance is personalized, and confidence is the lever you control through consistent facts and corroborating links. Also bind your own facets to one name.

### 7. Calculate whether to fix or swap review platforms  `40.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 40*

Kasra's rule for deciding whether to rehabilitate a damaged review platform or abandon it for a new one is pure arithmetic: estimate how many months of new reviews it would take to pull the average rating back up on the existing platform, versus how quickly a brand-new profile on an alternative platform could accumulate enough volume to rank and get cited in AI Overviews. In one case, a client had 1,035 Trustpilot reviews with 250 negative, dragging the score from 4.6 to 3.7; recovering in place was estimated at six months, versus roughly two weeks to get a new Feefo profile ranking and cited. In a more extreme case - 33,000 Trustpilot reviews with 15,000 one-star - Kasra treats the platform as effectively unsalvageable and recommends ranking a replacement asset above it rather than trying to fix it. His standing advice: run this comparison whenever a platform's negative-review share climbs, and run both a short-term swap and a long-term in-place recovery simultaneously.

> "we did the math and figured we could do it"

**How to do it**

1. Pull your current review platform's total review count and the count or percentage of negative (1-2 star) reviews.
2. Estimate, based on your current organic review-request rate, how many months it would take to move your average rating back above a healthy threshold such as 4.0 purely by adding new positive reviews.
3. Separately estimate how long it would take a brand-new profile on an alternative platform to accumulate enough reviews to rank and get cited in AI Overviews for your brand plus 'reviews' query.
4. If the fix-in-place timeline is much longer than the swap timeline, treat the existing platform as effectively burnt and prioritize the swap.
5. Run both tracks simultaneously: push new review volume onto the fresh platform as the short-term fix while continuing to slowly solicit positive reviews on the original platform as the long-term fix.
6. Re-run this same volume and percentage calculation any time a review platform's negative percentage climbs, rather than waiting until it is overwhelmingly negative.

**Pitfall:** Don't try to fix a review platform once negative reviews have overwhelmed it if the math says recovery would take many months - past a certain negative-review threshold you're better off ranking a fresh asset above the damaged one rather than fighting to rehabilitate it in place.

### 8. Change the reality behind sentiment, because public sentiment is sticky  `76.20`
*useful · general insights · source 76*

The paper's closing analysis treats AEO as an adversarial contest between model developers and marketers, fought in two categories: reviews and validation. Developers want detailed accurate reviews and want to validate that information comes from reputable sources. Marketing specialists are incentivized to bias those reviews favorably and inflate their credibility. The authors propose two equilibria. First, companies align public web sentiment with their desired brand image, which removes the incentive to manipulate because the image already matches the reviews. They flag the difficulty honestly, citing Schultz et al. that public sentiment toward companies is sticky because cognitive biases resist change, while citing Benoit that firms willing to invest and take the necessary steps can still accomplish it. Second, developers adopt validation systems resistant to brand manipulation, such as an independent third-party reviewer platform, though the authors note no such system currently exists.

> "as people's cognitive biases resist change"

**Evidence:** Schultz et al. on the stickiness of public sentiment toward companies; Benoit cited for the counterpoint that determined firms can still shift it.

**Pitfall:** Running a repositioning campaign and expecting sentiment to follow within a quarter. Stickiness means the old characterization persists in the corpus and keeps being synthesized long after the messaging changed.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau wants engines to describe it differently, change what customers and press actually say first, then expect a long lag before the synthesized description follows.

**Apply anywhere:** If you want engines to describe you differently, change what customers and press actually say first, then expect a long lag before the synthesized description follows.

### 9. Check Google Trends to confirm your name is a topic  `09.9`
*useful · concrete actions · source 09*

Typing a name or brand into Google Trends reveals whether Google treats it as a disambiguated "topic" (an entity) versus a plain "search term" — the same distinction Trends makes between an ambiguous keyword and a specific movie or entity. If your name/brand surfaces as a selectable topic, that's confirmation Google has established it as a recognized entity; the Compare feature can then benchmark your topic's relative search interest against another established entity in your space (the source's own example: comparing himself to Neil Patel) to gauge relative traction.

> "there's a search term, and here it's a topic"

**How to do it**

1. Go to Google Trends and start typing your name or brand name into the search/comparison field.
2. Review the suggestions Trends offers, checking whether your name appears only as a plain "search term" or also as a disambiguated "topic."
3. If your name/brand appears as a selectable topic, treat that as confirmation Google has established it as a recognized entity.
4. Use the Compare feature to benchmark your topic's relative search interest against another recognized entity in your space.
5. Repeat this check periodically as you build out schema and branded search volume elsewhere, to track whether entity status is strengthening over time. (inferred)

**Tools:** Google Trends

### 10. Check the knowledge graph for a KGMID before assuming no panel  `73.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 73*

The host reported that Salesforce and Moz showed no knowledge panel while HubSpot, Ahrefs, Canva and Unbounce did. Barnard corrects the inference: not seeing a panel does not mean the entity does not have one. Google decides per query what a great result looks like, and the knowledge panel is one optional module alongside videos, images, news and a Twitter feed - it can choose not to show it. Being in the knowledge graph and having a knowledge panel are also separate states. You can be in the graph without the panel showing, and you can have a panel without being in the main graph, because there are multiple knowledge graphs - Google Maps is one, Google Books is another. The thing you actually need is to be in the knowledge graph, identified by a KGMID, the unique identifier that means Google has understood you and is confident about it. He offers three reasons a panel might be suppressed: too few elements to be worth the space, insufficient confidence that the query means that company, or an ongoing knowledge graph update.

> "does not mean to say they don't have the knowledge panel"

**Evidence:** Barnard confirms Moz's knowledge panel does not appear in his location either, then produces the panel link directly, showing it exists but is not being displayed.

**How to do it**

1. Search the brand name in Google and note whether a panel renders, but treat that as a display observation only.
2. Look the name up in a knowledge graph explorer to check whether a KGMID exists for the entity.
3. If a KGMID exists but no panel shows, check whether the panel has too few elements to be worth displaying.
4. If the panel is sparse, add and corroborate more fundamental facts rather than assuming the entity is missing.
5. Test the query in more than one location, since panel display varies by where the search happens.
6. Rule out a knowledge graph update before reacting to a panel disappearing.
7. Re-check in a week before treating a missing panel as a real loss.

**Tools:** Kalicube Pro

**Pitfall:** Reading a missing panel as a missing entity and restarting entity work from scratch. The panel is one optional SERP module and Google can decline to show it while the KGMID is perfectly healthy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before any Pabau knowledge panel work, check whether a KGMID already exists for Pabau. If it does, the job is enriching and corroborating facts, not building an entity from zero.

**Apply anywhere:** Never conclude an entity has no knowledge panel from a SERP that does not show one. Look up the KGMID in a knowledge graph explorer, and if it exists, treat sparse facts or an ongoing graph update as the reason for suppression.

### 11. Check your brand's official Google Knowledge Graph confidence score  `26.8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 26*

Google maintains a 'confidence score' for every entity in its Knowledge Graph, retrievable for free via the Knowledge Graph Search API documented at developers.google.com — unlike Ahrefs DR, Moz DA, or Semrush toxicity score, this number comes directly from Google itself and, per Dooley, is still actively updated even though classic PageRank no longer is. Third-party tools such as Dennis Yu's BlitzMetrics and Jason Barnard's Kalicube wrap the same underlying API to show a friendlier view of the Knowledge Graph Machine ID (KGMID) without needing to query it directly.

> "gives you the Knowledge Graph machine ID"

**How to do it**

1. Go to developers.google.com and locate the Knowledge Graph Search API documentation.
2. Create a Google Cloud project and enable the Knowledge Graph Search API, generating a free API key. (inferred)
3. Query the API with the brand name or founder's name as the search parameter.
4. Read the 'resultScore' field in the returned JSON — this is the entity's confidence score.
5. Note the KGMID if one is returned; no KGMID means the entity isn't yet established in the Knowledge Graph.
6. Cross-check the same entity via a friendlier third-party interface, such as Kalicube or BlitzMetrics, to validate the reading.
7. If competing for shared naming or branded search terms, look up the competing entity's score too and treat it as the number to beat.
8. Re-check the score quarterly to see whether entity-building work (press, citations, schema) is raising it over time. (inferred)

**Tools:** Google Knowledge Graph Search API, Kalicube, BlitzMetrics

**Pitfall:** Don't substitute third-party authority proxies (DR, DA, toxicity scores) for this number — those are outside estimates, whereas the Knowledge Graph confidence score is the actual metric Google itself maintains and, by Dooley's account, keeps updating, which is evidence it's still functionally in use.

### 12. Claim your knowledge panel with ID verification  `09.5`
*useful · concrete actions · source 09*

To claim a knowledge panel, first confirm the entry is unambiguously about you (all shown results actually refer to you, not a namesake or unrelated entity), then click "Claim this knowledge panel" and "Get verified," confirming "yes, it's me." Verification requires holding up a passport or ID card to the camera to be verified online, after which a control panel becomes available (the source reports access within about an hour) where you can suggest a new photo, edit information, propose a name change, or request additions — each submission returns a "thanks for your feedback" confirmation with updates typically processed in roughly an hour.

> "you'll hold up your passport or ID card to be verified"

**How to do it**

1. Locate the knowledge panel entry that is unambiguously about you or your brand, confirming all shown results actually refer to you.
2. Click "Claim this knowledge panel" on that entry.
3. Click "Get verified" and confirm "yes, it's me" to begin the verification flow.
4. Complete identity verification by holding up a passport or ID card to the camera as prompted.
5. Wait for control-panel access, which the source reports typically arrives within about an hour.
6. Inside the control panel, suggest edits: a new photo, an information edit or change, a name change, or a request to add missing information.
7. Submit each suggested edit and expect a confirmation message, with updates typically processed in about an hour per the source's experience.

**Tools:** Google Knowledge Panel

**Pitfall:** Trying to claim a panel that isn't clearly and unambiguously about you (e.g. one mixed with a more famous namesake's results) instead of first confirming every visible result actually refers to you.

### 13. Discount award badges and percentage stats as agency proof  `178.11`
*useful · best practices · source 178*

Grow and Convert list the proof substitutes agencies reach for when they have no published results: badges claiming award-winning digital marketing services, quotes from past clients, and isolated statistics such as we grew company X's organic traffic by 200%. They call these testimonials and unsupported claims unreliable proof, because none of them carry the method. Without published results plus an explanation of how those results were achieved, the buyer is blindly trusting a competency claim, which they say is why experiences with agencies are so inconsistent. The rule they derive is a ratio: the more transparent an agency is about both the result and the route to it, the more you can trust the result is repeatable for you. Theory and advice with no data behind it counts as marketing, not evidence.

> "These types of testimonials and unsupported claims"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say it is very common for content companies to share theoretical advice with little or no data or case studies backing it.

**How to do it**

1. Sort every proof element on the agency site into two piles: results with method, and everything else.
2. Put badges, award claims and client quotes in the second pile and give them zero weight.
3. Treat a bare percentage with no article count, timeframe or keyword list as the second pile too.
4. Ask for one written case study that names the client, the pages, the keywords and the timeline.
5. If none exists, ask why, and treat the answer as the data point.
6. Apply the same standard to your own marketing before you criticize theirs.

**Pitfall:** Weighing a wall of client logos as evidence. Logos prove someone paid the invoice, not that the work produced a result, and they are the cheapest thing on the page to obtain.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's own proof pages should follow the same standard: publish the practice, the workflow changed and the measured outcome, rather than star ratings and generic testimonials.

**Apply anywhere:** Give zero weight to award badges, logo walls and bare percentages. Only a published result with the method behind it predicts whether the agency can repeat it for you.

### 15. E-E-A-T is really an old 'machine trust signal' concept, and it's niche-specific  `35.4`
*useful · content insights · source 35*

Charles reframes E-E-A-T as SEO-industry jargon for a much older and broader idea — computer trust signals and machine verification of whether something is a 'real entity' — that predates the SEO industry entirely (he traces the underlying concept to the 1980s). He argues AI models weight different trust vectors by niche: a local service business (his example, a landscape gardener) gets weighted heavily on local reviews, local newspaper coverage, and charity sponsorships, while niches where money pages often have little content of their own (his examples: online slots/casino sites, adult sites) get weighted instead on press releases, company updates, a visible changelog, active developers, an active community, and customer reviews as substitute authenticity signals.

> "it all just goes back to computer trust signals"

**Evidence:** Direct claim: E-E-A-T 'is more of a jargon term that was created by the SEO industry, whereas it all just goes back to computer trust signals — machine verification... existed in the 1980s.' Supporting example: local landscape gardener queries get weighted toward 'local reviews for that area, local newspaper testimony, sponsorships of local charities,' while contentless money-page niches get weighted toward 'press releases, company updates, a change log, devs, a community, active social media, customer reviews.'

**Apply at Pabau:** When building Pabau's trust signals for AI visibility, don't apply a single generic E-E-A-T checklist — identify which specific trust vectors AI models actually weight for B2B SaaS/healthtech software buyers (likely case studies, third-party reviews, product-update transparency, and named experts) and prioritize those over generic author-bio-style E-E-A-T box-ticking.

**Apply anywhere:** When building trust signals for AI visibility, don't apply a single generic E-E-A-T checklist — identify which specific trust vectors AI models actually weight for buyers in your category (often case studies, third-party reviews, product-update transparency, and named experts) and prioritize those over generic author-bio-style E-E-A-T box-ticking.

### 16. Expect knowledge panels to become AI-generated and less stable  `73.20`
*useful · general insights · source 73*

Barnard's caveat on the whole topic: today's knowledge panels are built from human-defined templates, and as Google moves to AI he thinks it would be foolish to keep that system. So panels will become AI generated and much more dynamic, which also makes them less reliable in what they contain, how they are presented and what information they pull. The knowledge graph, by contrast, is very stable, which is why he says the actual goal is getting into the knowledge graph rather than getting the panel. He also flags a migration: Google is moving from the current knowledge graph, which was built on something like an equivalent of Wikidata, to an enterprise knowledge graph that is more big-data in character and whose contents you will be less able to predict. His conclusion is still to get a panel today, knowing today's panels will die out and be replaced by something that still looks like one - he has already seen the format in ChatGPT, with photos, a description and videos.

> "knowledge panels are built from templates that are human defined"

**Evidence:** Barnard reports already seeing panel-equivalent cards in ChatGPT with photos, a description and videos, and describes Google's migration to an enterprise knowledge graph.

**How to do it**

1. Set the goal as knowledge graph inclusion with a KGMID, not the visible panel, since the graph is the stable layer.
2. Keep pursuing a panel now, but plan for the format and contents to change within a year or two.
3. Do not build processes that depend on a specific panel field being present or worded a certain way.
4. Watch how AI assistants render your entity, since panel-style cards with photos, descriptions and videos already appear in ChatGPT.
5. Focus effort on the underlying facts and corroboration, which carry across whatever the presentation becomes.
6. Re-check the entity periodically after graph updates, since panel changes can be caused by an update rather than by anything you did.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Kalicube Pro

**Pitfall:** Optimizing for the current panel template - specific fields, a specific subtitle format - when the presentation layer is expected to be regenerated by AI and become dynamic.

**Apply at Pabau:** Treat any Pabau knowledge panel work as investment in the underlying entity facts, and report progress internally as knowledge graph inclusion rather than a screenshot of the panel.

**Apply anywhere:** Aim at knowledge graph inclusion, not the panel itself. Panels are template-driven today but will be AI-generated and dynamic, so build on facts and corroboration that survive a change in presentation.

### 17. Fix your Knowledge Panel name via suggested edit, not schema  `21.2`
*useful · concrete actions · source 21*
