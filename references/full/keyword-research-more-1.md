### 1. A coined position can create its own search volume later  `91.16`
*useful · content insights · source 91*

Grow and Convert note an outcome they did not plan for. Disruption stories are not created with SEO in mind and carry no keyword, but the term they coined, pain point seo, now has its own search volume in Ahrefs, reported at 50. So a piece written purely for social distribution ended up owning a branded-concept query, with the originating article as the obvious best answer. This inverts the normal order of operations. Instead of finding demand and writing for it, you create a named idea, spread it through social and speaking, and demand appears afterwards. They present it as potential rather than a plan, and the volume is small in absolute terms, but a query where you are definitionally the best result behaves more like branded search than a competitive keyword.

> "the term 'pain point seo' even has its own search volume now"

**Evidence:** Ahrefs shows 'pain point seo' at a search volume of 50, several years after Grow and Convert published the article that coined it.

**How to do it**

1. Name the position in your disruption story with a short, repeatable phrase.
2. Use that exact phrase consistently across the article, social posts, podcasts and talks.
3. Add the phrase to your rank tracker and check its volume in Ahrefs or Semrush every few months.
4. Once volume appears, make sure the originating article is the clear best answer for the definition query.
5. Add a plain definition sentence near the top of the article so it can be extracted and cited.
6. Watch for others using the phrase and check whether their pages outrank yours for it.
7. Treat the resulting query as branded search: defend it, but do not build the strategy on its volume.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Coined-term volume is tiny and slow. Chasing it as a keyword strategy inverts the point, which is that the naming spreads the position, not that the term drives traffic.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau names a methodology for how aesthetic practices should handle a workflow, track that phrase in the rank tracker and make sure the originating page defines it plainly, so Pabau owns the query if demand appears.

**Apply anywhere:** Name your position with a short phrase and repeat it everywhere. If the phrase catches on it becomes a query you are definitionally the best answer for, so track it and keep a clear definition on the originating page.

### 2. A real user question proves demand even at zero volume  `11.12`
*useful · best practices · source 11*

Gotch's standing rule for local and long-tail keyword research: don't require a search-volume number to justify pursuing a topic — if a topic is even being asked as a question anywhere, whether Reddit, Quora, People Also Ask, or a sales call, that alone is evidence of some demand, and the fact that most competitors filter purely on volume and ignore everything else is precisely the gap this creates. He frames requiring search volume as a topic-selection gate as 'a huge mistake' he sees constantly, especially since local-intent volume is inherently hard to quantify in standard tools.

> "If this is even being asked as a question, that already proves"

**How to do it**

1. When evaluating a candidate topic with zero recorded search volume, check whether it appears as a real question anywhere: Reddit, Quora, People Also Ask, or direct customer queries, rather than rejecting it for lack of volume.
2. If any genuine instance of the question being asked exists, tag the topic as having 'proven demand (non-volume)' in the tracking sheet rather than excluding it.
3. Prioritize these topics higher when they are local or highly specific, since local-intent volume is especially hard to quantify in standard tools even when real demand exists.
4. Continue to apply the 80/20 allocation so zero-volume topics don't crowd out proven-demand topics, but stop using zero volume alone as an automatic disqualifier.

**Pitfall:** Filtering out every zero-volume topic by default, which Gotch says is what almost everyone competing on SEO does, meaning the zero-volume-but-real-demand topics are exactly where less-diligent competitors leave opportunity on the table.

### 3. Accept that pain-point terms are harder to spot outside consumer niches  `138.14`
*useful · content insights · source 138*

Nat Eliason adds a caveat to his own case study. In the tea niche the pain-point keywords are obvious: people search for tea for sleep, for stress, for an upset stomach. For some businesses, he says, these will not be as obvious, and he names very niche B2B software and services as the hard case. His position is that it does not mean it cannot be done, and he points readers to Benji Hyam's separate article on finding high-converting ideas. The practical read for a B2B team is that the ease of the tea example is not evidence the method transfers cheaply. Where the buyer does not phrase their problem as a search, the problem list has to come from sales calls and support tickets rather than from a SERP.

> "For some businesses, these won't be as obvious as in the tea niche"

**Evidence:** Eliason names 'very niche B2B software or services' as the case where pain-point keywords are not obvious, while maintaining the method still applies.

**Pitfall:** Copying the consumer example directly into B2B, searching for 'best [software] for [problem]' and finding nothing, then concluding the demand is not there. In low-volume B2B categories the term can be real with no reported volume.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sits between the two cases. Some pain points, such as no-shows, are searched plainly; others, such as multi-location reporting, may show no volume. Build the second group from sales-call language and publish anyway rather than waiting for a volume figure.

**Apply anywhere:** In B2B and niche categories the pain-point terms will not surface from a keyword tool. Build the problem list from sales calls and support tickets, and publish against terms with no reported volume when the problem is real.

### 4. Add company size, vertical or stakeholder as a final keyword layer  `102.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 102*

After pain points and differentiators, Grow and Convert add a third layer of audience characteristics at the keyword level. In B2B they suggest company size ("enterprise CRM software"), specific industry verticals ("HVAC scheduling software"), or key stakeholders ("executive assistant service for startup founders"). In B2C they suggest demographic qualifiers such as "running shoes for women". The point is that a generic category keyword attracts the whole market, while a qualified one attracts the slice you serve, and the qualified variant is usually much easier to rank for because fewer pages address it directly. They frame this as the final layer, applied after the pain-point and differentiator keywords have been found and prioritized, rather than as a separate research method.

> "keywords that include company size"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite "enterprise CRM software", "HVAC scheduling software", "executive assistant service for startup founders" and "running shoes for women" as the pattern.

**How to do it**

1. List the segments you serve well: company sizes, verticals, job titles, or consumer demographics.
2. Append each segment to your core category keyword and pull the resulting suggestions from a keyword tool.
3. Check the SERP for each variant; keep the ones where no ranking page is written specifically for that segment.
4. Build one page per segment variant, and write it for that segment only rather than a generic page with a segment title.
5. Use segment-specific proof inside: a case study, screenshot or number from a customer in that segment.
6. Do this layer last, after the pain-point and differentiator keywords are already committed to the calendar.

**Pitfall:** Publishing near-identical segment pages that differ only in the swapped noun. Google treats them as duplicates and they cannibalize each other rather than ranking.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build genuinely distinct segment pages for the specialties it serves, each carrying its own workflow detail and a customer example from that specialty, not a template with the specialty name swapped.

**Apply anywhere:** Layer segment qualifiers onto your core keyword to find lower-competition variants your buyers actually type. Write each segment page with proof and workflow detail unique to that segment, or the set will cannibalize itself.

### 5. Add semi-solution-aware keywords to bridge pain point and product  `137.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 137*

Grow and Convert note the line between problem aware and solution aware is not always clear, so they deliberately target the middle. For Cognitive FX that meant 'post concussion syndrome treatment', where they reached position four, and 'physical therapy for post concussion syndrome', where they reached number one. Someone searching either already knows the named condition and is looking at treatment options, which makes them savvier than a pure pain-point searcher but not yet searching for a clinic. These terms sit between the two categories the agency usually names, pain-point posts and direct product keywords, and they matter because the direct-buying keyword pool is small. Adding the semi-solution-aware layer extends a bottom-funnel program once the handful of direct product terms is taken.

> "since the line between problem aware and solution aware is not always clear"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX reached position four for 'post concussion syndrome treatment' and number one for 'physical therapy for post concussion syndrome'.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the technical or clinical name for the problem your product solves, as an informed buyer would say it.
2. Generate 'treatment for [named problem]', '[named solution type] for [named problem]' and 'how to fix [named problem]' variants.
3. Keep only variants where using the technical term proves the searcher has already done research.
4. Check the SERP to confirm the results discuss options and providers rather than defining the term.
5. Publish one page per variant that compares the available solution routes honestly, including yours.
6. Prioritize these immediately after the direct product keywords and before broad pain-point topics.
7. Track their conversion rate separately to confirm they sit between the other two groups as expected.

**Pitfall:** These terms look informational in a keyword tool and get filed under top-of-funnel, so they never get a dedicated page. The tell is that the searcher used a term a novice would not know.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's equivalent semi-solution-aware terms are things like 'electronic medical records for aesthetic clinics' or 'online booking for medspas', where the searcher already knows the solution category. Each deserves its own page after the direct comparison pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Target the middle layer where searchers use the technical name for their problem and are comparing solution types. These extend a bottom-funnel program once the small pool of direct buying keywords is exhausted.

### 6. Add use-case and how-to queries before declaring a keyword shortage  `129.13`
*useful · best practices · source 129*

Grow and Convert warn that the category-name search is only the start of sizing demand. Their own examples in the article are deliberately narrow — 'natural pre workout', 'accounting software' — and they point out that per Pain Point SEO there are usually many use cases, how-tos and other question-based queries that still carry good buying intent. For pre-workout drinks they suggest 'are pre-workout drinks effective' or 'tips for getting a good workout'. For accounting software they suggest 'how to generate a balance sheet' or 'how to do small business accounting'. The practical consequence is that the 20-to-40-keyword screen should not be failed on category terms alone. A niche that looks thin at the category level often clears the threshold once the jobs its buyers are trying to do are added.

> "don't forget to also check for slightly higher in the funnel keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite 'how to generate a balance sheet' and 'how to do small business accounting' as buying-intent queries for accounting software, alongside the category term itself.

**How to do it**

1. List the category-name searches first and record their volumes as a baseline.
2. Write down the three or four concrete jobs a buyer is trying to do with your product, in their words.
3. Turn each job into question-based queries: 'how to ...', 'is ... effective', 'tips for ...'.
4. Run those through the keyword tool and keep only the ones where the searcher plausibly needs your product to finish the job.
5. Add the survivors to the keyword count before applying the 20-to-40 threshold.
6. Mark them as slightly higher funnel so you can compare their conversion rate separately later.
7. Only declare a keyword shortage after this second pass, never after the category search alone.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Sweeping in genuinely top-of-funnel questions to pad the count. The filter is whether finishing the job in the query plausibly requires your product; without that filter you have added volume and no intent.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau this is the case for the clinic-operations how-to library and the code-reference pages: queries like how to document a specific procedure or run a consent workflow carry intent because completing them needs software. Count those in the keyword inventory alongside the software-category terms.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not size demand from category names alone. Write down the jobs your buyer is trying to do, turn each into question-based queries, keep the ones that plausibly need your product to complete, and add them to the keyword count before judging whether the niche has enough keywords.

### 7. Add use-case keywords where the product helps someone do something  `112.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 112*

The third keyword type in Grow and Convert's B2C stack is the use case: search queries showing someone wants to do a thing the product helps them do. They keep it deliberately mechanical. Take the verb the product enables, then complete it with every context the customer operates in. For Truwild that is 'stay hydrated while' followed by essentially any outdoor activity, hiking, snowshoeing, running, and the parallel shape 'get energy before working out'. They point out these sit one step further from the purchase than the pain-point set, but the searcher is still trying to solve something the product solves, so they convert far better than lifestyle topics. The instruction is to run the loop for both pain points and use cases, which together they estimate yields another 50-100+ keywords for a single small brand.

> "get energy before working out"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert estimate pain-point plus use-case ideation adds 50-100+ keywords for a four-product supplement brand.

**How to do it**

1. Write the outcome your product delivers as a verb phrase, for example 'stay hydrated' or 'recover faster'.
2. Type that phrase into Google without finishing it and record every autocomplete completion.
3. Repeat with 'while', 'before', 'after' and 'without' appended, since each surfaces a different context set.
4. List the activities and situations your customers are actually in, and complete the phrase manually for each.
5. Check volume and SERP intent for each completion, and drop the ones whose SERP is dominated by medical or news sites.
6. Write each article to solve the task first, then position the product as the thing that makes it easier.
7. Keep these lower in the queue than the bottom-of-funnel product and pain-point terms.

**Pitfall:** Use-case terms shade into top-of-funnel fast. If the article can be written without mentioning your product, the keyword has drifted too far up the funnel.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau the use-case shape is 'how to reduce no-shows', 'how to run a clinic without paper notes', 'how to track client consent forms'. David should build these after the comparison and template pages are done, and every one should end at the Pabau section rather than being a neutral how-to.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn the outcome your product delivers into a verb phrase, then use Google autocomplete with 'while', 'before', 'after' and 'without' to find the contexts people search it in. Build these after your product and pain-point pages, and only where the article cannot honestly be written without your product.

### 8. Ask who else would benefit to find an audience you never targeted  `161.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 161*

Question six asks the customer what type of person they think would benefit from the product. Khanal's reason is that teams assume they already know the perfect use case, and this question repeatedly surfaces an audience nobody at the company had considered. It works because customers see the product from outside your positioning. They think of the colleague, the neighboring department or the adjacent industry that has the same problem. For a content team this is a direct source of new page targets: a new vertical qualifier, a new job title, a new use case, each of which becomes its own landing page or article rather than a paragraph inside a general one.

> "you'll be turned onto an audience that could be a great use case"

**Evidence:** Khanal reports this question repeatedly turns up use cases the company was completely unaware of.

**How to do it**

1. Ask 'What type of person do you think would benefit from X?' as free text with no options offered.
2. Log every job title, industry and situation named, even the ones that sound wrong.
3. Count mentions and keep any audience named by two or more unrelated customers.
4. Check whether you have a page written specifically for that audience; usually you do not.
5. Search the head keyword with that audience as a qualifier and read the top ten titles.
6. If no result is genuinely built for that audience, commission a dedicated page for it.
7. Ask sales whether any deals in that segment have already closed quietly.
8. Track conversion rate for the new segment pages separately, so you can tell whether the audience is real or a stray answer.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What Type Of Person Do You Think Would Benefit from (Product or Service Name)?
```

**Pitfall:** Dismissing an unexpected audience because it is not in the ICP document. The ICP was written from assumptions; this answer is data. The signal you got it wrong is finding closed deals in that segment after you dismissed it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should ask this of existing customers and watch for adjacent practice types, such as podiatry, dental aesthetics or veterinary clinics, that keep getting named. Each named type that repeats justifies its own vertical page.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask customers who else would benefit, and build a dedicated page for any audience two or more of them name independently.

### 9. Assume a fraction of definitional searchers always want a tool  `135.14`
*useful · content insights · source 135*

Grow and Convert state as a general finding from their experience that there is always a fraction of searchers on a definitional term who genuinely want a tool to do the task, even though they did not add 'tool' or 'software' to the query. That fraction is what makes a 'what is X' post worth writing for a product company, provided the term names a task rather than a concept. It also decides the structure: the post has to answer the definition properly, because most of the traffic is there for that, while still giving the tool-seeking minority a clear route. Their 'rough cut video editing' title, 'What Is Rough Cut Video Editing? (And a Simple Tool to Do It)', is built for both audiences at once, and they say they approached the post with both intentions in mind.

> "fraction of searchers who are searching a definitional term"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite this as consistent across their client work, and the 'rough cut video editing' post sat inside a mid-funnel set converting at 1.06%.

**Pitfall:** Writing a definitional post as a disguised product page. The majority intent is the definition, and failing it causes pogo-sticking that costs the ranking that made the minority reachable.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's code-reference and definitional pages should answer the term fully and then offer the practical route, for example a short section on how the code is recorded in a patient chart, rather than converting the page into a pitch.

**Apply anywhere:** A share of searchers on any task-based definitional query want a tool for the job. Answer the definition properly for the majority, then give that minority an explicit route to the tool.

### 10. Bridge two adjacent categories in one title to claim the all-in-one spot  `135.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 135*

The client's product both transcribed video and edited it, so Grow and Convert targeted transcription keywords as well as editing ones. Their observation was that most people transcribing footage want the transcript for something else, usually analysis or editing. That gave them a competitive advantage on a keyword they were not the obvious answer for. The title for 'video transcription software' became 'Best Video Transcription Software (& Tools to Use Your Transcripts)'. The bracketed half signals the second category from the SERP, so searchers who need more than a transcript self-select into the post. This is a way to enter a crowded category from the side: rank on the term everyone competes for, then win the subset of searchers whose real job spans both categories.

> "Best Video Transcription Software (& Tools to Use Your Transcripts)"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite this title as an example of readers self-selecting from the search results, within a program where 22 of 31 posts reached page one.

**How to do it**

1. List the two or more established categories your product straddles, for example transcription and video editing.
2. For the category you are weaker in, write down what the searcher does with the output after they get it.
3. Confirm that follow-on job is something your product does, and that competitors in that category do not.
4. Target the head keyword of the weaker category anyway, since demand there is already proven.
5. Title the post as the category keyword plus a bracketed clause naming the follow-on job.
6. In the roundup body, review the category tools fairly, then position yourself as the option covering both steps.
7. Add a section showing the handoff from step one to step two inside your product.
8. Measure conversion on that post separately, since it should convert lower than pure bottom-funnel terms but higher than top-funnel.

**Pitfall:** Claiming the all-in-one position without the second capability being usable. Reviewers and readers check, and a thin second half turns a category post into an obvious advertisement.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau straddles booking, charting, marketing and payments. Titles for single-category terms should carry the bridge, for example 'Best Clinic Booking Software (& What to Do With the Bookings)', so a searcher who also needs consent forms clicks through.

**Apply anywhere:** When your product spans two categories, rank on the weaker category's head term but title the post with a bracketed clause naming what the searcher does next, so buyers whose job spans both categories select themselves in.

### 11. Broad head keywords convert worse than modifier-appended variants  `08.12`
*useful · content insights · source 08*

High-volume head keywords (e.g., 'car accident lawyer') are still worth targeting regardless of conversion rate because of sheer volume, but keywords with appended modifiers like 'service' or 'emergency' were observed converting noticeably better despite lower search volume. The explanation offered is that broad head terms skew informational (people 'trying to learn' rather than ready to buy) and their apparent volume may be inflated by rank-tracking bots repeatedly querying the same head terms, making raw volume on very broad terms less trustworthy than it looks. The practical takeaway is that keyword intent and conversion quality often live in the modifier, not the head term.

> "those tend to convert a little better than just the keyword itself"

**Evidence:** Direct comparison: 'car accident lawyer' must be targeted regardless of conversion rate due to volume, while adding modifiers like 'service' and 'emergency' 'tend to convert a little better than just the keyword itself,' attributed partly to head terms being contaminated by informational searchers and bot-driven rank-tracking queries.

**Apply at Pabau:** When prioritizing keywords for Pabau content/pages, David shouldn't assume a high-volume head/category term is automatically the best conversion target — check whether modifier variants (more specific, solution- or urgency-flavored phrasing) convert better using actual funnel data, and treat raw search-volume numbers on very broad head terms with some skepticism since informational searchers and bot traffic can inflate them.

**Apply anywhere:** When prioritizing keywords for your content/pages, you shouldn't assume a high-volume head/category term is automatically the best conversion target — check whether modifier variants (more specific, solution- or urgency-flavored phrasing) convert better using actual funnel data, and treat raw search-volume numbers on very broad head terms with some skepticism since informational searchers and bot traffic can inflate them.

### 12. Build a keyword tracker with these 12 specific columns  `11.3`
*useful · concrete actions · source 11*

Gotch's keyword-research spreadsheet template has a specific minimum column set that should be built before any keyword is added: approval status, priority (high/medium/low), source (which of the 7-10 ideation channels the idea came from), cluster (kept isolated from adjacent clusters, e.g. core SEO vs. technical SEO vs. AEO services), the keyword plus any SERP features present, search volume, keyword difficulty or his own opportunity score, CPC, current ranking position, current URL if one exists, intent (commercial or informational), an auto-calculated opportunity tag (low-hanging fruit for positions 2-15, clustering opportunity for position 50+ or brand-new keywords), and status (needs approval/approved/disapproved/excluded). This structure is what lets every later filtering and scoring step in his process run automatically off clean, consistent inputs.

> "make sure we're going after approved ideas"

**How to do it**

1. Create a new spreadsheet with one row per keyword idea and add these columns in order: Approval, Priority, Source, Cluster, Keyword, Search Features, Volume, Keyword Difficulty/Opportunity Score, CPC, Position, Current URL, Intent, Opportunity, Status, Notes.
2. Set Approval as a dropdown (e.g., Needed/Approved/Disapproved) so stakeholder or client sign-off is tracked per keyword.
3. Set Priority as a dropdown of High/Medium/Low, where High means 'must action this week.'
4. For Cluster, assign each keyword to one topic bucket only, keeping adjacent-but-distinct service lines in separate clusters even if they'd sit on a similar page type.
5. For Intent, use a simple system: Commercial or Informational (inferred: add Uncategorized for ambiguous cases).
6. Build or copy a formula, or ask ChatGPT/Claude to write it, that auto-populates Opportunity from Position: positions 2-15 as low-hanging fruit, position 50+ as clustering opportunity, no-position keywords as untapped.
7. Add a Status column with values such as Needs Approval, Approved, Disapproved, Excluded so the sheet stays the single source of truth for active scope.
8. Before adding any new keyword row, filter it mentally against the existing clusters so the sheet stays clean rather than becoming an unfiltered dump of 500-1,000 ideas.

**Tools:** Google Sheets, ChatGPT, Claude

**Pitfall:** Throwing every keyword idea onto the sheet without filtering as you go — the sheet is meant to be the source of truth, so unfiltered dumping just recreates the same filtering workload later at a larger, more painful scale.

### 13. Build honeypot sites to surface zero-volume keyword demand  `16.5`
*useful · concrete actions · source 16*

The speakers build small, standalone sites purely to reverse-engineer real search demand that keyword tools can't yet see — for a term like "AI SOC" or "agentic SOC management," a keyword tool might return around 30 keywords, many flagged as having no monthly search volume, simply because the vocabulary is too new for tools to have accumulated data on it. Rather than trusting that "no volume" reading, they publish a site (capped around 50 pages) broad enough to rank for many long-tail permutations of the topic, then treat it as a honeypot: once it ranks, they read the Performance/Queries data in Google Search Console to see the actual, often "word soup" phrasing people are typing (e.g., "AI optimization guardrail"). Because the site is linked from the parent domain and never scaled past ~50 pages, they report it has "never been penalized" despite existing purely to harvest query data rather than to serve visitors directly.

> "build sites that effectively act as honeypots that collect that data"

**How to do it**

1. Pick a narrow, emerging topic where you suspect real search demand exists but keyword tools show thin or zero-volume data.
2. Query a keyword research tool (e.g., DataForSEO Labs, Semrush Keyword Magic) for seed terms in that space to confirm they read as low/no volume.
3. Build a small site, capped at roughly 50 pages, covering the topic broadly enough to rank for many long-tail permutations of it.
4. Publish in small batches and confirm indexing before adding more (apply the same 10-pages-then-verify rule) (inferred).
5. Add at least one contextual link from your parent/main domain so the site has some authority and isn't a fully orphaned property.
6. Once pages rank, open Google Search Console's Performance report for the honeypot property and read the Queries tab for the exact phrases people typed.
7. Mine those real queries for recurring patterns invisible to keyword tools, then prioritize the strongest recurring terms for content on your main site.
8. Do not scale the honeypot past ~50 pages or brand it as a marketing asset — its only job is data collection, which is also what keeps it low-risk.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, keyword research tool (e.g., DataForSEO, Semrush)

**Pitfall:** Treating a keyword tool's "no monthly search volume" reading as proof nobody is searching a term is the mistake — tools lag behind newly-adopted terminology, and a live honeypot site reveals real query volume and phrasing that keyword databases haven't caught up to yet.

### 14. Classify the keyword type before you plan the article's structure  `72.19`
*useful · concrete actions · source 72*

Khanal's agency sorts pain-point SEO keywords into four types before outlining: category, competitor, alternative, and jobs to be done. For 'best ai copywriting tools' the classification is obvious, a category keyword, and it determines what the page must be: the searcher is typing in the category and wants a list of options to evaluate, so the format is a listicle. He uses the classification alongside the SERP read rather than instead of it, checking that the ranking pages agree. In this case they did, with almost every result a listicle roundup and only two exceptions, a Reddit thread and a vendor landing page. The classification also tells him how hard to work at pitching the brand: on a core category keyword he says you will pitch most of the key features anyway.

> "Category keyword competitor alternative jobs to be done"

**Evidence:** Khanal's SERP read for 'best ai copywriting tools' found almost every result was a listicle roundup, with only a Reddit thread and Jasper's landing page as exceptions, confirming the category classification.

**How to do it**

1. Classify the target keyword as category, competitor, alternative, or jobs to be done before outlining.
2. For a category keyword, expect the searcher wants a list of options to evaluate, and plan a listicle.
3. Confirm the classification against the SERP: count how many ranking results match the expected format.
4. Note the exceptions in the SERP, such as a forum thread or a vendor landing page, and decide whether to address them.
5. Derive the persona from the classification and check it matches the personas you already sell to.
6. On a core category keyword, do not over-plan the brand pitch, since the feature discussion is unavoidable anyway.
7. Record the classification in the brief so later editors know why the format was chosen.

**Pitfall:** Classifying by keyword wording alone. Khanal notes 'copywriting' was ambiguous enough that it could mean landing page copy, headlines or long-form, and that ambiguity changed what the intro had to do.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a keyword-type field to the Pabau brief: category, competitor, alternative, or jobs to be done. It determines whether the target is a listicle, a comparison page or a how-to, before any outline work happens.

**Apply anywhere:** Classify each target keyword as category, competitor, alternative or jobs-to-be-done before outlining, then confirm the classification against the formats actually ranking.

### 15. Combine jobs-to-be-done phrasing with a competitor name once the plain how-tos run out  `103.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 103*

Grow and Convert give an escalation for when the obvious jobs-to-be-done keywords are used up. Once you have exhausted the highest-intent JTBD searches, typically the plain 'how to' terms, cross JTBD phrasing with competitor names. Their example is 'how to customize [competitor's] checkout page'. The searcher is inside a rival product, hitting a limit, and looking for a workaround. That is a buyer in the middle of a frustration you can convert, and the query has no competition from the rival, who is not going to write about their own limitation in those terms. It is a later-stage angle because it depends on knowing which rival features frustrate users, which comes out of sales and support conversations.

> "how to customize [competitor's] checkout page"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name it as the advanced angle after the highest-intent JTBD keywords are exhausted.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm the plain 'how to [task]' keywords for your category are already covered by published pages.
2. Ask support and sales which competitor limitations come up in switching conversations.
3. For each limitation, write the query the frustrated user would type, including the competitor's name.
4. Check the SERP: if the rival's own help doc or a forum thread ranks, the query is real and weakly served.
5. Write the article as an honest workaround guide for the competitor's product first.
6. Add a section explaining why the workaround is fragile and how your product handles it natively.
7. Keep the tone factual about the competitor; a hit piece kills the trust the workaround earned.
8. Track these separately from plain JTBD pages, since volume is lower and conversion should be higher.

**Pitfall:** Writing this before the basic JTBD pages exist wastes effort on tiny volumes. It is a follow-on angle, not a starting point.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can build these once the core 'how to' articles are live: how to do a specific charting or booking task in a rival system, then how Pabau removes the workaround. Source the limitations from the sales team's switching notes.

**Apply anywhere:** After the plain how-to keywords are covered, cross JTBD phrasing with competitor names to catch users hitting a rival's limits, and answer the workaround honestly before offering your product.

### 16. Do not invent a new category and rule out search demand by mistake  `157.2`
*useful · best practices · source 157*

Grow and Convert warn that B2B SaaS companies routinely invent a category name for branding reasons when they are really in an existing category with feature differentiation. The cost is real: if you research demand for the invented category you find nothing, conclude there is no search volume, and wrongly rule out paid search altogether. Their fix is to place the product in the existing category, bid on those established terms, and use the differentiators as sales arguments inside the ad copy and landing page rather than as the category label. The only case where a new category genuinely blocks paid search is when the audience truly is not aware of the problem or product type at all.

> "often try to invent a new category"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report seeing this pattern repeatedly in B2B SaaS and startups.

**How to do it**

1. List the category name your marketing currently uses and the name buyers actually type.
2. Check search volume for both in a keyword tool; a near-zero volume on your label is a warning, not a verdict.
3. Ask whether competitors solve the same job under a different name; if they do, you are in an existing category.
4. Adopt the established category term as the paid search target and keep your invented label for brand messaging only.
5. Move the differentiators into ad headlines, descriptions and landing page copy as reasons to choose you.
6. Re-run the fit test on the established category term before deciding paid search does not work.

**Pitfall:** Loyalty to the brand-invented category name. The signal you have hit this is a keyword research doc where every target term has zero volume while competitors clearly buy traffic.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should target the terms buyers use, such as practice management software and clinic software, rather than any internally coined positioning phrase. Keep coined language for brand pages, not for keyword targets or page titles.

**Apply anywhere:** Target the terms buyers actually use rather than a category name your marketing invented. Keep coined positioning language for brand pages, and never let it decide your keyword targets.

### 17. Do not use Google Ads broad match to source organic keywords  `104.4`
*useful · best practices · source 104*

Grow and Convert flag a common tactic and reject it. Some marketers turn on broad match in Google Ads to discover related keywords, then feed those into organic content planning. Their objection is that Google's broad match interpretation strays too far from the original key phrase to be useful, returning terms that share a loose topical association rather than the same intent. They say this is one of the reasons they do not use broad match in their own PPC service either. The practical consequence is that the discovery list you get back is polluted with queries from adjacent markets and different funnel stages, and screening it costs more than the few usable ideas are worth. Their preferred sources stay autocomplete, People also ask, related searches and a keyword tool seeded from category terms.

> "often strays too far from the original keyphrase"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert do not use broad match in their own paid search service for the same reason.

**How to do it**

1. Do not run a broad-match campaign purely to generate organic keyword ideas.
2. If broad-match search-term data already exists in the account, export it and treat it as a low-priority source, not a primary one.
3. Screen every broad-match term by checking that it names your product category or a job your product does.
4. Delete terms whose SERP is dominated by a different market, which is the signature of a broad-match drift.
5. Use phrase and exact match search-term reports instead, since those stay tied to the seed intent.
6. Prefer autocomplete, People also ask, related searches and a seeded keyword tool as your discovery stack.
7. Validate any survivor against the live SERP before briefing an article.

**Tools:** Google Ads, Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** The broad-match list looks impressively long, so it gets adopted as the keyword plan. Months later the published pages rank for queries from a neighboring industry and convert nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau ever runs paid search for demo bookings, David should not recycle broad-match search terms into the blog calendar. Use exact and phrase search-term reports, which are tied to real practitioner intent.

**Apply anywhere:** Skip broad match as an organic keyword discovery source. Its interpretations drift too far from the seed phrase; use exact and phrase search-term reports instead.

### 18. Dominate one keyword cluster per 30-90 day sprint  `11.2`
*useful · best practices · source 11*

Rather than doing blanket keyword research across a whole site, Gotch's process forces you to pick one dedicated category or cluster to fully dominate before moving to the next, sizing each sprint to roughly 25-50 unique keywords over a 30-to-90-day execution window, which typically breaks down to about half updates to existing pages and half brand-new assets. He frames this as necessary for focus and for creating a 'sense of completion' in ongoing SEO work — agencies especially get stuck in a perpetual, never-finished feeling when they don't treat each cluster as its own closed project with a defined finish line before starting the next one.

> "pick one category that you really want to dominate"

**How to do it**

1. List every candidate topic cluster or category for the site (e.g., for Pabau: clinic scheduling, patient intake, med-spa marketing, insurance billing) rather than researching all of them at once.
2. Select exactly one cluster to attack first based on strategic priority such as revenue potential, competitive gap, or sales-team demand.
3. Set a sprint window of 30 to 90 days for that cluster depending on its complexity (inferred: shorter sprints for narrow or local clusters, longer for broad national ones).
4. Within the chosen cluster, cap the working keyword list at 25 to 50 unique keywords, where unique means each requires its own dedicated page.
5. Split the list roughly in half between updating or upgrading existing ranking pages and building brand-new pages, adjusting the ratio based on how many existing assets already touch the cluster.
6. At the end of the sprint window, mark the cluster complete once the planned pages are built or updated, and only then select the next cluster to attack (inferred: log sprint start and end dates in the tracking spreadsheet).

**Pitfall:** Setting an unfocused goal like 'target 500 keywords' creates paralysis and never produces the sense of completion needed to keep an SEO program, especially an agency-client relationship, feeling like it is making progress.

### 19. Drop the 100-search minimum and mine the whole sub-100 range  `95.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 95*

Matt Goolding closes by pointing at the volume floor most teams use. Many companies will not even consider keywords under 100 monthly searches. Given that Grow and Convert are getting good results from sub-20 volumes, he argues there are large conversion opportunities being missed across the whole sub-100 range, which is far wider than the mini-volume band he studied. The practical implication is that the biggest untouched pool in most keyword sets is the 20 to 100 band, since it is above the noise floor where nobody bothers and below the threshold where planning tools and stakeholders start paying attention. He frames ignoring them as leaving signups, demos and inquiries on the table regardless of what you sell.

> "Many companies won't even consider keywords with a volume of less than 100 monthly searches"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report good results from sub-20 volumes across 17 clients, and infer the sub-100 range holds larger untapped opportunity.

**How to do it**

1. Open your keyword research export and remove any volume filter you have set at 100 or 50.
2. Filter instead to the 0 to 100 band and sort by commercial intent signals such as CPC and SERP composition.
3. Discard rows where the results are definitions, forums or news rather than vendor and comparison pages.
4. Group what remains into the sub-20 mini-volume tier and the 20 to 100 tier.
5. Schedule the 20 to 100 tier first, since it has both weak competition and measurable volume.
6. Write one page per term and measure conversions per page rather than sessions.
7. After a quarter, compare conversion counts from the sub-100 pages against your above-100 pages.
8. Use that comparison to set the floor permanently, or to remove it.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Removing the floor without an intent filter floods the calendar with worthless long-tail informational terms. The floor exists for a reason; replace it with an intent test rather than deleting it outright.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's keyword briefs should not carry a 100-search minimum. The 20 to 100 band contains most specific treatment, workflow and integration queries that aesthetic practice owners type before choosing software.

**Apply anywhere:** Remove the 100-search minimum from your keyword filters and replace it with a buying-intent test. The 20 to 100 band is usually the largest untouched pool in a mature keyword set.

### 20. Expect 5X to 10X more keyword opportunities in B2C than B2B  `112.9`
*useful · general insights · source 112*

Grow and Convert name a structural advantage that offsets the B2C average-order-value problem. There are far more consumers than businesses, so both search volume and traffic potential run higher in consumer categories. More importantly, with more people searching there are many more variations of what people call the same product or service, so a B2C business commonly has 5X or 10X more keyword opportunities than the average B2B business. They frame this as the answer to the earlier arithmetic: you need far more conversions to break even at a $100 order value, and the larger keyword universe is what makes that reachable. The catch they name is throughput, since capturing those opportunities requires producing a proportionally higher volume of content.

> "5X or 10X more keyword opportunities"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's agency observation across B2B and B2C clients.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sits closer to the B2B end, so David should not assume a large keyword universe exists automatically. The practical route to volume is the template and code-reference page families, where one page shape multiplies across many terms, rather than a bigger blog calendar.

**Apply anywhere:** In consumer categories expect 5X to 10X more keyword opportunities than a comparable business-to-business one, because more people search and they name the same product more ways. That larger universe is what makes a low order value viable, but only if you can publish at matching volume.

### 21. Expect one page to win one keyword because you get one title and H1  `145.7`
*useful · content insights · source 145*

Grow and Convert give the mechanism behind one-page-per-keyword, crediting the argument to a conversation with Bernard Huang of Clearscope. You only get one SEO title, one H1 and one meta description per page. Point them at several target keywords and one of two things happens: the page ranks for a single one of them, or it fails to match any individual keyword's intent closely enough and ranks for none. They pair this with a SERP observation: sick leave tracking and sick leave app have nearly identical intent, but Google ranks different pages for each, which they read as a signal that two dedicated pages are available rather than one. The takeaway is that near-duplicate intent is an opportunity, not cannibalization, when the SERPs differ.

> "only end up ranking for one of them"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show the sick leave tracking and sick leave app SERPs ranking different pages despite near-identical intent.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Stuffing several close keywords into one title and H1. The worst case is not choosing badly between them, it is matching none of them well and ranking for nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before merging two near-identical Pabau targets, David should compare the two SERPs. If Google ranks different pages, keep both as separate articles with their own title, H1 and meta description.

**Apply anywhere:** You get one title, one H1 and one meta description per page, so a page aimed at several keywords usually wins one or none. When two near-identical keywords show different ranking pages, build two pages.

### 22. Filter every tool-generated keyword list by buying intent before briefing  `104.5`
*useful · best practices · source 104*

Grow and Convert use keyword tools such as Semrush, Ahrefs and the free Ahrefs Keyword Generator, seeding them with a partial phrase like 'accounting software for' to get a long list of completions. But they attach an explicit caveat: not every keyword a tool suggests will be long-tail or relevant to the business, so the output has to be filtered by buying intent and judgment before it becomes a plan. The tool is a generator, not a prioritizer. They also note many of these tools have free tiers, so the generation step costs nothing; the expensive part is the human screening. This pairs with their volume and difficulty check, where they warn that high buying-intent terms often show very low estimated monthly volume and should not be cut for that reason.

> "filter by buying-intent and use your best judgment"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert seed the free Ahrefs Keyword Generator with 'accounting software for' and screen the output manually.

**How to do it**

1. Seed the keyword tool with a partial phrase ending in 'for' or 'software' rather than a single head noun.
2. Export the full suggestion list without applying a minimum volume filter.
3. Delete every suggestion that does not name your product category or a job your product does.
4. Delete suggestions aimed at segments you do not want to sell to, however good the volume looks.
5. Mark each survivor high, medium or low on buying intent from its wording and its SERP.
6. Pull volume and keyword difficulty for the high-intent survivors only, so you do not spend credits screening noise.
7. Keep high-intent terms showing under 20 estimated searches rather than cutting them.
8. Order the final list by intent, then difficulty, and brief in that order.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Accepting the tool's ranked output as a priority list. Keyword tools sort by volume and relatedness, neither of which predicts whether a searcher is shopping, so the top of the export is usually the worst-converting part of it.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should treat DataForSEO and Semrush exports for Pabau as raw candidate pools only. The screening pass, deleting anything not tied to running an aesthetic or healthcare practice, matters more than the export itself.

**Apply anywhere:** Use keyword tools to generate candidates, never to prioritize them. Seed with a partial phrase, export everything, then delete by hand whatever does not name your category or your buyer's job.

### 23. Generate JTBD topics from where a rival product is weakest  `93.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 93*

Goolding sketches how a company can turn a competitor's weaknesses into JTBD topics. Shopify could target how to customize woocommerce shop page or how to edit a woocommerce checkout page, answer clearly and honestly, then highlight how much easier, faster or cheaper the same job is on Shopify, because a reader suffering the hassle of WooCommerce is ripe for a switch. Slack could target best way to manage email, how to organize your email and how to manage your inbox, since it has historically positioned against business email and already publishes Slack vs email comparisons. QuickBooks, FreshBooks or Xero could target how to make invoices on word, how to do invoices on excel and how to make a budgeting spreadsheet. The common construction is a task performed painfully inside the thing you replace.

> "how to customize woocommerce shop page"

**How to do it**

1. Pick one named competitor or incumbent and list the tasks users complain are hard in it.
2. Check support forums and the competitor's own help docs for the tasks with the most workarounds.
3. Turn each into a 'how to [task] in [competitor]' or 'how to [task] on [free tool]' keyword and check volume.
4. Confirm the SERP is dominated by help-style content you can outrank with a better walkthrough.
5. Write the honest, complete answer for doing the job in that tool.
6. Add the comparison section showing the same job in your product, with time or step counts.
7. Link the post to your existing '[competitor] alternative' page.
8. Repeat per competitor so each rival has a cluster of task-level posts, not just one alternatives page.

**Pitfall:** Publishing a post about a competitor's product that is really a hit piece. Searchers wanted the task done, and a page that does not do it bounces regardless of how good the pitch is.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build task-level posts for jobs clinics struggle with in rival systems and in Google Calendar or Word, then link each to the matching Pabau alternative page.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn each competitor's most-complained-about tasks into 'how to do X in [their tool]' posts, answer them properly, then link to your alternatives page.

### 24. Generate the keyword list from your own product copy  `64.1`
*useful · ai workflows · source 64*

Cody's starting move for keyword research on a tool or product is to paste the product's own copy into ChatGPT and ask it to list 100 keyword ideas for it. Then he scans the list for the terms that map to what the product actually does - in his demo the product's job is writing, so he searches the list for anything containing 'write' and anything containing 'content', finds nothing useful under 'write' and a large cluster under 'content', and works from there. The framing is that the seed comes from the product rather than from a keyword tool's suggestions, so the resulting list is inherently relevant to what you sell.

> "do keyword research for the following"

**How to do it**

1. Copy the product's own marketing copy - what it does, who it's for, what problem it solves.
2. Paste it into ChatGPT and ask for 100 keyword ideas based on it.
3. Scan the output for the terms containing the words that describe your product's actual function.
4. Discard the clusters where nothing relevant comes back rather than forcing them.
5. Take the cluster with genuine density as your working set.
6. Validate that set with a real keyword tool before writing anything.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Here is all the copy from my product page. Do keyword research for the following - list 100 keyword ideas.
```

**Pitfall:** Model-generated keyword lists have no volume or difficulty data and include invented terms, which is why he treats this only as the seed and validates every candidate against a real tool.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, seeding a keyword list from the actual feature copy rather than from a tool's suggestions keeps the list tethered to what Pabau does, which is the relevance test that everything else in this corpus turns on.

**Apply anywhere:** Seeding a keyword list from the actual feature copy rather than from a tool's suggestions keeps the list tethered to what you do, which is the relevance test that everything else in this corpus turns on.

### 25. Google corrals near-duplicate query variants into separate rank buckets  `21.3`
*useful · content insights · source 21*

David explains that because real user queries are far longer and messier than the clean phrases SEOs think in, Google's first move is to 'corral' those queries into specific buckets/indexes so it doesn't have infinite index sprawl - a typo or a phrase like 'EMD domain' versus 'EMD double space domain' usually gets shoved into the same index and tracked together. But if a variant is common enough (his example: simply adding an 's' to a term), Google will split it into its own index with its own independently tracked rank position, which can differ from the singular form's position. The underlying reason is that a single word change can shift the entire intent and expected answer for a query.

> "Google does is try to corral searches into specific buckets"

**Evidence:** David's direct observation in his own GSC data using the 'EMD' example: singular vs. plural and common typo variants of the same core phrase can each carry separate, independently-tracked rank positions rather than being merged into one ranking signal.

**Apply:** When mapping keywords to pages, don't assume one page's optimization automatically covers every plural/typo/phrasing variant of a target term - check GSC's query-containing filter for each meaningful variant to see whether it is tracked (and ranking) as its own separate bucket before assuming full coverage.
