### 1. Attribute every resume claim to a department before crediting the candidate  `166.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 166*

The author's specific warning is about claims that span departments. A marketer writing 'I grew revenue from x to y in one year' is taking credit for an outcome that product, engineering, marketing and sales all touched. He says the candidate had better have a damn good answer to exactly how they did it. The same applies to the standard resume lines he lists: grew site traffic from x to y percent, contributed the highest volume of leads, grew social following from x to y. His observation is that hiring managers do not dig deep enough into what the person actually did, and that the failure is worst with candidates from well-known companies, where the excitement of poaching someone displaces the questioning. The fix is to force attribution before the claim counts.

> "the candidate better have a damn good answer"

**Evidence:** The author names revenue growth as the clearest example, since it touches product, engineering, marketing and sales at once.

**How to do it**

1. Highlight every outcome claim on the resume that could involve more than one department, starting with revenue and lead volume.
2. For each one, ask who else worked on it and how large the team was.
3. Ask what the candidate personally owned inside that outcome, in terms of decisions made and work produced.
4. Ask what the number was before they joined and whether anything else changed in the same period, such as a funding round, a pricing change or a new sales team.
5. Ask for one decision inside that project that they made and would now make differently.
6. Discount any claim where the candidate cannot separate their own contribution from the team's.
7. Apply the same interrogation harder, not softer, to candidates from well-known companies.

**Pitfall:** The hiring manager gets excited about poaching someone from a famous startup and never establishes their actual contribution. A name brand company does not always mean a better candidate.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David screens agency or in-house SEO candidates, make them separate their work from the rest of the account team, since agency case studies usually credit one person for a five-person result.

**Apply anywhere:** Mark every cross-department claim on a marketing resume and make the candidate isolate their own contribution, including what the number was before they arrived and what else changed at the same time.

### 2. Build and sell something with SEO on the side  `52.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 52*

Edward's concrete 6-month plan for a 22-year-old junior SEO working a 9-to-6 agency job is to build and sell something with SEO on the side, ideally a vibe-coded SaaS product since it avoids inventory and overhead, with e-commerce or Shopify named as viable but harder alternatives. He argues that learning to sell with SEO is fundamentally different from learning to just rank content, because selling forces you to care about topical authority, keyword targeting and research, conversion rate optimization, tracking, and marketing that earns genuine referral-traffic backlinks — skills that build much faster than working on content with no commercial stakes attached. He backs this with his own history at iProspect: working the agency job 8am-6pm, then continuing personal SEO work in the evenings and during lunch breaks writing his own SEO landing pages, while also founding and hosting the New York City SEO meetup group on weekends specifically to learn from other practitioners and web developers.

> "vibe code something ASAP. I have an episode on this"

**How to do it**

1. Pick a sellable product you can build quickly on the side: a vibe-coded SaaS is recommended as the easiest option since it avoids inventory and overhead, but e-commerce, e.g., via Shopify, is a viable alternative.
2. Build a minimum version of that product using AI-assisted, vibe coding, tools rather than spending months on a polished build.
3. Write and publish your own SEO-targeted landing pages for the product during off-hours, e.g., lunch breaks or evenings, rather than only doing SEO for your employer's clients.
4. Apply real commercial pressure to your own SEO work by focusing on topical authority, keyword targeting and research, conversion rate optimization, and tracking, since these are what selling actually requires beyond just ranking.
5. Pursue relevant backlinks and referral traffic through genuine marketing and networking for your own product, rather than purely content-based tactics.
6. Set up or join a local SEO or marketing meetup, Edward's own example was founding the New York City SEO meetup group, to learn from peers and web-development contacts on weekends.
7. Maintain this side project consistently over the full 6 months alongside the day job, using the day job for agency-side exposure and the side project for full-funnel, commercially-accountable practice.

**Pitfall:** Only ever practicing SEO on content or client work with no commercial stakes attached slows learning — selling something yourself forces engagement with topical authority, CRO, and tracking in a way that ranking-only practice does not.

### 3. Have a practicing marketer, not the CEO, run marketing interviews  `166.2`
*core · best practices · source 166*

The article's blunt rule is that only marketers should hire marketers. The reasoning is mechanical rather than territorial: the 5-Hows framework only works if the interviewer already knows which follow-up to ask and can tell marketing speak from a real result. A CEO or generalist recruiter asks the wrong questions, weights the wrong metrics and screens for the wrong candidate profile, which both loses good candidates and signals to strong applicants that the company does not know what it is doing. The author reports being asked 'how many golf balls fit inside a 747' and how many cups of coffee are in Manhattan, in marketing interviews, and says brain teasers that do not touch the role should be dropped. If there is no marketer in the company, borrow one: call in a favor from a friend or an advisor to sit the interview.

> "Only Marketers Should Hire Marketers"

**Evidence:** The author's own experience of being asked golf-ball and coffee-cup brain teasers in marketing interviews.

**How to do it**

1. Name a practicing marketer as the interviewer of record for every marketing role, even if the hiring manager is the CEO.
2. If no marketer works at the company, recruit an outside marketer friend or advisor to run the technical stage before you post the role.
3. Delete generic brain teasers such as golf-balls-in-a-747 from the question bank.
4. If you want to test analytical reasoning, replace the brain teaser with a marketing-specific estimation question about your own channels.
5. Have the marketer, not the recruiter, write the scoring rubric for the 5-Hows answers.
6. Give the non-marketer hiring manager a separate stage scoped to culture and communication, and no veto over technical scoring.
7. Debrief with the marketer's assessment first so the brand name on the resume does not anchor the discussion.

**Pitfall:** Non-marketer interviewers get excited about a candidate poached from a well-known startup and never establish what that person personally did there. The article calls this out directly: a name brand company does not always mean a better candidate.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not let a non-marketing manager be the only technical screen for a content or SEO hire. If nobody internal can score the answers, David should sit the technical stage himself or bring in an outside SEO to do it.

**Apply anywhere:** Put a practicing marketer in the room for every marketing interview, and borrow one from your network if you do not employ one. Cut brain teasers that have nothing to do with the role and score technical answers against a rubric the marketer wrote.

### 4. Map the five SEO skill sets before choosing agency, freelancer or in-house  `163.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 163*

Grow and Convert's first hiring factor is that SEO is not one job. They break execution into five skill sets: keyword strategy (research, valuation, prioritization), content writing that both ranks and converts, technical SEO (audits and ongoing monitoring), analytics (GA, Search Console, Semrush or Ahrefs tracking of traffic, conversions and backlinks), and web development to actually ship page and sitemap changes. Their own client accounts run 3 to 5 people: SEO and content strategists, writers, a PPC specialist, a project manager and a designer. The hiring implication is direct. If you hire one consultant or one in-house manager, that person will have to build a team underneath them through further hires, contractors or agencies. Decide up front which of the five you already cover internally, and hire only against the gaps.

> "there are often 3 to 5 people with"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert staff 3 to 5 specialists per client account and say they could not produce their results without those teams.

**How to do it**

1. Write the five SEO functions down as a checklist: keyword strategy, content writing, technical SEO, analytics, web development.
2. Mark each one as covered in-house, partially covered, or absent, naming the specific person for the covered ones.
3. For web development, confirm you have someone who can publish new pages and edit the sitemap within a week of a request, not a quarterly release train.
4. For analytics, confirm someone owns GA4, Search Console and one of Semrush or Ahrefs, including conversion tracking, not just traffic.
5. Count the absent boxes: three or more absent means a single hire cannot execute and you are buying a team, not a person.
6. If you still hire an individual, budget explicitly for the contractors they will need and agree who manages them.
7. Ask any agency to name the actual roles on your account and how many people, then check those roles cover your absent boxes.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Google Search Console, Semrush, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Hiring one generalist and assuming the gaps close by themselves. The signal is a strategy deck in month two with no one able to publish the pages it calls for.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau adds SEO headcount or a vendor, David should map who owns each of the five functions for pabau.com. Web development and analytics are usually the hidden gaps, and template and code-reference pages need dev capacity to ship at all.

**Apply anywhere:** Before adding SEO headcount or a vendor, map who owns each of the five functions for your site. Development and analytics are usually the hidden gaps, and page types that need custom templates cannot ship without dev capacity.

### 5. Run the 5-Hows chain on every marketing result a candidate claims  `166.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 166*

Grow and Convert's post proposes a 5-Hows interview framework, borrowed from Toyota's 5 Whys, but aimed at what a person actually did rather than the root of a problem. The interviewer takes the headline claim, then asks how five times in sequence: how did you do that, how did you grow that fast, how did you promote it, how much traffic did that drive monthly, how did you convert traffic to revenue. The author's point is that a bad candidate collapses into buzzwords by the third how, while a strong one answers with named channels, monthly numbers and conversion math. He gives a passing answer as the benchmark: two blogs a week, one post at 20,000 visitors, expert guest contributors, 20,000 monthly visits, 3% blog conversion, 600 leads a month, 5% close rate, $3,500 average sale, 30 closed deals.

> "In order to quickly cut through the BS in marketing interviews"

**Evidence:** The author's worked example contrasts a failing chain ending in '1000 visitors' and 'those people turned into customers' with a passing chain ending in 3% blog conversion, 600 leads, 5% close, $3,500 average sale, 30 deals.

**How to do it**

1. Open with the candidate's own headline claim from their resume, for example revenue grown from zero to $100k in a year.
2. Ask 'how did you do that' and write the channel they name verbatim.
3. Ask 'how were you able to grow that quickly' and require publishing cadence, specific sites and any single outlier result.
4. Ask 'how did you promote your content' and require named communities, not 'we shared it on Twitter and Facebook'.
5. Ask 'how much traffic did that drive on a monthly basis' and require a monthly figure, not a cumulative one.
6. Ask 'how did you convert traffic to revenue' and require conversion rate, lead count, close rate and average sale price.
7. Score the chain pass only if the last answer contains at least four numbers that multiply correctly.
8. Stop the interview early if any two consecutive answers contain no number, channel name or specific site.

**Pitfall:** Interviewers let a vague but confident answer slide because it sounded like a real strategy. The signal you have been fooled is that the whole chain contains no monthly figure and no conversion rate.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David interviews for a content or SEO seat at Pabau, run this chain against the candidate's biggest claim and require the traffic-to-demo-request math, since Pabau's blog and template pages are judged on demo requests rather than sessions.

**Apply anywhere:** Take the biggest number on a marketing candidate's resume and ask 'how' five times in sequence, forcing channel, cadence, monthly traffic and the full conversion math. A candidate who cannot close the chain with figures that multiply is quoting articles, not experience.

### 6. SEO has split into theoretical and experimental — the experimentalists win  `29.e1`
*core · general insights · source 29 · universal-edition only*

Asked what advice he would give SEOs who want better work and bigger careers, Hank Azarian frames the field as split in two: people who create content and gather theories, and people who are out testing, experimenting and pushing the narrative forward. His claim is that the closer you sit to the experimentalist end — testing, building and learning on your own — the bigger the career win available. He describes acting on this at Raptive by making testing one of the first things he built, working with Nick Swan at SEO Testing to stand up testing infrastructure across what publishers were doing. The mental shift he names matters more than any single result: you constantly have hunches about your work, so capture them and test them rather than letting them stay hunches. He applies it to his own site too, testing things like semantic HTML to find out whether it works for him specifically.

> "The closer today you can be to an experimentalist ... the biggest win you can get."

**Evidence:** Career advice from someone who built testing infrastructure across a 5,000-publisher network, plus his own ongoing site experiments; the concrete corroboration is the SEO Testing partnership used to systematize what publishers were doing into findings.

**Apply:** Build a personal habit of capturing hunches as testable statements and running them on a property you control, rather than only consuming other people's conclusions. The differentiator being described is not knowing more theory but having your own evidence, which also means accepting that some tested tactics will turn out not to work for your particular site.

### 7. Send a $5k spending challenge before you read any resume  `166.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 166*

Rather than filtering on resumes, the author proposes a short challenge question sent to every applicant at the screening stage, before the resume is even reviewed. His example is to describe the company briefly, then ask: if you had $5,000 to spend in any way you wanted, how would you spend it and why. He argues this does three things at once. It filters the non-serious applicants who will not spend the time, it exposes thought process rather than credentials, and it removes the bias that creeps in when the reviewer recognizes the company name or the school on a resume. He is explicit about sequencing the two exercises: the cheap challenge question goes out early to everyone, and a full plan for how the person would approach the role is reserved for candidates already far along in the process.

> "If you had $5k to spend in any way you wanted"

**Evidence:** The author used this as an early test in his own hiring and says it quickly separates people who know what they are doing from those who do not.

**How to do it**

1. Write a short factual description of the company, its product and its current marketing, no longer than a paragraph.
2. Attach the challenge: given $5,000 to spend any way you want, how would you spend it and why.
3. Send it to every applicant at screening, before anyone on the panel opens a resume.
4. Strip names, employers and schools from the answers before the panel scores them.
5. Score for a specific channel choice with a stated reason and an expected outcome, not for the size of the idea.
6. Advance only the answers that name what they would measure to know the $5k worked.
7. Reserve the full role plan for late-stage candidates only, so you are not asking finalist-level work from a hundred applicants.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Describe a little bit about the company. Challenge: If you had $5k to spend in any way you wanted, how would you spend it and why?
```

**Pitfall:** Asking every applicant for a full strategic plan up front. That is finalist-stage work, it collapses your applicant pool and good candidates refuse to do it unpaid.

**Apply at Pabau:** For a Pabau content or SEO hire, send the $5k challenge scoped to pabau.com specifically, and ask which page type they would spend it on: blog, templates or code-reference pages. The answer tells you whether they understand where the demo requests come from.

**Apply anywhere:** Send every applicant one short paid-budget challenge question before you read resumes, then anonymize the answers for scoring. Keep the full role plan for finalists only.
