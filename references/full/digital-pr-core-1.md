### 1. Active PR beats reactive PR on traffic and anchor control  `33.1`
*core · content insights · source 33*

Reactive PR links (from HARO/Qwoted-style expert-quote placements) often get diluted because multiple sources are cited in the same article, the link frequently points to a homepage rather than an author page, and the pitcher has zero control over anchor text (name, business name, or generic phrasing). Active/proactive PR — pitching your own original data or content — instead drives more referral traffic because the resulting link is actually about the business, and lets you shape anchor text deliberately (e.g. "a study by [Brand]" or a specific stat like "57% of algae bloomed over two weeks"), which both diversifies anchor text and reinforces brand-entity association. The guest frames this as the core reason to graduate from reactive to active PR once a business has the resources to create original content.

> "the link value gets diluted because there are multiple links going out"

**Evidence:** Direct comparison given by the guest: reactive PR links are diluted by multiple co-cited sources and offer no anchor-text control, while active PR links are framed around the brand and let the pitcher choose the anchor phrasing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Once Pabau has the resources to produce original data/content, prioritize active/proactive PR pitches over chasing reactive quote opportunities, since the resulting links carry more referral traffic and let Pabau control brand-name anchor text instead of leaving it to chance.

**Apply anywhere:** Once you have the resources to produce original data/content, prioritize active/proactive PR pitches over chasing reactive quote opportunities, since the resulting links carry more referral traffic and let your site control brand-name anchor text instead of leaving it to chance.

### 2. Answer exactly one journalist quote request per day  `24.10`
*core · best practices · source 24*

The recommended habit is to maintain profiles on the major expert-quote platforms — Help a Reporter Out, Source of Sources, Featured.com, and Qwoted — and respond to exactly one journalist question per day total across all of them combined, not one per platform (which would be up to four responses a day). Outcomes vary per placement (a name mention with no link, a nofollow backlink, or — described as happening often — a dofollow backlink), but kept up consistently for a month, this low-daily-effort habit is framed as compounding into a surprising volume of high-quality links without ever feeling like a heavy time commitment.

> "one journalist response a day across all these platforms"

**How to do it**

1. Create profiles on Help a Reporter Out (HARO), Source of Sources, Featured.com, and Qwoted.
2. Each day, review the incoming journalist questions across all of these platforms combined.
3. Select and respond to exactly one question total per day, not one per platform.
4. Write a genuine, specific, well-informed answer to that single chosen question.
5. Accept variable outcomes: some placements give a name mention with no link, some a nofollow backlink, and some (reportedly common) a dofollow backlink.
6. Sustain this as a standing daily habit for at least a full month before judging results, since the payoff is a monthly accumulation of links, not an immediate one-off win.

**Tools:** Help a Reporter Out, Source of Sources, Featured.com, Qwoted

**Pitfall:** Trying to answer a question on every platform every day (up to four responses/day) instead of capping it at one response per day total across all platforms combined, which the source says is unsustainable.

### 3. Ask every interviewee at the end for one more expert to interview  `162.12`
*core · best practices · source 162*

Peralta treats each completed interview as the source of the next one. As soon as an interview finishes, he asks the expert whether they know anyone else who would be open to being interviewed. He says that when they say yes, they typically make a warm introduction rather than just handing over an email address, which converts far better than cold outreach. That is how he landed the CEO of Receptive.io. He pairs this with the network route: before starting cold outreach, check whether anyone on your team already knows the target. Grow and Convert landed David Cancel, CEO of Drift, because Benji already knew Drift's Director of Marketing and could make the introduction directly.

> "I ask them if they know of anyone who would be open for an interview"

**Evidence:** Referrals produced the interview with the CEO of Receptive.io, and a colleague's existing connection produced the interview with Drift CEO David Cancel.

**How to do it**

1. Before any cold outreach, share the target list internally and ask who already knows someone on it.
2. Route every warm connection through the colleague who knows the target, not through a cold email.
3. At the end of every completed interview, ask the expert who else they think should be interviewed on this topic.
4. Ask them specifically for a warm introduction by email rather than for contact details.
5. Add each referred name to the target list the same day, with the referrer's name in a column.
6. Open the next pitch by naming the referrer in the first line.
7. Send the published article back to the referrer so the next ask is easy.

**Pitfall:** Asking for the referral days later by email. The goodwill from the interview has cooled and the reply rate drops sharply; the ask has to happen in the call itself.

**Apply at Pabau:** After every Pabau customer or clinician interview, ask for one more name before ending the call and log the referrer, so the expert pipeline for blog articles compounds instead of restarting each quarter.

**Apply anywhere:** Mine your own team's connections before cold outreach, and close every completed interview by asking for one warm introduction to the next expert.

### 4. Ask hosts to describe you in your exact fixed wording  `73.14`
*core · concrete actions · source 73*

Barnard separates self-declared information from third-party proof. You can self-declare your way to a knowledge panel through your own site, Crunchbase, TheOrg, LinkedIn and Twitter, but the result will not be very stable and will not be authoritative. Third-party press is what makes the understanding stable and gives real industry authority. His tactic is small and specific: when he appears on a podcast, he asks the host to describe him in a particular way in the show notes, and the host does. That is third party because he has no control over it, and it does two jobs - it vouches that he exists, and it binds the exact descriptor ('the brand SERP guy', 'digital brand engineer') to the specific person. He observes people are nervous about asking, but distinguishes the ask: telling someone to call you the best in the universe is one thing, asking them to say you are a digital brand engineer is not a big ask. The goal is consistency, not praise.

> "anything self-declared has limited ability to survive"

**Evidence:** Barnard describes asking the host on a previous episode to describe his brand a certain way in the description, and the host doing it; he calls each repetition a bit more meaningful.

**How to do it**

1. Write the one-sentence descriptor you want third parties to use, with your name as the subject and a category noun as the object.
2. Send it to every podcast host, event organizer and journalist after the recording or interview, framed as 'here's how to describe me'.
3. Keep the ask factual - the category and the role - rather than superlatives, which makes it easy to say yes to.
4. Ask specifically for it in the show notes or description, which is the indexable text.
5. Do not stop the self-declared work while waiting for press; profiles and the entity home come first.
6. Collect every third-party mention on a media or press pillar page and link to it from the entity home.
7. Use the identical wording each time so the repetition compounds rather than adding new variants.

**Pitfall:** Asking for flattering superlatives instead of the plain descriptor. Hosts decline, and even when they agree the wording varies each time, which produces variants rather than the consistency you were after.

**Apply at Pabau:** Whenever a Pabau employee appears on a podcast or in a trade article, send the host the exact one-line Pabau descriptor for the show notes, and collect every resulting mention on a press page linked from the About page.

**Apply anywhere:** Send every podcast host and journalist the exact one-sentence descriptor you want used, keeping it factual rather than flattering. Aggregate the resulting third-party mentions on a press page linked from your entity home.

### 5. Ask the company's content marketer or PR lead to book the interview for you  `162.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 162*

Peralta's last resort before dropping a prospect is to stop pitching the executive and pitch their marketing team instead. His reasoning is that an executive may ignore a stranger but will listen to someone inside their own company, and the content marketer or head of PR has a standing interest in getting coverage, so they will usually help if the angle is right. This is how he landed the CEO and CTO of Amplitude Analytics: after no response from the CEO, he emailed Amplitude's Content Marketing Manager. His tracker showed the email had been forwarded and opened 35 times in three days, which told him internal interest was real, so he followed up a few days later and got the interview. That piece produced more than 2,300 pageviews in two weeks.

> "reach out to the content marketer on their team"

**Evidence:** Peralta's email to Amplitude's Content Marketing Manager was forwarded and opened 35 times in three days and produced an interview with the CEO and CTO; the article got over 2,300 pageviews in two weeks.

**How to do it**

1. After the executive ignores two emails, find the company's content marketer, PR lead or communications manager on LinkedIn.
2. Find their email with Voila Norbert or Email Hunter.
3. Send the same angle-led pitch, but ask them to set up the interview with the named executive rather than to be interviewed themselves.
4. State the audience and the coverage the company gets, since that is the marketer's own objective.
5. Watch the open count in your tracker: repeated opens mean the email is being forwarded internally.
6. Follow up a few days after you see the forwarding pattern, not before.
7. Once the marketer agrees, let them schedule the call and copy them on the write-up.

**Tools:** Voila Norbert, Email Hunter, Mixmax

**Pitfall:** Skipping this step and marking the prospect dead. Peralta's biggest names came through this route, and a marketer's internal ask converts far better than a stranger's cold email.

**Apply at Pabau:** For larger clinic groups and aesthetic device brands, Pabau should pitch the marketing manager rather than the clinical director, since the marketer's job is coverage and they will book the clinician internally.

**Apply anywhere:** When the executive stops responding, pitch their content marketer or PR lead to arrange the interview, since coverage is that person's job description.

### 6. At enterprise scale, link reclamation is a rounding error — go content-led instead  `46.e9`
*core · content insights · source 46 · universal-edition only*

Tallent's team added a digital-PR function only about halfway through his four-and-a-half-year tenure, driven by the need to win RFPs with that skill natively in-house rather than as an add-on. What he learned running it is that the traditional backlinking motions — reclaiming broken backlinks, chasing unlinked brand mentions — stop working at enterprise scale, because a few fixes are a drop in an already enormous backlink ocean, and getting meaningful movement would require outreach in the tens or hundreds of thousands. He is explicit that the clients in question were companies like Microsoft, Lego and AT&T, and the interviewer notes the caveat that this does not describe startups. What did work was content-driven: data-driven white papers and original infographics used as assets to pitch journalists.

> "all of the traditional backlinking methods ... none of that works anymore"

**Evidence:** Direct operational experience running digital PR for enterprise clients (Microsoft, Lego, AT&T) at an agency that scaled to 90 SEOs and CROs, with the interviewer explicitly flagging that the finding is scale-dependent and does not transfer to startups.

**Apply:** Size the tactic to the backlink profile you already have: on a small or mid-sized site, reclamation and unlinked-mention outreach can still move the needle measurably, whereas on a site with an enormous existing profile that effort is better redirected into original data assets and infographics built to be pitched. Treat 'this tactic is dead' claims as scale-conditional rather than universal.

### 7. Beat competitors with 1/100th the backlinks via niche PR  `24.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 24*

A guest commenter (Jake Hundley) describes an agency that ranks number one in its niche despite having roughly 1/100th the backlinks and a fraction of the on-site content of its competitors, achieved through a two-pronged PR strategy: writing full content pieces tailored to niche, highly relevant publications and pitching the editorial team directly for a placement backlink (in-body or in the byline), plus guest-appearing on industry-specific podcasts to earn backlinks from both the show's site and the podcast platforms. The framing is explicit that this outperforms competitors' larger, less-relevant backlink profiles precisely because every placement is tightly relevant to the niche, and that the links came from relationship-building and content value rather than payment.

> "we have 1/100 of the backlinks that our competitors have"

**How to do it**

1. Identify a small set of niche-specific, highly relevant publications and industry podcasts in your exact vertical rather than generic high-DA sites.
2. Write full articles/content pieces tailored to each target publication's audience and pitch them directly to that outlet's editing team for placement.
3. Negotiate a backlink either within the article body or in your author byline as a condition of the placement.
4. In parallel, pursue guest appearances on industry-specific podcasts, securing backlinks from both the show's website and, where available, podcast distribution platforms.
5. Prioritize the relevance of each placement over its raw domain authority or the target's overall backlink count.
6. Keep tracking which niche outlets/podcasts you've already secured and continue building each relationship for repeat coverage rather than treating placements as one-off.
7. Avoid paying directly for placements — frame the whole effort as relationship- and content-driven rather than a link-buying budget line.

**Pitfall:** Assuming you need to match or exceed a competitor's raw backlink count — this agency ranked #1 with 1/100th the backlinks and less on-site content by prioritizing niche relevance over volume.

### 8. Build a media list with Muck Rack, Hunter, and Sheets  `33.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 33*

The process: search Muck Rack (a global journalist database showing outlet, employment status, and topic "beat") for recent articles containing the campaign's core terms, to surface journalists already covering that beat. Every matching article and its byline journalist gets logged into a master Google Sheet, then each journalist's outlet domain is run through Hunter, an email-finding tool, to surface likely address patterns (name@domain.com, info@domain.com, etc.) which are matched to the journalist and verified — shrinking an initial list of "hundreds of journalists" down to a smaller, cleaner one. The sheet's columns are first name, last name, email, publication, and last relevant article, and the list is filtered to journalists who wrote on the topic within the last 60-90 days, on the reasoning that this recency window is when they're most likely to want fresh data to update or extend a piece.

> "we used Hunter in order to find all of those emails"

**How to do it**

1. Open Muck Rack and search for recent articles containing your campaign's core terms (the source's example: Facebook, social media, Instagram, teenagers).
2. Log every matching article and its byline journalist into a master Google Sheet.
3. Run each journalist's outlet domain through Hunter to surface associated email address patterns for that domain.
4. Match the most relevant found email to each journalist by name and add it to the list.
5. Verify each email and drop unverifiable ones, which the source says typically shrinks an initial list of hundreds of journalists down to a smaller, higher-quality list.
6. Structure the sheet with columns for first name, last name, email, publication, and most recent relevant article.
7. Filter the final list to journalists who wrote about the topic within the last 60-90 days, since that recency window is treated as the point where they're likely to want fresh, updatable data.

**Tools:** Muck Rack, Hunter, Google Sheets

**Pitfall:** Not filtering by recency — targeting journalists whose last relevant article is stale reduces the odds they'll want to cover an update, so the source explicitly bounds the list to a 60-90 day window.

### 9. Build a shareable micro-tool, then 301-redirect its links to you  `48.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 48*

The core mechanic is to build a "linkable asset": a small, free tool, calculator, or novelty page that's inherently fun, useful, or interesting enough that people share it unprompted in blogs, articles, podcast show notes, and on social media, with each share generating a natural backlink. There are two deployment models: host the asset directly on your own site, as its own page or subdomain, so links accrue to your domain immediately, or launch it on a separate, catchy standalone domain with a call-to-action pointing back to your brand, and once it's gathered links, 301-redirect that standalone domain to your site so all the accumulated backlinks and SEO authority transfer over. Either way, the intent is the same: an inherently shareable asset can accumulate backlink volume no manual outreach or paid campaign could realistically match, then consolidate that authority onto the domain you actually want to rank.

> "making things that are so fun, useful, or interesting, or cool"

**How to do it**

1. Brainstorm a simple, free micro-tool, calculator, or novelty page relevant to your niche that a stranger would find fun, useful, or interesting enough to share unprompted.
2. Decide on a deployment model: host it directly on your main site, e.g., a subdomain or subdirectory, if you want links to accrue immediately to your domain, or register a separate, catchy standalone domain if you want a distinct, highly shareable brand for the asset itself.
3. If using a standalone domain, add a clear call-to-action on the asset pointing back to your main brand or site.
4. Build the tool, see the AI-idea-generation and vibe-coding steps in the related insights, and publish it.
5. Let the asset accumulate organic shares and backlinks over time through blogs, newsletters, influencers, and social media.
6. Once the standalone domain has accumulated meaningful backlinks, 301-redirect it to the equivalent page on your main site so the accumulated link authority transfers over.
7. Verify the transferred authority in Google Search Console or a backlink tool such as Ahrefs or Semrush by confirming the referring domains now show against your main site post-redirect (inferred verification step).

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Relying on a purchased-link budget to try to replicate these results is pointless — the source is explicit that no link building budget could get the results a genuinely shareable free asset can generate organically.

### 10. Build a tiered-link campaign from one research asset  `33.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 33*

For a client in an adjacent gray niche, the guest built one research asset — a study plus infographic on TikTok's effect on teenagers, covering age breakdown, time spent, usage by country, and year-over-year change — then pitched it to publications under multiple different angles (one framed for general readers, one framed as "how to stop it" for parents/teachers/psychologists). The campaign earned five to six initial links, several of which then earned their own links, creating a tiered structure (a DR91 outlet and a health site linked directly, and a third site then linked to the DR91 page). Impact was measured not on the asset page's own ranking but in Google Search Console, which showed roughly a 30%+ increase in the number of keywords/queries the site surfaced for containing the target term after publication.

> "it ended up becoming its own sort of tiered link building strategy"

**How to do it**

1. Pick a topic broad and newsworthy enough to interest several publication types, even if only tangential to your product (the source's example: TikTok's effect on teenagers, for a client selling social-media engagement services).
2. Research the topic thoroughly using public data (age breakdown, time spent, usage by country/region, year-over-year change).
3. Turn the research into both a written study and a visual infographic hosted on your own site.
4. Write multiple distinct pitch angles for the same asset — e.g. one for general publications, one aimed at parents/teachers/psychologists — to widen the pool of relevant outlets.
5. Pitch each angle to the matching publication type via your media list.
6. Track which outlets pick it up and whether those outlets' pages are then cited by further sites, forming a tiered link chain.
7. Once the asset earns external links, add internal links from it to your commercial/money pages to pass the new authority through the site.
8. Measure impact in Google Search Console by comparing total queries/impressions containing your target term before vs. after publication, rather than judging success only by the asset page's own ranking.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Measuring success only by whether the asset page itself ranks — the real value showed up as a broad increase in site-wide query volume in Search Console and as referred authority to commercial pages via internal links, not the asset page outranking anything directly.

### 11. Build founder credibility first, then pitch them as an expert quote  `32.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 32*

The second niche-link-building tactic is to first manufacture visible credibility for the client's founder or a thought leader, then use that credibility to earn links via expert-quote contributions rather than full guest posts. Build the credibility cheaply by getting the founder booked on podcasts through AI Podcast Matcher.com (so a Google search of their name fills with podcast appearances) and having an intern post daily on their behalf on LinkedIn/X — plain text posts, no video required; once that credibility exists, find broader industry sites already publishing relevant articles and pitch the founder as a quick expert-quote source rather than a guest author, since the resulting link is earned through the person's demonstrated expertise rather than requiring the whole site to be perfectly on-topic.

> "when you search this person's name, you see them going"

**How to do it**

1. Identify the founder or a thought leader at the company to build as the credible face of the niche.
2. Sign that person up for guest podcast appearances via a matching service such as AI Podcast Matcher.com.
3. Book and complete enough podcast appearances that a Google search of the person's name returns multiple podcast-appearance results.
4. Assign an intern or equivalent low-cost resource to post daily on the founder's behalf on LinkedIn and/or X — plain text posts or threads are sufficient, video isn't required.
5. Search broader (not just exact-niche) industry sites already publishing articles adjacent to your topic.
6. Instead of pitching a full guest post to these sites, pitch the founder as an available expert source for a quick quote on their existing article topic.
7. Provide a short, specific expert quote when the site accepts, and confirm the published article links back to the company as the source.
8. Track which of these expert-quote placements result in an actual live link back, since not all publishers link the source by default.

**Tools:** AI Podcast Matcher.com

**Pitfall:** Pitching a cold expert-quote submission without first building any visible credibility for the founder (podcast history, active social presence) undermines the pitch, since the mechanism depends on the site or journalist doing due diligence and finding the founder already reads as a legitimate source.

### 12. Build the data-led link magnet around a shocking regional map  `57.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 57*

Asked to walk through a digital PR play concretely, Jackie describes competing with a real-estate directory on a low budget. The piece: an article arguing that a specific state is becoming increasingly impossible for the average person to own real estate in, built on public data and presented beautifully. Then get it to the front page of a small local subreddit - he notes moderators aren't watching local subreddits because they're too small to care about - which alone earns links. To go national is much harder, and the device he uses is a state-by-state colour map of where the average person can't afford to own: the obvious entries like New York City don't drive the story, the in-betweeners that shock people do. That's what produces the reaction journalists want to write about. Cody's framing ties it to short-form social: both are the same idea - create a novel thing with virality built in, because the journalist knows their audience will find it interesting, click it, and generate revenue for them.

> "come up with an article that is saying something along the lines"

**How to do it**

1. Pick a claim in your category that is arguable from public data and emotionally charged - his template is 'X is becoming increasingly impossible for the average person'.
2. Assemble the data from public sources (census, land registry, government statistics) and design the presentation properly; the visual is the asset, not the article.
3. Structure it geographically or by segment so readers can look up their own situation, which is what drives sharing.
4. Deliberately surface the surprising middle cases. His point is that New York City being unaffordable drives nothing - the in-betweeners that shock people are what produce the reaction journalists write about.
5. For a local campaign, seed it in the relevant small local subreddit where moderation is light, and let it reach that community's front page.
6. For a national campaign, accept it is much harder: the piece has to carry a genuinely counterintuitive national finding, and the outreach does more of the work than the seeding.
7. Cold email every journalist covering that beat or geography with the finding, the visual, and the traction.
8. Point your paid placements and niche edits at this page rather than at your commercial pages, then internally link from it to the pages that convert.
9. Judge it on links and referring domains earned, not on signups.

**Pitfall:** He says plainly that digital PR is only good for backlinks - you're not getting app signups or users from it. Judging the campaign on conversions will make a successful link campaign look like a failure.

**Apply:** Pabau's data-led link magnets should be built for links and coverage, measured that way, and kept separate from the bottom-of-funnel pages that carry the conversion target - the same page rarely does both jobs.

### 13. Canva trend-jacked Zoom backgrounds with a template category plus supporting content  `62.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 62*

The clearest tactical example in the episode, and it maps exactly onto the newsjacking pattern from elsewhere in the corpus. When the pandemic hit and Zoom usage exploded, Canva moved quickly and did two things at once: created an entire new template category specifically for Zoom backgrounds, and created a body of supporting content - blog posts and landing pages - all optimised around the keyword 'Zoom backgrounds'. So they captured the search surge with a product surface and a content surface simultaneously. The lesson the episode draws is that agility is the requirement - the ability to see the trend and ship both halves fast is what converts a spike into a durable category.

> "their approach to Zoom backgrounds"

**How to do it**

1. Monitor for demand surges in searches adjacent to what your product can already produce.
2. Ship the product surface first - a new category, template set, or preset for the trend.
3. Ship the content surface at the same time: landing pages and articles optimised for the trend keyword.
4. Point the content at the product surface so the search converts into usage.
5. Move before the SERP solidifies; the speed is what makes it work.
6. Keep the category if it persists, and prune it if the trend dies.

**Pitfall:** Shipping only the content half means you rank for a trend you can't satisfy; shipping only the product half means the trend traffic never finds it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's version is regulatory and treatment trends: when a new treatment or compliance requirement spikes, ship both the template or feature and the content for it at the same time, before the trade press consolidates the SERP.

**Apply anywhere:** When demand spikes around something your product can already satisfy, ship both the asset or feature and the content for it at the same time, before the trade press consolidates the SERP.

### 14. Check that a micro-influencer's content actually ranks before paying them  `19.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 19*

Before working with or paying any influencer for AEO/GEO purposes, take their most recent post descriptions or transcripts and paste them directly into Google to check whether the posts themselves are actually ranking, since most brands skip this and just assume reach equals ranking. Also check across every platform the influencer cross-posts the same video to, such as TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, and YouTube, since one platform's copy may rank while the others don't. Deliberately target micro-influencers, even those with only a few hundred to a few thousand followers, whose content demonstrably ranks, because they're both cheaper to work with and, since their ranking posts can be picked up and surfaced by LLMs, capable of influencing AI answers, essentially a low-cost form of "parasite SEO" through influencer UGC.

> "put those into Google, and see if the posts are ranking"

**How to do it**

1. Identify a candidate micro-influencer in your niche before reaching out with any paid offer.
2. Pull the text description or a transcript from several of their most recent posts.
3. Paste each description or transcript into Google search and check whether the corresponding post is actually showing up in the results.
4. Check every platform the influencer cross-posts the same video to, such as TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, and YouTube, since ranking often varies by platform even for an identical video.
5. Only shortlist influencers where you can confirm at least one platform's version of their content is ranking.
6. Use ranking confirmation as a negotiating point, since these creators are usually smaller and cheaper, and expect an affordable rate relative to their follower count.
7. Before finalizing, run a relevant prompt through an LLM such as ChatGPT to see whether it surfaces or would plausibly cite that influencer's ranking content, confirming AI-discoverability and not just Google ranking (inferred verification step).
8. Brief the confirmed influencer on the specific keyword-led description to use in their post before they publish it.

**Tools:** Google Search, ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Brands commonly pay influencers for UGC video content without ever checking whether that influencer's existing posts actually rank anywhere, and a brand-new account's content usually won't rank at all regardless of follower count.

### 15. Cold-email guests with a PR-plus-clips offer to hit 10-20% reply  `68.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 68*

Cody's outreach template for booking podcast guests leads with what the guest gets, not what you want. The wording he uses is roughly: hey, love the content you're posting on LinkedIn, can I host you on X show, it gets emailed out to 5,000 people and I'll give you clips for social, interested? He reports a 10 to 20% response rate with that exact template, varying with how big the person is. The mechanism he names is that you arrive not empty-handed: you are offering a free PR placement plus eight to ten social clips they can post themselves. There is a second-order effect he values more than the reply rate. The guest shares the appearance internally with their team, so suddenly a whole company knows who you are. His example: a product studio had sent a target CTO 11 LinkedIn DMs over 18 months with no reply, then sent this podcast invite and got a yes in seven minutes.

> "gets emailed out to 5,000 people and I'll give you clips"

**Evidence:** 10-20% response rate on the template; a CTO who ignored 11 LinkedIn DMs over 18 months replied yes within seven minutes to the podcast invite.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the single industry or niche you sell into before writing any outreach; if you cannot name it, do not start the show.
2. Build a target list of guests from people already posting substantive content on LinkedIn in that niche.
3. Send a short cold email or DM opening with a specific compliment on a post they wrote, not a generic one.
4. State the distribution number honestly: how many people the episode gets emailed to.
5. Offer eight to ten social clips cut from the episode as part of the invite.
6. End with a one-word ask ('interested?') rather than a calendar link.
7. Track reply rate by guest size and expect 10 to 20% overall.
8. After recording, deliver the clips fast so the guest shares the appearance internally with their team.

**Tools:** LinkedIn

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Hey, love the content that you're posting on LinkedIn. Can I host you on [X] show? It gets emailed out to 5,000 people and I'll give you clips for social. Interested?
```

**Pitfall:** Sending this before the show exists or with no real email list makes the distribution claim false, and a guest who finds out gets you a reputation problem in a small niche.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau could book clinic owners, aesthetic practitioners and industry suppliers onto a show using this exact template, and the clips deliverable is what makes it land. Each guest becomes a relationship, a case-study source and a link/mention opportunity at once.

**Apply anywhere:** Use this template to book industry experts onto your show. Lead with the email list size and the clips you will hand back, and the invite converts far better than a cold pitch asking for their time.

### 16. Earn independent corroboration because grounding discounts self-published claims  `76.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 76*

Under NEEATT, authoritativeness is granted, not claimed. The paper argues the LLM, seeking consensus across diverse sources before generating a response, must find independent evidence corroborating a brand's claims, and cites Chris Long's work on digital authority for the point that this is not a new marketing objective but one whose strategic weight has been elevated. The concrete mechanisms it names are mentions in reputable industry publications, analyst coverage, original research that others cite, and review profiles on trusted platforms. Each creates an independent data point the retrieval pipeline uses to confirm brand claims during grounding. The authors add a claim worth testing: AI recommendations appear harder to game than traditional search, partly because LLMs weigh earned media from reputable outlets more heavily than self-published content, and the grounding phase actively seeks multi-source corroboration while discounting single-source narratives.

> "Authoritativeness under NEEATT is granted, not claimed"

**Evidence:** Chris Long's digital authority work is cited; the paper argues earned media from reputable outlets is weighted more heavily than self-published content during grounding.

**How to do it**

1. List the three or four claims your brand most needs an engine to believe, such as category leadership or a specific capability.
2. For each claim, find whether an independent source already states it; if not, that is the PR brief.
3. Pitch industry publications in your vertical rather than general press, since the claim needs to be corroborated in the domain where the query is asked.
4. Publish original research with a dataset others can cite, so the citation itself carries your claim.
5. Get onto analyst coverage and trusted review platforms so aggregator scores add a second independent leg.
6. Verify by asking the answer engines the claim as a question and checking which sources they surface as grounding.

**Pitfall:** Stating a claim only on your own site. The grounding phase discounts single-source narratives, so an uncorroborated claim is filtered out rather than repeated.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should decide which two or three claims about the product it wants engines to repeat, then run digital PR aimed specifically at getting aesthetics trade publications to state those claims independently.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide which two or three claims you want engines to repeat, then run digital PR aimed specifically at getting trade publications in your vertical to state those claims independently.

### 17. Engineer the creator cascade: one outlier video, a hundred copies  `61.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 61*

The passive half of digital gravity. Jordan's observation from the Startup Ideas podcast: they find a tool, cover it, and two weeks later there are 40 videos about the same tool - and the company did nothing except put a good product out. Cody's explanation of the mechanic: creators are all chasing the thing most likely to go viral, and they're all looking for outlier videos to copy and remix. So the game as a founder is finding the outlier video format for your product, producing it repeatably, and letting the cascade happen. His concrete example is a friend running an Etsy analytics tool whose entire growth strategy is YouTube: he has about a hundred creators in his affiliate program doing a video a month, but what actually happens is roughly a thousand videos get posted about the product - 'I couldn't afford that ever.' The system version of it: find where your customer spends time, work out what will resonate there, then hire and contract creators to make that content. Both note the emerging job title of an in-house YouTuber, and the risk that comes with it - Jordan's cautionary example is Tommy Clark at Triple Whale, who was the face of their content, left, and was never quite replaced.

> "one creator to make a video that has a hundred creators"

**How to do it**

1. Find the outlier video format in your category - the one creators are already copying.
2. Produce that format yourself, or pay one credible creator to make the first version.
3. Recruit creators into an affiliate program rather than paying per video, so the cascade is self-funding.
4. Expect the multiplier: Cody's example is 100 affiliate creators producing roughly 1,000 videos.
5. Consider an in-house creator role if the channel is proven, but plan for their eventual departure.
6. Hedge the in-house face against other channels so the brand isn't one person's audience.

**Tools:** YouTube

**Pitfall:** Both flag the personality risk explicitly - Triple Whale and HQ Trivia are the examples of a brand whose gravity was attached to one face that left.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, an affiliate or partner program for clinic-facing creators and consultants would produce far more content than commissioning it directly - and the content should not depend on one presenter's face.

**Apply anywhere:** An affiliate or partner programme for creators and consultants in your niche will produce far more content than commissioning it directly - and the content should not depend on one presenter's face.

### 18. Find the outreach angle in a detail a press article mentioned but never explained  `162.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 162*

Peralta calls this the single most important step and says it takes about five minutes. He googles the target's company alongside a known press outlet, opens the first few results, and looks for interesting details that stand out but are not the focus of the story. Researching Amplitude Analytics, he searched techcrunch.com plus Amplitude, and found a passing mention that the company had unusually talented engineers, with no detail behind it. Because his client's audience wanted to know how to build an engineering team and nothing existed on how Amplitude did it, that gap became the angle. The mechanism is that a specific, unasked question stops the expert thinking 'I have answered this a million times', gives their company positive coverage, and proves you did research before pitching.

> "looking for interesting details that stand out to me but aren't the focus"

**Evidence:** Peralta says the whole process hinges on this step, and that the Amplitude angle came from a single unexplained TechCrunch line about talented engineers.

**How to do it**

1. Search a well-known trade outlet's domain plus the company name, for example site-style queries like techcrunch.com + Amplitude.
2. Open the first three or four articles about the company.
3. Highlight every claim that is asserted in passing but never explained, such as an unusual team, process or growth event.
4. Check whether anything already exists that explains that detail; if it does, discard it and pick another.
5. Test the surviving detail against your audience's pain points and keep the one they would most want explained.
6. Phrase the angle as a specific question about how the company did that one thing.
7. Open the pitch with that question, not with a request for an interview.

**Tools:** Google

**Pitfall:** Pitching the company's headline story back to them. Experts have answered that repeatedly and will decline or ignore it, and the resulting article is generic even if they say yes.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before pitching any aesthetic practice owner for a Pabau article, search trade press and their own site for one unexplained detail, such as how they staff a second location, and pitch that specific question instead of a general interview request.

**Apply anywhere:** Search trade press for a detail about the target's company that was mentioned but never explained, confirm nothing covers it, and build the pitch around that one unanswered question.

### 19. Host your own side event at a partner's industry conference  `173.3`

Once the immediate cash pressure lifted, Dane shifted from cold email to borrowing other companies' audiences. > "our party at CRO day had just as many attendees as Unbounce's did"

**Evidence:** KlientBoost's CRO Day taco party drew as many attendees as Unbounce's own event; the Unbounce relationship went on to drive their guest posting and traffic growth.

**How to do it**

> "If you want a guest post or link"

3. Set up a simple podcast format, even a basic recorded video call, if you don't already have one, specifically to use as your outreach vehicle.

### 22. Judge a piece on distribution, not on its first launch numbers  `172.9`
*core · content insights · source 172*

Evan Blum released his song and shared it on Facebook, YouTube and Instagram, and after five months it had 800 views. His other videos averaged around 1,200 views. The asset was not the problem. When the song reached the right audience five months later, through a fan community and then a retweet and then press, it took nearly 40,000 views in a day and a half and grew his subscriber base at the same time. Benji Hyam's framing is that the piece was already influencer-ripe and only lacked a route to the influencer's audience. The practical lesson is that a self-publish-and-share launch is not a test of the content. Before killing or rewriting a piece that flopped, check whether it was ever put in front of the audience it was built for.

> "But we only ended up with 800 views."

**Evidence:** Blum's song sat at 800 views for five months across Facebook, YouTube and Instagram, then reached nearly 40,000 views in a day and a half once it hit the right audience.

**How to do it**

1. Separate the two questions when a piece underperforms: was the asset weak, or did it never reach the right audience?
2. Check referral and social data to see whether the piece was seen by anyone outside your existing following.
3. If it never reached the target audience, run the distribution plan before touching the content.
4. Identify the communities, newsletters and individuals whose audience the piece was built for.
5. Seed it into one community and one newsletter, then measure over two weeks.
6. Only rewrite or retire the piece if it flops after it has genuinely been placed in front of the intended audience.
7. Keep older pieces in the promotion rotation, since the case study's window between publication and breakout was five months.

**Pitfall:** Declaring a piece a failure after a week of flat social numbers and moving on. You end up rewriting content that was fine and never fixing the actual constraint, which is distribution.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not retire or rewrite a blog article on the basis of its first month of traffic. David should confirm the piece was actively distributed to clinic-owner communities and newsletters before scheduling a rewrite.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not judge a piece by its launch-week numbers. Check whether it ever reached the audience it was built for, run the distribution plan first, and only rewrite or retire it after real placement has failed.

### 23. Launch a blog with a mega project no rival can copy  `123.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 123*

Benji Hyam's blog launch strategy has two requirements and he insists the first matters most: a post idea another company cannot easily replicate, then a plan to put it in front of a large audience. > "unique post idea that's hard for another company to replicate"

**Evidence:** The Apple Watch concept-art post was syndicated to over 200 tech and design publications and drove over 20,000 unique visitors within a week of launch.

**How to do it**

1. Reject the default launch post ideas first: a standard how-to, a listicle, or a roundup any competitor could publish.
2. Pick a news event or industry shift your audience is already watching, the way ThinkApps picked the Apple Watch announcement.
3. Choose an asset type that requires real effort: original design work, original data, a strong opinion, or a narrative nobody else has.
4. Recruit outside contributors who own the skill you lack, and pay them in promotion rather than cash.
5. Set the bar explicitly: would a reader say 'wow', and could a competitor reproduce this in a week?
6. Build the piece and the promotion plan together, not sequentially, so the launch date is fixed before the asset is finished.
7. Only after the asset passes the replication test, start pitching it.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not open a new blog or content hub with a post any competitor could write. Build one hard-to-replicate asset first, such as original survey data from your own customer base, and hold the launch date until that asset exists.

### 24. Make the influencer the subject of the piece, not a name in a roundup  `172.2`
*core · best practices · source 172*

Benji Hyam draws a hard line between content an influencer shares out of delight and content they share out of guilt or annoyance. He calls out the two common failures by name: rehashing something the influencer already said, and the roundup post that mentions dozens of people so each is pinged for a share. What worked in the case study was an original piece that wove the influencer's own details into the story and made her look good. Evan Blum researched Camila Cabello while writing, including that she often wears a bow-tie in her hair, and worked those details into the song. Hyam's rule is that the asset must clear two bars at once: the influencer would want to share it, and it is genuinely interesting to your own intended audience. Half-assing the piece to earn a share fails both.

> "out of guilt or annoyance, but out of delight"

**Evidence:** Evan Blum's song wove researched details about Camila Cabello, such as the bow-tie she often wears in her hair, and she retweeted it to 3.4M followers unprompted.

**How to do it**

1. Reject any format where the target is one of many names, including expert roundups and quote compilations.
2. Research the target's public work for 30 to 60 minutes and collect five specific details only a real follower would know.
3. Choose a format that makes the target the subject: a profile, a teardown of their method, or a story built around their work.
4. Work at least three of the collected details into the piece so it reads as researched, not generic flattery.
5. Check the draft against a second bar: would your own audience read this if the target's name were removed?
6. Cut the piece if it fails either bar rather than shipping it and hoping for a share.
7. Publish it on your own domain so the traffic and links land on your site, not a third party's.

**Pitfall:** Shipping a rushed piece purely as share-bait. The influencer either ignores it or shares once out of politeness, and your own audience gets nothing, so the piece has no second life.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's outreach pieces should profile a named aesthetics practice owner or industry educator in depth rather than running expert roundups, so the subject has a reason to share and the article still stands alone for clinic readers.

**Apply anywhere:** Build outreach assets that make one influencer the subject rather than listing many names. Research their public work, weave in specific details, and confirm the piece would still interest your own readers with the name removed.
