### 1. Turn vertical positioning into a most-tools-do-not-solve-this piece  `125.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert's second source of thought leadership is positioning: how your product does something differently or better than competitors. Their worked example is a CRM built for the specific needs of one industry while competitors sell generic CRMs for any vertical. The piece writes itself as an argument: most CRMs do not solve these problems for HVAC businesses, so we built a product that does, and here is how. Their point is that within that industry, a claim based purely on positioning reads as thought leadership even though no new research was done. This is the cheapest category to produce because the idea already exists inside the company's go-to-market, it just has not been argued in public. The work is naming the specific problems the generic tools ignore, not inventing an idea.

> "could be perceived as thought leadership"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's illustrative example of an industry-specific CRM versus generic multi-vertical CRMs, framed as: most CRMs do not solve XYZ problems for HVAC businesses, so we built a product that does.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the one vertical or segment your product was built for.
2. Interview sales for the specific problems generic competitors fail on in that segment, in the customer's words.
3. Keep only problems a generalist tool genuinely cannot solve, not ones it solves less prettily.
4. Title the piece as the argument: most X tools do not solve Y for Z, so we built one that does.
5. Devote one section per named problem, describing how the generic tool handles it today.
6. Show your own approach to each problem in the same order.
7. Cite a customer situation for at least one problem so the claim is not self-reported alone.
8. Link the piece from comparison and alternatives pages, where the same argument is being weighed.

**Pitfall:** The piece collapses if the named problems are ones any competitor also handles. The signal is that a reader can name a generic tool that already does each item on your list.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's positioning piece is the aesthetic and healthcare practice specificity against generic salon or generic CRM software. David should list the workflows a generic booking tool cannot handle, such as consent forms and clinical records, and argue them one per section.

**Apply anywhere:** Argue your vertical positioning as an article: name the specific problems generic tools in your category fail on for your segment, one section each, then show how you solve them.

### 2. Turn your own in-the-weeds process problem into an SEO-targeted post  `125.14`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert's implementation example is content attribution. Their business depended on showing ROI, so they needed to attribute leads to content. It is a nuanced piece of a larger process and an in-the-weeds topic, but they observed that most companies either do not know how to measure content attribution or do not care to, and nobody in their industry was talking about it. So they published a piece covering why most companies do not measure attribution or do it wrong, the Model Comparison Tool in Google Analytics that shows conversions from content, how to set that tool up yourself, and examples of blog post conversion data from different companies. They note the piece was also optimized for SEO and ranks in position 2 for content attribution, which is the point: an in-the-weeds process topic can be both the originality and the keyword target.

> "this post ranking in position 2 for"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's content attribution post, built around the Google Analytics Model Comparison Tool with setup instructions and multi-company conversion data, ranks in position 2 for content attribution.

**How to do it**

1. List the internal process steps your team argues about that outsiders never discuss.
2. Check each for search volume; if a keyword exists, the topic is both original and rankable.
3. Structure the piece as: what most people get wrong, the specific tool that solves it, how to set it up, then real examples.
4. Name the exact tool and screen, as they did with the Model Comparison Tool in Google Analytics.
5. Include a full setup walkthrough so the piece is usable, not just an argument.
6. Add real data examples from more than one company so the method is shown working.
7. Optimize the piece normally for the keyword, since the originality does not exempt it from on-page work.
8. Track the position, as a topic nobody covers can reach the top of page one quickly.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Treating an in-the-weeds topic as too niche to target a keyword. The opposite is true: nobody covering it means low competition, and the piece can rank while also being the differentiator.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's internal operational methods, such as how it measures no-show reduction or onboarding time, are both original and searchable. David should target those as keywords rather than treating them as internal-only material.

**Apply anywhere:** Take the internal process problem your team solved that nobody in your industry discusses, check it for search volume, and publish it as what most people get wrong, the tool that fixes it, setup steps and real data.

### 3. Unmaintained content goes to zero - the Bricks case  `56.12`
*core · content insights · source 56*

Cody's counterexample to 'AI content decays' is a company he worked with, an AI spreadsheet tool called The Bricks, that went from zero to 400,000 clicks a month in about 12 months targeting the 'how to Excel' keyword category, using exactly the process he describes. The person running it then left the company, nobody maintained the content, and it fell off a cliff. His conclusion is that the decay people attribute to AI content is actually the absence of maintenance: 'a website is a living breathing thing', and if you're not maintaining what you wrote, of course it goes to zero. He qualifies it - top-of-funnel content needs this treatment for certain, some bottom-of-funnel content does not - and Edward adds that a tool with a share feature can partially offset decay by continuously earning links.

> "they're now defunct so we can actually like talk about them"

**How to do it**

1. Assume every top-of-funnel article has an owner and a review cadence, or don't publish it.
2. Put refresh on a monthly cycle covering both your best articles and the ones falling off.
3. Track a decay metric per article (clicks versus its own trailing peak) rather than sitewide traffic.
4. Distinguish top-of-funnel pages, which need active maintenance, from bottom-of-funnel pages, which he finds hold up with less.
5. When a page is beyond saving, no-index it or 301 it rather than leaving it in the catalogue.
6. If the person running the programme leaves, treat the content portfolio as an unowned asset that will decay, and reassign it explicitly.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** The single-owner risk is the real lesson: a 400k-clicks-a-month asset collapsed because one person left and nobody picked up the maintenance loop.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's published library needs a named owner and a standing refresh cadence, because the risk isn't that articles were written badly - it's that a working programme quietly stops when whoever ran it moves on.

**Apply anywhere:** A published library needs a named owner and a standing refresh cadence, because the risk isn't that articles were written badly - it's that a working programme quietly stops when whoever ran it moves on.

### 4. Update a page only when intent changes or you have new news  `153.7`
*core · best practices · source 153*

Grow and Convert contradict the common refresh advice directly. The standard line is to update content on a schedule because Google rewards updates. In their experience you only need to update when the intent behind the keyword changes, or when you have something genuinely new to say that is relevant to the keyword, such as a new product feature. Updating for the sake of updating does not reliably improve results and often is not worth the effort. This sits against the broader base position that refreshing declining pages beats publishing new ones. The reconciliation is the trigger: they are arguing against calendar-driven refreshes, not against fixing a page that is losing rankings or whose SERP has changed shape.

> "you only need to update content if the intent behind the keyword changes"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's experience across 80+ clients since 2017: scheduled updates did not reliably improve rankings, while intent-change and new-information updates did.

**How to do it**

1. Delete any recurring calendar reminder to refresh posts on a fixed schedule.
2. Set two triggers for a refresh instead: the SERP for the keyword has changed format or dominant persona, or you have a genuinely new fact, feature or dataset.
3. Once a quarter, re-search each money keyword and compare the current top 10 against the mix you recorded when you wrote the page.
4. Flag for rewrite only where the format or persona has shifted.
5. Add a third trigger for measured decline: sustained impression or position loss over both a 30- and a 90-day window.
6. When a trigger fires, rewrite the substance rather than changing the date and swapping a few words.
7. Spend the freed-up hours on new posts in your topical lane or on stalled pages that need links.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Date-bumping and light-editing a whole library on a schedule. It consumes the production capacity that would otherwise go to new posts or to genuinely stale pages, and produces no ranking change.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should drop any blanket annual refresh of the /blog/ library. David should trigger rewrites from a quarterly SERP re-check plus GSC decline, and from product releases that change what a page should say about Pabau.

**Apply anywhere:** Stop refreshing on a schedule. Rewrite a page when the SERP for its keyword has changed shape, when you have genuinely new information, or when Search Console shows sustained decline across both 30- and 90-day windows.

### 5. Use a blog post for category keywords unless your domain can rank a product page  `130.6`
*core · best practices · source 130*

Grow and Convert usually prioritize software category keywords, but on this engagement they skipped them because the client had already taken those terms with in-house product landing pages. They flag the condition that made that possible: the client is a platform valued at over $5B with the domain and topical authority to rank product pages for category terms. Their stated rule for everyone else is that many SaaS websites do not have that authority, and for those businesses a blog post will likely rank higher or faster, or both, for the same keyword. The mechanism is the one they name as flaw one: product pages have limited room for keywords, exist to explain features rather than to meet search intent, and do not mention competitors, while ranking in the top few spots for software category keywords often requires listing software options.

> "Many SaaS websites don't have the domain or topical authority to rank"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client, a platform valued at over $5B, ranked category terms with in-house product pages; the agency states most SaaS sites lack that authority and should use posts instead.

**How to do it**

1. List your software category keywords, such as the plain 'X software' terms.
2. Check the current top 5 for each and note whether they are vendor product pages or editorial listicles.
3. Check whether your own product page already ranks on page 1 or 2 for the term in Search Console.
4. If listicles dominate and your product page is not ranking, assign the keyword to a blog post that lists and compares options including yours.
5. If your product page already ranks top 5, leave the term to the product page and do not build a competing post.
6. Where the blog post ranks, link it prominently to the product page so the visit still reaches the buying surface.
7. Recheck each category term annually, since authority gains can let a product page take over later.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Building a blog post for a category term your product page already ranks for creates cannibalization; building a product page for a listicle-dominated term wastes months because the page cannot satisfy the intent to compare options.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's authority does not match a $5B platform on generic terms like practice management software. David should target those with editorial comparison posts that include Pabau, not with the product pages, and reserve product pages for the terms where they already rank.

**Apply anywhere:** Only use a product page for a category keyword if your domain can actually rank one there. Check the SERP: if listicles hold the top spots and your product page is nowhere, write a comparison post instead, since it can meet the intent to see options and a product page cannot.

### 6. Use the introduction as the litmus test for whether a post has originality  `100.2`
*core · best practices · source 100*

The author says he is obsessed with blog introductions and treats the intro as a tell-tale sign of whether an article has originality. The reasoning is simple: if a piece has originality in it, you should sell that hard in the introduction, because the intro is a little ad telling the reader why your result beats the other results Google has waiting. So the converse holds. If writing the intro is hard, the post probably has no originality, and he says bluntly that the piece is likely not worth writing. A second signal is a generic opener. If your intro starts 'When it comes to [topic]', you are writing that because you have no idea what unique thing you have to add. He notes the exact phrase 'when it comes to content' has been written 123 million times.

> "if you're having trouble writing the intro, you may not have any originality"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own examples: the mobile checkout intro announces it is a report on an extensive study; the agency-scaling intro sells that the concept came from a large agency owner; the Customer Content Fit intro opens with a story.

**How to do it**

1. Write the introduction before the body of any article.
2. State the piece's original element in the first two or three sentences, not below the fold.
3. Ban formula openers such as 'When it comes to' and 'In today's world'.
4. Search the exact opening phrase you wrote and see how many results use it.
5. If you cannot articulate why this result beats the other page-one results, stop and re-plan the angle.
6. Kill the piece or find a new angle rather than writing a body around an empty intro.
7. Study proven intro shapes: an explicit study framing, a credibility claim about the source, or a first-person story.

**Pitfall:** Writers push through a weak intro on the assumption the body will save it. The article then ships with nothing to promote it in search snippets or social shares.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau articles, David should require the intro to be drafted and approved before the body, and reject any intro that could sit on a competitor's page unchanged. The intro must name the specific thing this piece has that the other results do not.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the introduction first and use it as a go/no-go gate. If you cannot say in two sentences why your page beats the other results, the piece has no original angle and should not be written yet.

### 7. Use the patient's outdated term, not the clinician's current one  `109.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 109*

Cognitive FX is a concussion treatment center in Provo, Utah. Its doctors dislike the term 'post-concussion syndrome' because 'persistent post-concussion symptoms' is the medically accurate and current description. Patients, and non-specialist clinicians, search for post-concussion syndrome and the abbreviation PCS. Grow and Convert used the patient's term across dozens of posts, targeting keywords like 'post concussion syndrome test', 'living with post concussion syndrome', 'what can a neurologist do for post concussion syndrome' and 'how to treat pcs'. These are high intent despite being the wrong term. This is the same pattern as the geothermal case but in a regulated, clinical field, where the pressure to use only the approved term is strongest and the gap between professional and patient vocabulary is widest.

> "The doctors at Cognitive FX are not fans of the term"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX grew to 70,000 pageviews in 14 months, with PCS-term articles used across dozens of posts.

**How to do it**

1. List the conditions, procedures or products your specialists describe with current professional terminology.
2. For each, find the older, lay or abbreviated name patients and non-specialists use.
3. Check search volume on both versions, including the abbreviation on its own.
4. Build the keyword list around the lay term with modifiers: test, living with, how to treat, what can a specialist do for.
5. Get clinical sign-off on accuracy of the content, not on the choice of keyword, and separate those two approvals in the review process.
6. Use the lay term in the title, URL and first sentence so the page matches the query.
7. Add one sentence early in the body giving the current correct term and why it changed.
8. Repeat the pattern across many posts rather than one, since the value came from dozens of pages.

**Pitfall:** Clinical reviewers reject the keyword and the accuracy edit together. Split the approval: they own whether the medicine is right, marketing owns which words the title uses.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's aesthetic and healthcare content faces the same split. Practitioners say one thing, patients and clinic owners search another. Pabau should target the lay term in titles and URLs while keeping the correct clinical term inside the article, and keep clinical review focused on factual accuracy.

**Apply anywhere:** In technical, medical or financial fields, patients and buyers search the outdated or lay term while your experts use the current one. Target the lay term in the title, URL and opening sentence, and give the correct term inside the body. Keep expert review focused on accuracy, not on word choice.

### 8. Weave the product into top-of-funnel posts too, not only buying-intent ones  `145.6`
*core · best practices · source 145*

Grow and Convert extend their sell-the-product rule past bottom-funnel pages. They say that even when you write about top-of-funnel topics that are not product-focused, the content has to weave in a compelling discussion of the product or service if it is going to convert. Their diagnosis of why most teams skip this is the advice not to be too salesy, paired with a hope that the reader remembers the brand later. They call that unlikely. The practical version is that a passing mention with a link to a product page is not a product discussion; the post needs a real passage explaining how the product does the thing the article is about, and why that differs from the alternatives.

> "weave in a compelling discussion"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's position across five years of client conversion tracking is that content without a product discussion underperforms regardless of the CTA design.

**How to do it**

1. Open each top-of-funnel post and find every reference to your own product.
2. If the only reference is a passing mention plus a link, mark the post as not selling.
3. Add a section that explains how your product performs the specific task the article covers.
4. State one concrete differentiator in that section rather than a feature list.
5. Keep the section tied to the article's topic so it reads as fulfilling the question, not as an ad break.
6. Leave the design furniture, pop-ups and CTA graphics in place but stop treating them as the conversion mechanism.
7. Re-check the post's conversion rate after the change, segmented to organic traffic.
8. Apply the same pass to every new brief so the product passage is commissioned, not retrofitted.

**Pitfall:** Relying on page design elements such as pop-ups, opt-in forms and CTA graphics to take care of conversions. They do not carry the argument, so the post gets read and the reader leaves without learning what you sell.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's informational blog posts need a real passage on how Pabau does the task in question, not just the standard Pabau section before the conclusion. David should treat the product passage as a briefing requirement for top-of-funnel posts as well as comparisons.

**Apply anywhere:** Even on top-of-funnel posts, include a genuine passage explaining how your product does the thing the article is about, with one concrete differentiator. A link plus a pop-up is not a product discussion.

### 9. Write a solution-agnostic problem statement before you brief an article  `75.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 75*

The Spicy Margarita author closes the piece with the research habit that drives everything else: before writing, state the reader's job as a problem, with the solution stripped out. His own worked example is the keyword "retention rate formula". The solution-focused version is "the reader needs the retention rate formula". The solution-agnostic version is "the reader wants to know their company's retention rate". The second version does not assume a formula, a blog post or an article at all, so it lets a calculator, a spreadsheet template or a landing page win the brief instead. He argues the reframe strips away assumptions about what format is right and opens up options a keyword-led brief never surfaces. He supplies a worksheet with a do and don't column of these statements for teams to copy.

> "solution-agnostic, problem statements as part of your research"

**Evidence:** The author's retention rate formula worked example, plus a published worksheet with paired solution-focused and solution-agnostic statements.

**How to do it**

1. Take the target keyword and write down the literal thing the searcher typed.
2. Ask what outcome the searcher wants once the search is over, and write that as one sentence.
3. Delete every noun from that sentence that names a solution, format or tool, including the word article.
4. Check the statement still reads as a goal the reader could state out loud, for example calculate our retention rate.
5. Note what the keyword implies about the reader's expertise, such as forgetting the formula meaning retention is not their day job.
6. Decide the format from the problem statement, not from what already ranks.
7. Add both columns, solution-focused and solution-agnostic, to the brief so the writer can see what was rejected.
8. Keep the statement at the top of the brief and check the finished draft against it before publishing.

**Pitfall:** Writing a statement that still names the deliverable, such as needs a guide to X. That smuggles the format back in and the brief collapses into the same blog post everyone else published.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a solution-agnostic problem statement line to the top of every Pabau brief. For a keyword like no-show rate, the statement is the practice wants to cut no-shows this month, which points at a calculator or a template page rather than another explainer.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a solution-agnostic problem statement to the top of every content brief. Write the outcome the reader wants with all solution nouns removed, then pick the page format from that statement rather than from the keyword's wording.

### 10. Write at sales-conversation depth, never at intro-guide level  `78.3`
*core · best practices · source 78*

Grow and Convert argue that content marketing's habit of writing everything as a beginner guide is a leftover from chasing high-volume SEO keywords, which skew beginner. That habit fails in AI search because users ask LLMs advanced, specific questions and the model wants to recommend brands that talk at the customer's level. Their benchmark is the depth of an actual sales conversation: detailed feature discussion, screenshots, specific scenarios where the product applies, use cases and case studies. This is a standing rule for every page, not a format for one asset. It also explains why generic 'what is X' content no longer earns recommendations.

> "at the depth of a sales conversation, not an intro guide"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert attribute the intro-guide habit to high-volume SEO keyword chasing and say beginner content does not help when customers ask LLMs advanced questions.

**How to do it**

1. Before drafting, write down the question a prospect would ask on a sales call about this topic, in their words.
2. Set the assumed reader as someone already evaluating options, not someone learning the category.
3. Cut any section that defines the category unless the page's whole intent is definitional.
4. Include product screenshots and name specific settings, fields or workflows rather than describing features abstractly.
5. Add at least one named scenario with the customer type, their situation and the outcome.
6. Link a case study that matches the scenario the page describes.
7. Review the draft against a recorded sales call and add any detail the rep gave that the page lacks.

**Pitfall:** Defaulting to beginner framing because that is where the search volume sits. The signal is an article whose first 300 words define the category, which is exactly the material an AI answer absorbs and replaces.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog articles should assume the reader is already comparing practice management systems. David should replace category definitions with named practice scenarios, Pabau screenshots and specific workflows.

**Apply anywhere:** Write every commercial page at the depth of a sales conversation. Assume the reader is comparing options, show screenshots, name specific scenarios and outcomes, and cut the category definition unless the page is definitional.

### 11. Write case studies at 3,000 words including the client challenge to you  `125.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert contrast their Leadfeeder case study with the standard agency or SaaS case study, which they parody as: this customer came to us, accounting used to take them a lot of time, now they use our software and it saves them time. That is not thought leadership. Theirs runs over 3,000 words and covers the strategy they were using at the start, a question their founder asked challenging the long-term viability of that approach, their own hesitations about a more SEO-focused strategy, a discovery in the conversion data that shifted the whole strategy into what they later named Pain Point SEO, and screenshots from Google Analytics and Data Studio backing the findings. The unusual element is including the client's challenge to them and their own hesitation. That transparency is what makes it unique rather than the word count.

> "It's a 3,000+ word story that discusses"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's Leadfeeder case study, over 3,000 words, covering scaling Leadfeeder to over 200 signups per month and the data discovery that became Pain Point SEO, with Google Analytics and Data Studio screenshots.

**How to do it**

1. Choose an account where your own approach changed as a result of the work.
2. Open with the strategy you were running at the start, including what you believed at the time.
3. Include the question the client asked that challenged your approach, in their words.
4. State your own hesitations about changing course honestly rather than editing them out.
5. Describe the specific discovery in the data that forced the change.
6. Include screenshots of the actual analytics dashboards behind each claim.
7. Name the resulting method so it becomes a citable concept.
8. Budget 3,000 words or more, and cut the piece rather than thinning it if it will not sustain that.
9. Get written client sign-off on every figure and screenshot before publishing.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Google Data Studio

**Pitfall:** Most teams strip out the parts where they were wrong, and what remains is the before-and-after template nobody links to. The signal is a case study with no moment of doubt in it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's practice case studies should include the part where the practice pushed back or the rollout stalled, plus real dashboard screenshots. A 3,000-word case study on one clinic beats six one-page testimonials.

**Apply anywhere:** Write case studies long enough to include the client's challenge to your approach, your own hesitation, the data discovery that changed your mind, and screenshots of the actual dashboards. Around 3,000 words, one account.

### 12. Write in first person to separate a post from 80 percent of the web  `100.5`
*core · best practices · source 100*

The author calls first person the best hack to originality he knows. His reasoning is about reader perception: most articles are a stale third-person list of advice, so opening in first person immediately makes the reader think this is an actual personal account, that it must be real, must have knowledge behind it, and is not just a paid piece written for Google. He estimates it distinguishes a post from 80 percent of posts online. In their 'How to get a marketing job' example, first person came with real personal stories from Benji's life plus an honest section on bullshitting your way through interviews, which none of the other page-one results matched. Note the honesty is doing the work alongside the pronoun. First-person padding with no real account does not qualify.

> "That's the best a hack to originality that I know"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's 'How to get a marketing job' post ranks #2, carrying Benji's first-person stories and an honest section on bullshitting through interviews that no other page-one result had.

**How to do it**

1. Assign each article to a named person with genuine experience of the topic.
2. Open the piece with a first-person account of a specific moment, not a general claim.
3. Include at least one story with names, numbers or dates that only that author could tell.
4. Add the section the competitors avoid, such as what actually goes wrong or what people fake.
5. Keep the third-person how-to structure for the practical steps so the piece still serves the query.
6. Publish under the author's byline with a real bio, not a house account.
7. Check the other page-one results and confirm none carries a comparable personal account.

**Pitfall:** First-person voice with no real story behind it reads as a manufactured personal brand and does not create the credibility effect. The signal is that a competitor could swap their name in and nothing would change.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles about running an aesthetic practice should carry named practitioners or named Pabau staff telling first-hand accounts, not neutral third-person advice. David can pair each article with one short interview to source the account.

**Apply anywhere:** Give each article a named author with real experience and open with a first-person account containing specifics only that person could supply. Neutral third-person advice is what everyone else already published.

### 13. Write in semantic triples: name the entity, skip pronouns  `26.10`
*core · best practices · source 26*

A semantic triple (subject-predicate-object, e.g., 'James Dooley is 42 years old') is how Google's parser connects a fact to a specific named entity; writing 'I am 42 years old' or 'it is a great keyword research course' leaves the parser with an ambiguous 'I' or 'it' that can't be resolved to anything. Dooley's rule is to always name the entity explicitly as the subject ('Compact Keywords is a great keyword research course,' 'Edward Sturm is the founder of Compact Keywords') in every sentence meant to build topical or entity association, avoiding pronouns like 'it' or 'he' entirely in those key sentences. This is a free, simple editing discipline applicable to every page, bio, and caption a site publishes.

> "how you connect things together within sentence format"

**How to do it**

1. When drafting or editing any sentence meant to associate a fact with your brand, product, or a person, write the literal name as the grammatical subject instead of 'it,' 'he,' 'she,' 'we,' or 'this.'
2. Apply this specifically to About pages, author bios, image captions, case study intros, and any sentence stating a product fact or claim.
3. Read back each such sentence and check whether it would still make sense to someone with zero prior context — if it relies on a pronoun referring to something several sentences back, rewrite it with the explicit name.
4. Search a draft article for standalone instances of 'it is,' 'it's,' 'this is,' and 'he/she is' at the start of sentences and replace the pronoun with the entity name where the sentence states a fact about that entity. (inferred)
5. Repeat this check on every new page or content piece before publishing, since the payoff compounds the more consistently the entity is named across the site.

**Pitfall:** Writing 'it is a great keyword research course' instead of 'Compact Keywords is a great keyword research course' leaves the parser with no way to resolve what 'it' refers to, wasting a sentence that could otherwise have reinforced the entity association.

### 14. Write more casually than you used to now that AI sets the formal baseline  `72.5`
*core · best practices · source 72*

Khanal says that since AI writing took hold he feels even more unapologetic about writing as casually as possible. His reasoning is comparative: AI has a standard tone and formality, so adding casual human phrasing now makes a piece stand out where before it would have read as unprofessional. He gives a live example, choosing 'buckets' over 'categories' and noting he would probably not have published 'buckets' before AI. He also accepts unpolished sentences: mid-edit he says a sentence is not the most polished but in the age of AI writing an unpolished sentence is fine. He adds the caveat that this is not literally writing how you speak, and that he can do it because he is effectively a native English speaker, so writers should calibrate to their own command of the language.

> "I feel even more unapologetic of writing as casually as possible"

**Evidence:** Khanal notes his own edit made a sentence sound more corporate than the AI original, which he called bad, and he deliberately kept the informal 'buckets'.

**How to do it**

1. Read your draft and mark every word you chose because it sounded professional rather than natural.
2. Swap the safest of those for the plain word you would use out loud, for example 'buckets' for 'categories'.
3. Keep a sentence that reads slightly unpolished if it is direct and clear, rather than smoothing it.
4. Add italics or emphasis where you would stress a word in speech, since AI rarely does this.
5. Read the paragraph aloud and cut any clause that you would never say.
6. Calibrate the amount of casual phrasing to your own fluency rather than copying someone else's register.
7. Leave the formal register in place where accuracy matters, such as factual or compliance claims.

**Pitfall:** Overcorrecting into slang or writing exactly as you speak. Khanal is explicit that it is not literally writing how you speak, and mid-edit he catches his own prose sounding more corporate than the AI's.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's style rules already push plain US English. Extend that to accepting a slightly unpolished but direct sentence in blog prose rather than smoothing every sentence toward the register AI defaults to.

**Apply anywhere:** Now that AI sets a uniformly formal baseline, deliberately keep plain and slightly unpolished phrasing in your drafts, because that is what now reads as human.

### 15. Write pain points that name who suffers and what it costs  `126.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 126*

Grow and Convert distinguish surface-level pain-point one-liners from the kind that only come out of an interview. In the TapClicks piece the pains name the role experiencing them, for example that account managers are often spending most of their week building various reports for different stakeholders, and they name the business consequence, for example that in large enterprises underperforming campaigns can be allowed to stay live for weeks or longer. Both details came from April, a customer success manager who works with enterprise customers every day. Their test is direct: if we wrote this piece without interviewing someone at TapClicks, would we know a detail like this, and would we think to include it? A pain point with no role attached and no consequence attached is a pain point a competitor also has.

> "Account managers are often spending most of their week"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert attribute both TapClicks details to an interview with April, a customer success manager, and say there is zero chance they would have known them otherwise.

**How to do it**

1. In the interview, ask the expert which job title complains about this problem most often.
2. Ask what that person does today as a workaround, and how long it takes them.
3. Ask what it costs the business when the problem goes unsolved, in money, time or missed action.
4. Write each pain as one sentence containing a role, a behavior and a duration or cost.
5. Delete any pain point that could be pasted onto a competitor's blog unchanged.
6. Attribute the detail internally to the interview so the fact-check pass can verify it.

**Pitfall:** Writing pains as abstract nouns like inefficiency or lack of visibility. Those pass an editor and convert nobody, because the reader cannot recognize themselves in them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau pain points should name the role in a practice: the front-desk coordinator rekeying appointments, the practitioner writing notes after hours, the owner reconciling two payment reports. Pull these from Pabau's own CS team rather than from competitor blogs.

**Apply anywhere:** Make every pain point name a job title, the workaround that person performs today, and what it costs the business. Get those three things from an internal interview; a pain point without them is generic.

### 16. Write product content as the pitch an LLM will deliver for you  `86.6`
*core · best practices · source 86*

Grow & Convert's framing is that in AI search the LLM acts as your SDR. It reads your content and your competitors' content, synthesizes it, and delivers a recommendation in its own wording, so your content is no longer just the thing that ranks, it is the thing that teaches the model how to position you. Their evidence is a ChatGPT answer to 'best pain point focused SEO agency' that recommended Grow & Convert and described them as an agency that 'prioritizes content that solves specific buyer problems and targets bottom-of-funnel keywords with clear purchase intent'. That phrasing came from their own site and their Pain Point SEO framework. The requirement this creates is specificity. Content that could describe any vendor in the category gives the model nothing to match a user against, so the model has no basis to recommend you over anyone else.

> "LLMs are acting as your SDRs"

**Evidence:** ChatGPT recommended Grow & Convert for 'best pain point focused SEO agency' and described them using the exact positioning language from their own site and their Pain Point SEO framework.

**How to do it**

1. On every bottom-of-funnel page, state explicitly who the product is for: named industries, company sizes, use cases and personas.
2. State the concrete problems it solves in the customer's words, not vague benefit language.
3. State how it solves them differently from named competitors, not just that it is better.
4. Add proof: case studies, specific results, named customer examples.
5. Read the page back and ask whether a competitor could publish it verbatim; if yes, it is too generic to earn a recommendation.
6. Query ChatGPT for your category's recommendation prompt and read how it describes you; the wording tells you which page it pulled from.
7. Fix the source page when the description is wrong or generic, rather than trying to correct the model.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Prompt / template:**

```text
best pain point focused SEO agency
```

**Pitfall:** Positioning statements that live only on the homepage or in sales decks never reach the model, because the pages that rank for commercial queries are usually blog posts that say nothing specific about the product.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau comparison, alternatives and category page needs an explicit paragraph naming the practice types Pabau serves, the specific workflow problems it fixes, and how that differs from the named competitor, with a case study attached. Generic clinic-software prose gives ChatGPT nothing to repeat.

**Apply anywhere:** Every comparison, alternatives and category page needs an explicit paragraph naming who you serve, the specific problems you fix, how that differs from the named competitor, and a case study. Generic category prose gives the model nothing to repeat.

### 17. Write the What to look for section about the thing that drives users mad  `134.11`
*core · best practices · source 134*

Grow and Convert include a 'What to look for' section in many of their review articles, and they are specific about how theirs differs. Most software review articles fill that section with basics: operating system compatibility, how many user seats you get, pricing. Theirs cuts to the heart of the things that will drive you nuts when using any competitor's solution, which is to say the pain point that made the reader type the query into Google in the first place. The section then doubles as the criteria the rest of the article judges each tool against, so the list is not arbitrary. This is the same discipline as their claim-backing rule applied at the structural level: every criterion has to trace back to a real frustration, and the article backs each claim with an explanation of impact rather than asserting it.

> "the thing(s) that will drive you nuts when using any competitor's solution"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use this section format across client review articles, including the top-converting Rainforest QA listicle.

**How to do it**

1. Collect the recurring complaints from competitor reviews gathered during the research phase.
2. Pick the three or four that would actually make someone abandon a tool, not the spec-sheet items.
3. Write each as a criterion phrased from the reader's frustration, not from a feature name.
4. Delete generic criteria such as device compatibility, seat counts and price tiers unless they are the actual pain.
5. State why each criterion matters, with the consequence of getting it wrong.
6. Judge every product in the listicle against those same criteria in the same order.

**Pitfall:** Filling the buying-guide section with specifications. It reads as filler and gives the reader no basis to choose, so they leave to compare elsewhere.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau's 'best software' pages the criteria should be the things that infuriate practice managers in rival systems, such as consent forms that do not sync to the record or reports that cannot separate practitioners, not seat counts and platform support.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your buying-guide criteria from the complaints in competitor reviews, phrased as frustrations with consequences, and score every product against the same list.

### 18. Write to the user's described context, not just the keyword, because the model carries it  `81.16`
*core · content insights · source 81*

Grow and Convert explain a second reason differentiator detail matters. An LLM develops an understanding of the user's context, intent and pain points through both the current conversation and past ones, then searches the web for solutions that best align with that accumulated context. So the match is not made against a keyword string, it is made against a described situation. If your content does not clearly explain how your solution addresses those needs, the model cannot connect your offering to the user's circumstances and visibility is limited even when your page is in the retrieved set. Being retrieved and being recommended are separate outcomes, and the second one depends on whether your page contains the situational detail the model needs.

> "AI develops a deep understanding of users (their context, intent, and pain points)"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report that when a borrower describes wanting a fast, low-down-payment lender that will not reject late, LLMs are far more likely to recommend Constitution Lending because the content states exactly that.

**How to do it**

1. Write out five customer situations in full sentences, the way a buyer would describe their circumstances to a chatbot.
2. For each situation, name the constraint that makes generic options fail: budget, timeline, team size, regulation, location.
3. Add a passage to the relevant page that names the situation and states plainly what you do about it.
4. Use the customer's vocabulary from interviews rather than internal product terminology.
5. Test each situation as a multi-sentence prompt describing the circumstances, not as a keyword.
6. Note whether the answer recommends you and whether it repeats your situational wording.
7. Where you are retrieved but not recommended, add the missing constraint detail to the page.
8. Re-test after recrawl and keep a log of which situations you now win.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Optimizing only for the short keyword. A page can rank and be retrieved, then still be passed over because it never states which situations it suits.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's pages should describe real practice situations, for example a two-room clinic switching from paper records or a group adding a second location, and state what Pabau does in each. Feature lists alone will not match a described situation.

**Apply anywhere:** Write for the situation a buyer would describe to a chatbot, not just the keyword. Name the constraints that make generic options fail and state plainly what you do about each, then test with full multi-sentence prompts rather than short queries.

### 19. Write toward the reader's job acts instead of listing job titles  `124.13`
*core · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert give this as one of their most repeated pieces of writer feedback. The failing pattern is an intro that lists titles: when you are an HR manager, founder, COO, or any other admin who handles time tracking, you know how difficult it can be to track time worked. They argue nobody reads that and thinks oh hey, I am an HR manager, and that titles are insufficient anyway for describing the breadth of the audience. Their edit instruction is exact: remove the job title, but still convey to the reader who the post is for by focusing on the tasks associated with the job. The rewrite opens on the work itself, managing time off requests segmented into paid time off and sick days, leave balances, total hours worked and carryover from last year. A middle manager, team lead or HR manager all recognize themselves in those tasks.

> "Remove the job title, but still convey to the reader"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's before-and-after time clock software intro, where the title list is replaced with the specific tasks of managing time off requests, leave balances, hours worked and carryover.

**How to do it**

1. Search the draft for every job title and delete them all from the opening paragraph.
2. List the four or five concrete tasks the reader performs while doing this job.
3. Name the sub-parts of each task, for example segmenting requests into paid time off and sick days.
4. Open the piece with those tasks in a single sentence describing what the reader is trying to achieve.
5. State why the current way of doing those tasks is not working, immediately after.
6. Check the opener would be recognized by every seniority level that performs the task.
7. Reinstate a title only where it is factually load-bearing, such as who has permission to act.
8. Apply the same test to headings and CTA copy, not just the intro.

**Pitfall:** Replacing one title with a longer list of titles to be inclusive. The list signals to every reader that the writer does not know who they are, and it still says nothing about the work.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles often open with a list such as clinic owners, practice managers and front desk staff. David should replace those with the actual tasks, for example reconciling no-shows against deposits or chasing consent forms before a treatment.

**Apply anywhere:** Delete the job titles from your opener and describe the specific tasks the reader is performing and why they are not working. Titles never make a reader feel addressed, and they under-describe who your audience actually is.

### 20. Writing bottom-funnel keywords in a top-funnel style is the core mistake  `44.5`
*core · content insights · source 44*

Across both bottom-of-funnel worked examples (the plumber service page and the room-planner app page), the diagnosed root mistake is identical: the copy is written in a top-of-funnel, informational register — explaining what the general category/service means — even though the actual keyword carries bottom-of-funnel, high purchase-intent. Because a bottom-of-funnel searcher already knows the category and is specifically deciding who to buy from, an informational opening reads as irrelevant filler, pushes them back to search results, and even when they stay, leaves less patience and trust for the business to convert them; correcting the register (a confident, sales-oriented statement about the specific business) is presented as directly improving both retention and conversion together.

> "written in a top-of-funnel way even though the keyword was"

**Evidence:** The two paired before/after copy examples (the "24-hour plumber Hoboken" service page and the "room planner app" product page) are presented as parallel illustrations of the identical underlying mismatch between funnel-stage intent and writing register.

**Apply at Pabau:** When auditing or briefing new Pabau pages, explicitly classify each target keyword's funnel stage first, then check the drafted opening sentence against it — a bottom-of-funnel or feature/pricing page that opens by defining the general software category (rather than confidently stating what Pabau specifically does for the reader) is the single most common and costly copywriting mistake this source identifies.

**Apply anywhere:** When auditing or briefing new pages, explicitly classify each target keyword's funnel stage first, then check the drafted opening sentence against it — a bottom-of-funnel or feature/pricing page that opens by defining the general software category (rather than confidently stating what your site specifically does for the reader) is the single most common and costly copywriting mistake this source identifies.
