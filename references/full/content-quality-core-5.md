### 1. Judge intros against the target buyer, never against your own taste  `127.9`
*core · best practices · source 127*

Grow and Convert open by refusing to let the reader answer the question they just asked. They show two CRM intros, then say they do not really care which one you prefer, because the two introductions are not for you. They are for sales managers at B2B enterprise sales organizations. Only after making the reader inhabit that persona do they declare that there is a right answer and it is number two. The procedural point is that intro review is not a preference exercise, and the usual review meeting, where marketers say which opener they like, is measuring the wrong audience. The correct question is which one makes the target buyer feel that the author gets them. That reframing is what turns intro editing into something with a right answer rather than a taste debate.

> "the two introductions above aren't for you"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state outright that they do not care which intro the reader prefers because the intros are written for B2B enterprise sales managers.

**How to do it**

1. Before any intro review, write the target reader's role, seniority and years of experience at the top of the doc.
2. Instruct reviewers to answer one question only: which version makes that person feel the author gets them.
3. Rule out of scope any comment about which version the reviewer personally prefers or finds punchier.
4. Where reviewers disagree, resolve it by checking which version matches something the persona would actually say about their problem.
5. Bring one real customer quote or interview line into the review as the tiebreaker.
6. Record the decision and the reason, so the same debate does not repeat on the next article.
7. Where nobody on the team can answer as the persona, book a customer interview instead of guessing.

**Pitfall:** Marketing teams review their own copy as readers, and marketers are almost never the target buyer. The tell is review feedback phrased as 'I like' or 'this flows better' with no reference to the buyer's knowledge level.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau intro reviews should be judged against a named practice owner or clinic manager persona, not internal preference. If nobody in the review can answer as that person, the answer comes from a customer call, not the room.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not review introductions by asking which one the team prefers. Write the target buyer's role and experience level at the top of the review doc and judge only whether that person would feel understood. If nobody can answer as that person, interview a customer.

### 2. Judge software listicles on editing time, not surface criteria  `72.25`
*core · concrete actions · source 72*

Khanal's read of the ranking pages' shared weakness is that every one of them evaluates tools on surface criteria: ease of use, price, template count. His complaint is not that these are wrong but that none of them ask the questions that determine editing time. He calls editing time the ultimate factor in picking a writing tool, because the goal of these tools is always time savings, so anything that does not affect how long you spend fixing the output is a tiebreaker rather than a decision criterion. He generalizes the move: find what every ranking page evaluates on, then find the criterion that actually determines the buyer's outcome, and build your page around that instead. It is his method for making a listicle non-generic without inventing new facts.

> "none of them actually ask questions that determines the editing time"

**Evidence:** Khanal's SERP analysis found the shared weakness across all ranking pages was surface-level criteria, and he built his article's three factors around editing time instead.

**How to do it**

1. List the evaluation criteria used by every page ranking for your target listicle keyword.
2. Mark which of those are surface criteria: price, ease of use, feature counts, template counts.
3. Identify the one outcome the buyer actually cares about, such as total time spent to a publishable result.
4. Work out which product properties genuinely drive that outcome.
5. Build your evaluation criteria from those properties and say explicitly that the common criteria are tiebreakers.
6. Structure the body sections to answer each of your criteria in turn.
7. Keep the surface criteria in the piece, but late and framed as tiebreakers rather than decision factors.

**Pitfall:** Discussing competing articles by name in your own copy. Khanal says he sees writers on his team directly reference the other posts on the SERP, and while it is not wrong, he avoids it as a style.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau software listicles, find the outcome practice owners actually buy for, such as admin hours removed per week, and build the criteria around that rather than around feature counts and pricing tiers everyone else lists.

**Apply anywhere:** Find the criterion every ranking listicle ignores but that actually drives the buyer's outcome, and build your evaluation around it, relegating price and feature counts to tiebreakers.

### 3. Judge the content operation on leads, not traffic volume  `122.6`
*core · best practices · source 122*

Grow and Convert define a working content operation as one driving useful leads or sales for the business, and say explicitly that they do not care whether it gets traffic. They back this with two client examples that sit at opposite ends of the traffic scale: one blog with under 1,000 visitors a month and another with over 200,000. Both companies hired them for the same reason, that the blog was not producing enough useful leads, and in some cases none at all. The point is that traffic is not evidence of content-customer fit. A blog can be large, well written and popular and still be aimed at people who will never buy, which is why the diagnosis has to be run on lead quality rather than sessions.

> "We don't even care if it gets traffic or not"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite one client blog under 1,000 monthly visitors and another over 200,000 that both hired them for the same lead problem.

**How to do it**

1. Define the content operation's success metric as useful leads or sales, and write it down before the next reporting cycle.
2. Pull the last 12 months of leads and tag each with the page that produced it.
3. List every page that produced zero leads despite meaningful traffic and mark it for re-aim or retirement.
4. Check the profile of the leads produced against the best-customer profile, not just the count.
5. Report traffic as a diagnostic input only, never as the headline number.
6. Re-run the check quarterly so a page that stops producing leads is caught early.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** A high-traffic blog masks the problem for years. Because sessions look healthy nobody audits fit, and the team keeps producing more of the content that is failing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should tag demo requests by landing page and check which blog and template pages have ever produced one. Pages with traffic and zero demo requests over a year are candidates for re-aiming at practice owners rather than at general readers.

**Apply anywhere:** Measure your blog by the leads or sales it produces, not sessions. A large blog and a tiny blog can fail in exactly the same way.

### 4. Keep 60-70% of an article in 20-25 word capsule answers  `07.9`
*core · best practices · source 07*

This is the exact specification for applying the content-capsule technique across a full article: roughly 60-70% of the piece should be capsule-style (immediate, self-contained answers directly under a heading), with the remainder allowed to be non-capsule explanatory/process content. Each capsule should target 20-25 words, though going up to 50 words is acceptable if it's still tightly answering the question rather than meandering. Capsule sentences should contain no inline hyperlinks, but any factual or numeric claim anywhere in the article must be cited with a link to a high-quality source at the point the claim is made. A first-person experience statement should also be worked into every article, since it's presented as the one differentiator AI-generated competitor content structurally cannot replicate.

> "around 60-70% of the content written in this content-capsule technique"

**How to do it**

1. For each H2/H3 in the draft, write the first 1-2 sentences directly beneath it as a self-contained answer of about 20-25 words (up to 50 words if still tightly on-topic).
2. Read that capsule sentence in isolation and confirm it would fully make sense if an AI or featured snippet displayed only that sentence out of context.
3. Do not place hyperlinks inside the capsule sentence itself.
4. Immediately after the capsule, add expanded detail, examples, or a table in ordinary non-capsule prose.
5. Scan the full draft and confirm roughly 60-70% of sections follow the capsule-first pattern, leaving some sections as pure explanatory/process content.
6. Wherever the draft states a number, price, statistic, or factual claim anywhere in the article, add an inline citation link to a credible source at that exact point.
7. Add at least one first-person experience sentence per article that a generic AI-written competitor page would not contain.
8. Before publishing, re-read each section on its own to confirm it still reads as a complete, standalone answer.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Claude

**Pitfall:** Converting the entire post into wall-to-wall Q&A capsules — the creator explicitly says 'not really' 100%; aim for 60-70% capsule content mixed with normal explanatory sections, or the piece reads mechanically.

### 5. Keep writing listicles and product pages: 78% of AI citations are them  `87.5`
*core · content insights · source 87*

Grow and Convert broke down every cited source by content type. 78% of all cited sources for high-buying-intent prompts were listicles, product pages, homepages and pricing pages, in other words ordinary marketing and SEO content. The share barely moved for the 60% of citations that did not appear in Google or Bing SERPs at all, falling only to about 74%. So the content that gets cited looks like the content you would publish to rank, whether or not it currently ranks. Their example is a Calendly post, The 13 best appointment scheduling apps on the market, which ranked for a fan-out query about scheduling without back-and-forth email and put Calendly first on its own list. This answers the recurring question about whether content formats need to change for AI. They do not.

> "78% of all cited sources for these high buying intent"

**Evidence:** 78% of cited sources were listicles, product, home or pricing pages; ~74% among sources not found in either SERP.

**Pitfall:** Rewriting a working library into AI-flavored formats, chunked answers and summary blocks, on the belief that classic formats are obsolete. The study says the classic formats are what gets cited.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's listicle and template pages are the right format for AI citation. Keep Pabau first in its own listicles and keep the pricing tables complete, since pricing pages are in the cited set.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep publishing listicles, product pages and pricing pages. They are what AI answers cite for buying-intent prompts, whether or not they currently rank.

### 6. Kill the dev-shop cliches: end-to-end delivery and a listening-first process  `159.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 159*

Grow and Convert use software development agencies as the worst case of undifferentiated positioning. They joke they can predict what any dev shop will claim: some version of 'we'll take your idea to completion', 'start to finish' or 'end to end', plus a 'unique process' whose step one is always getting to know the business or listening to you. They show five landing pages doing exactly this. Their fix is to replace those lines with specifics drawn from the targeting and pain point work. They give two lists of replacements. Customer specifics: ecommerce web design agency, experts in Shopify Plus, we do B2B websites really well, we design the best SaaS marketing sites. Pain point specifics: we specialize in SEO optimized websites, we build websites in under three weeks, we have copywriters not just designers, we do ongoing IT maintenance of your ecommerce backend.

> "they'll all mention their "unique process"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show five separate web development landing pages all making versions of the same end-to-end and listening-first claims.

**How to do it**

1. Search your site copy for 'end to end', 'start to finish', 'idea to completion' and any process step named 'we listen' or 'we get to know your business'.
2. Delete every one of those lines rather than rewording them.
3. Replace each with either a customer specific (a platform, a segment, a company type) or a pain point specific (a turnaround time, a capability competitors lack, an ongoing service).
4. Check each replacement against three competitor sites; if any of them could publish the same line, it is not specific enough.
5. Confirm delivery can actually meet the specific claim, especially numeric ones like a three-week build.
6. Put the strongest specific in the H1 or subhead, not buried in a process section.
7. Re-audit annually, since generic language creeps back with every site refresh.

**Pitfall:** Rewording the cliche instead of replacing it. 'From concept to launch' is the same claim in new clothes.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this search across pabau.com's product and template pages and swap any generic 'all-in-one, end to end' line for a specific claim, such as a named integration, a stated onboarding timeline or an aesthetics-specific capability.

**Apply anywhere:** Grep your site for end-to-end and listening-first process claims, delete them, and replace each with a platform, segment, timeframe or capability a competitor could not copy.

### 7. Learn where the process breaks, not how the industry works  `134.3`
*core · best practices · source 134*

Grow and Convert's argument for why domain expertise is optional is a scoping rule. Bottom-of-funnel readers are not looking for education about their industry; they already know their problem and want assurance that a product fixes it. So the writer only needs to understand the moments when a complex process breaks, not the process running smoothly. They point out that the breakages cluster in predictable places: transitions, communication errors, transportation problems, organization of data, transfer of records, misunderstandings between people. They also argue most B2B products solve mundane problems that recur across industries, such as moving information from A to B without a breach, or consolidating data without re-keying it into Excel. With Rainforest QA it was tempting to learn every software testing best practice; all they actually needed was where the common approach to testing fails.

> "You just need to know about the moments when their complex processes break."

**Evidence:** For Rainforest QA the team skipped software testing best practices entirely at the start and only studied where the common testing approach fails.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the reader's current workflow as a sequence of steps, without the product in it.
2. Mark every transition point in that sequence: handoffs, transfers, re-entry of data, approvals.
3. Ask the internal expert what goes wrong at each marked point and how often.
4. Translate each breakage into the plain human problem underneath it, such as lost records or duplicated work.
5. Scope the research phase to those breakages and drop the rest of the industry education.
6. Write each article against one breakage and the feature that removes it.

**Pitfall:** Writers try to reach parity with the audience's industry knowledge and stall, or produce top-of-funnel education the buyer never asked for. The signal is a draft that explains the field for several hundred words before naming a problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** For aesthetic and healthcare practices the breakages are the ones Pabau already fixes: no-shows between booking and appointment, consent forms redone on paper, records split between systems, stock counts drifting. David should scope research for a new article to one of those transition points, not to the clinical topic around it.

**Apply anywhere:** Map the reader's workflow, mark the handoffs and transitions, and research only what breaks there. The mundane failure at a transition is what the buyer is searching about, not the field itself.

### 8. Make the product's real differentiator the feature of the article  `96.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 96*

Grow and Convert's writing step turns customer research into an angle. Their in-depth customer research found TapClicks' main differentiator was template editing: one edit to a dashboard template applies automatically to every live client dashboard using it, where competitors required manual per-client updates. They made that the feature of the piece. They titled the post 'The Paid Search Dashboard That Scales Across Clients' to carry both the article positioning and the company positioning in the title. Per their Pain Point SEO approach, they opened with the pain points that drove customers to seek a dashboard tool, explained the positioning early in the introduction to hook readers who manage dashboards at scale, highlighted the feature again while walking through how the product works, and closed by reiterating pain points and positioning with a call to action.

> "we made this the feature of our piece"

**Evidence:** The TapClicks post built this way holds position 2 for 'paid search dashboard'.

**How to do it**

1. Run customer research to find the one thing your product does that competitors do not.
2. State that differentiator in a single concrete sentence, with the competitor behavior named.
3. Put it in the article title alongside the target keyword.
4. Open the introduction with the pain point that drives the search, not with a definition.
5. State the positioning in the introduction so the right reader self-identifies immediately.
6. Show the differentiator again in the walkthrough section where the reader would use it.
7. Close by restating the pain point and positioning, then the call to action.

**Pitfall:** Naming a differentiator that is really table stakes. If competitors' pages say the same thing, the reader learns nothing and the angle collapses into another me-too post.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's differentiator claims must be specific and checkable, for example that every subscription includes every feature, and should appear in the title, introduction, walkthrough and conclusion of comparison and bottom-funnel pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Find the one thing your product does that rivals do not, and repeat it in the title, the introduction, the walkthrough and the conclusion.

### 9. Mass AI listicles ('Mount AI') crash traffic below baseline  `46.7`
*core · content insights · source 46*

Edward describes a trend he calls 'Mount AI': companies publishing hundreds or thousands of AI-generated 'best X for Y' listicles purely to game LLM and AI-answer visibility. This works for a short window, then either the specific subfolder or the entire site gets hit with a ranking drop, and traffic ends up lower than before the listicles were published, because in his view the pattern of mass, low-quality, AI-generated content is detectable by Google as a distinct signature. Travis connects this to the finance saying 'pigs get fat, hogs get slaughtered': smart, patient investment in brand, content, and PR compounds, while corner-cutting for a quick AI-visibility win gets punished.

> "these articles are AI-generated — a lot of them are garbage"

**Evidence:** Observed pattern per Edward: sites publishing hundreds or thousands of mass AI-generated 'best X for Y' listicles see a short-term bump, then a subfolder- or site-wide ranking drop that leaves them with less organic traffic than before they started, attributed to Google detecting the uniform, low-quality pattern.

**Apply:** Don't scale programmatic or AI listicle production purely for LLM-visibility gaming — cap volume, keep each page genuinely differentiated and human-reviewed, and treat any format that looks templated at scale as a detectable risk pattern rather than a shortcut.

### 10. Match a category keyword with a ranked list, not a background guide  `92.10`
*core · content insights · source 92*

Grow and Convert treat website translation plugin as a prototypical bottom-of-funnel term, in what their Pain Point SEO framework calls the category keyword bucket. The searcher is actively looking for products. They note Google is favoring lists of plugins over how-to posts and guides for this term, and the reason is behavioral: searchers do not want a belabored background discussion on why translation is important, they want a list and they want to work out which option best solves their problems. That decision cascades into every writing choice. It rules out the explainer intro, it makes the evaluation criteria section the most valuable part of the page, and it means each product entry has to be judged against pain points rather than described. Grow and Convert's client article ranks second for a keyword Ahrefs rates moderately high difficulty on this format.

> "Google is favoring ranking lists of plugins, not how-to posts"

**Evidence:** The client article using the list format ranks #2 for website translation plugin, a keyword Ahrefs rates moderately high difficulty.

**How to do it**

1. Classify the target keyword before outlining: is it a category keyword where the searcher wants a product list?
2. Open the top five results and record the format of each; if lists dominate, do not write a guide.
3. Cut any planned section explaining why the category matters or defining basic terms.
4. Replace the intro with two or three sentences that state what the list covers and how you evaluated.
5. Put the buyer criteria section immediately after the intro.
6. Structure every entry as: what it does plainly, then how it performs against each criterion.
7. Recheck the SERP format after publishing, since Google's preferred format for a term can shift.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Writing a how-to or intro guide for a category keyword loses on format alone, however good the prose. The signal is that your outline has a what-is section and every ranking result starts with a numbered list.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau category terms such as clinic management software or aesthetics booking system, the page must be a ranked list with criteria up front. Any draft that opens by explaining why practice management matters is targeting the wrong format.

**Apply anywhere:** For category terms where searchers are comparing products, the page must be a ranked list with criteria up front. Any draft that opens by explaining why the category matters is targeting the wrong format.

### 11. Match content depth to how expert your buyers already are  `169.4`
*core · best practices · source 169*

Hyam's sharpest rule separates B2C beginners from B2B experts. If you sell yoga apparel, most customers are beginners, so a freelance writer who can search their way to a yoga routines post is fine. If you sell security software to a head of IT who has been in the field for decades, a lay writer with surface knowledge cannot produce anything that impresses them. That mismatch is why, he says, company after company publishes '10 trends of 2019 in [niche]' posts that do not work. He calls the weak output mirage content, and notes the hidden cost: a marketer who commissions it spends a ton of time editing. Grow and Convert caps output at 3 posts per month precisely so each one can clear the expert bar, producing pieces on neuroplasticity treatment for concussions or a node.js backend migration.

> "the writing was mostly mirage content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert limits to 3 posts per month and has published specialist pieces on concussion neuroplasticity treatment, Cars.com's node.js backend transition, CDN-based Edge SEO and hard money lending mechanics.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the years of experience and job title of the person you want the article to impress.
2. Decide from that whether beginner-level explanation is genuinely useful or insulting to them.
3. For expert audiences, refuse any draft a writer could have produced from search results alone.
4. Source the specialist detail from a practitioner interview, internal expert or primary document before drafting.
5. Cut monthly output until each remaining piece can clear the expert bar, the way Grow and Convert caps at three.
6. Test a candidate writer on the hardest topic in your space, not an easy one.
7. Budget editing time honestly, and treat heavy editing of a draft as evidence the sourcing was wrong.
8. Kill trend-roundup formats unless you hold data nobody else has.

**Pitfall:** Judging a writing sample by topic rather than depth. Hyam looked at agency samples where the topics looked alright but the writing was mirage content, and the difference only shows once a subject expert reads it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog articles aimed at clinic owners and practitioners must carry detail a generalist writer cannot search their way to, so pull it from Pabau's own clinical and support staff. Cut monthly volume before cutting the interview step.

**Apply anywhere:** Set content depth by your reader's expertise. For expert buyers, refuse any draft that could have been written from search results, source the specialist detail from a practitioner first, and cut monthly volume until every piece clears that bar.

### 12. Mine paragraphs two and three for the sentence that should have opened  `127.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 127*

Grow and Convert give a rescue move for weak intros that costs nothing to run. In the bad weight-loss example, they point out the author already had a great option sitting at the end of his first paragraph: 'Get ready for weight loss without hunger.' They note more usable candidates in the second and third paragraphs. He could have started with any of those and the post would immediately have sounded a lot more specific and original. The mechanism is that writers bury their strongest, most specific line under warm-up copy, because the warm-up feels like the polite way in. The fix is not to write a new opener but to promote one that already exists, then delete everything above it. This is the fastest intro edit available on an existing library of pages.

> "great option at the end of his"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert identify 'Get ready for weight loss without hunger' at the end of the first paragraph of a bad intro as the line that should have opened the post.

**How to do it**

1. Take the first three paragraphs of the draft or the live page and list every sentence separately.
2. Score each sentence on whether it says something a competing article would not say.
3. Pick the highest-scoring sentence, usually the last one in the first paragraph or something in paragraph two or three.
4. Move that sentence to the very top as the new first line.
5. Delete every sentence that sat above it rather than reshuffling them lower.
6. Re-read the new opening to check the piece still makes sense without the deleted runway.
7. Repeat across your worst-converting posts, since this edit needs no new research.
8. Check that the promoted claim is actually delivered in the body, and add the proof if it is not.

**Pitfall:** Writers rewrite the intro from scratch and produce a second generic version. The better line is usually already in the draft, and demoting it into paragraph two is the actual mistake being made.

**Apply at Pabau:** David can run this as a cheap pass over existing Pabau blog articles: promote the most specific line from the first three paragraphs to the top and delete the runway above it. No new research or interviews needed.

**Apply anywhere:** Before rewriting a weak intro, list the sentences in the first three paragraphs and promote the most specific one to the top, deleting everything above it. The best opening line is usually already in the draft, just buried under warm-up copy.

### 13. Mine the interview for customer objections and answer them in-line  `126.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 126*

Grow and Convert show a detail from their Circuit piece on delivery software for small business that they argue no writer could have produced. In a section listing the features Circuit customers like most, the last sentence is an italicized note assuring readers that even though the app routes stops automatically, you can override the default routing and designate a few stops as priority to do first. They call it insanely specific, and they include it for one reason: someone on Circuit's team told them the priority stop question is a key objection or concern of customers. So the interview is not only a source of pain points and features. It is a source of the objections the sales and support teams field, and each of those objections gets pre-answered in the article body at the point the reader would raise it.

> "is a key objection/concern of customers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's Circuit piece on delivery software for small business, which ranks #1 for that term, carries the priority-stop note sourced from Circuit's own team.

**How to do it**

1. Add one question to every expert interview: what do customers push back on or worry about most in this area?
2. Ask which objection kills deals and which one merely delays them.
3. List each objection with the exact answer the internal expert gives customers.
4. Place each answer immediately after the feature or claim that triggers the objection.
5. Format the answer as a short parenthetical or italicized note rather than a new section, so it does not derail the flow.
6. Reuse the same objection list across the sales page and the FAQ block.

**Pitfall:** Collecting features but not objections. You end up with an accurate feature list that still loses the reader at the exact doubt a salesperson would have handled in one sentence.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's CS and sales teams know the standing objections: data migration from the old system, whether staff will adopt it, what happens to existing patient records. Each one belongs as a one-sentence in-line answer in the relevant article section and in the Yoast FAQ block, not as a separate objection-handling page.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask your internal expert which objections customers raise, then pre-answer each one in a single sentence placed right after the claim that triggers it. Those sentences are the details no researching writer could invent.

### 14. Mount AI pattern: fast rankings then a fast crash  `54.6`
*core · general insights · source 54*

Glenn Gabe coined 'Mount AI' to describe a recognizable pattern in scaled AI-generated content: rankings and organic traffic climb extremely fast as Google's algorithms initially pick up the volume signals, then the site gets hit and traffic/rankings crash just as fast, forming a mountain-shaped curve over time. In this case, the client's site had 'pockets' of AI-generated content mixed into otherwise human content (not 100% AI) in a YMYL niche - enough that when the sudden de-indexing email arrived, Glenn's first instinct was to suspect scaled AI content abuse as the cause. His actual investigation proved that instinct wrong: the real cause was an unrelated hacked subdomain, not the AI content at all.

> "you see tons of rankings coming in really fast"

**Evidence:** Glenn Gabe's own stated definition of Mount AI (fast ranking/traffic growth from scaled AI content, followed by a fast crash), applied to this case: the client had known pockets of AI-generated content in a YMYL niche, which made Mount AI his leading hypothesis for the sudden de-indexing - a hypothesis his own GSC investigation later disproved in favor of a hacked-subdomain explanation.

**Apply:** Watch for the Mount AI fast-rankings/fast-crash pattern as a real warning sign on any site scaling AI-assisted content, especially in sensitive niches - but also take the meta-lesson seriously: having a known risk factor present (like AI content pockets) doesn't confirm it caused a specific problem, so always investigate the actual mechanism via data rather than assuming the most obvious or feared narrative is correct.

### 15. Move the value earlier by finding the first dynamic verb  `124.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert give a repeatable mechanic for cutting fluff at sentence level, based on verbs. Their example draft sentence is: the best thing about our software feature is that it lets you create schedules for your team that can then be auto populated each week. The reviewer locates the first dynamic verb, create, and treats the phrase starting there as the value. The rewrite promotes it: with our software you can create schedules for your team that will auto-populate each week. The phrase the best thing about is removed because the value lies in the feature and its benefit, not in the writer's opinion of it. They present verb-hunting as their own preference rather than the only method, but it turns a vague instruction to cut the fat into a check anyone can run on every sentence.

> "I identified the value by focusing on the first dynamic verb"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's before-and-after scheduling feature sentence, where the rewrite drops the opinion frame and promotes the verb create to the start of the clause.

**How to do it**

1. Take any sentence that feels slow and underline the first dynamic verb in it.
2. Treat everything from that verb onward as the value the sentence carries.
3. Rewrite the sentence so the value phrase comes as early as possible.
4. Delete opinion frames such as the best thing about, what is great is, and it is worth noting that.
5. Delete the construction it lets you and address the reader directly with you can.
6. Keep the outcome clause intact, including the timing detail such as each week.
7. Run the pass across the intro first, where opinion frames cluster.
8. Reread aloud to confirm the sentence still says what the expert meant.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Rewrite each sentence so the first dynamic verb and the value it carries appear as early as possible, and delete opinion frames such as the best thing about.
```

**Pitfall:** Cutting words for their own sake and losing the qualifier that made the claim true. The rewrite must keep the mechanism and the timing, only moving them forward.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau feature sentences habitually open with opinion frames such as one of the best things about Pabau. David should run the first-dynamic-verb pass on every feature description so the sentence starts with what the practice can do.

**Apply anywhere:** Underline the first dynamic verb in a slow sentence, treat everything from there as the value, and rewrite so it comes first. Delete opinion frames like the best thing about, since the value is in the feature, not your view of it.

### 16. Name the Google research paper failure mode when auditing draft quality  `178.3`
*core · content insights · source 178*

Grow and Convert have a label for what most agencies and freelancers actually produce: Google research papers. The writer is handed a topic, Googles around it like a high school student writing a term paper, and regurgitates what everyone else already says. The output reads fine and is undifferentiated and generic, which is why it fails to convert even when it ranks. They tie it directly to a missing process: outside writers do not have product or domain expertise, so absent a mechanism for extracting it from your experts, research-paper writing is the default they fall back on. For B2B audiences of advanced industry experts, they add a sharper cost than weak conversion. Writing below the reader's level risks reputational damage and turns off potential customers, so the generic draft is not neutral, it is negative.

> "Google research papers."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say this is what they see many agencies and freelancers in the market producing, and that it is undifferentiated and generic.

**How to do it**

1. Take three recent drafts and highlight every claim that appears in the top ten results for the same query.
2. If more than half the article survives that highlighting, it is a Google research paper.
3. Look for the inverse signal: a claim only someone inside your company could make about the product or the customer.
4. Check whether the piece explains why a feature was designed a certain way or what it replaces; research papers never do.
5. Send failing drafts back with the missing internal input named, not with a line edit.
6. Fix the cause by scheduling an expert interview for that topic rather than rewriting the draft yourself.

**Pitfall:** Judging the draft on readability. Research-paper content is usually clean, well structured prose, which is exactly why it passes review and then converts nobody.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial check should ask of every article: what does this say that a competitor's blog could not have said. If nothing, the piece needs a Pabau product or customer detail before it publishes.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit drafts for claims that only your company could make. If everything in the piece is already in the top ten results, you have paid for a research paper, and the fix is an expert interview, not an edit.

### 17. Name your product as one solution inside every jobs-to-be-done post  `90.9`
*core · best practices · source 90*

Grow and Convert define jobs-to-be-done keywords as queries where the searcher has a problem your product solves without searching for the solution, such as 'how to manage sales leads' or 'how to migrate from bigcommerce to shopify'. The rule for writing them is specific: provide genuinely helpful advice on the task, and also present your product or service as a possible solution, often the best one. That combination is what keeps buying intent in a query that is not obviously bottom of funnel, and it is the only thing separating a jobs-to-be-done post from a top-of-funnel post on a similar topic. Grow and Convert also stress the article must include other valuable solutions, not only yours, so the advice stands on its own. They put these posts mid-funnel and expect them to convert below category and comparison pages but far above traffic posts.

> "they still have buying intent despite not being as bottom of funnel"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert place jobs-to-be-done posts mid-funnel and report top-of-funnel posts converting at 0.03% to 0.19% against 0.3% to 4.3% for pain-point posts.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm the query describes a task your product actually performs; drop it if the connection needs explaining twice.
2. Write the manual method for the task in full, so the article is useful to a reader who never buys anything.
3. Include at least two other legitimate ways to solve the problem, named and fairly described.
4. Add a section that positions your product as one route, stating plainly what it does that the manual method does not.
5. Place the contextual CTA next to that section rather than at the foot of the page.
6. Check the finished draft against one test: could a competitor's product be swapped in without changing the article? If yes, the product section is too generic.
7. Track the post on product conversions and compare it against the category and comparison pages, expecting a lower rate than both.

**Pitfall:** Writing the how-to and never mentioning the product turns a mid-funnel post into a traffic post that converts at top-of-funnel rates. The signal is a post ranking well for a task query with zero product signups over several months.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's how-to articles about clinic tasks should each carry a section showing how Pabau performs the task, alongside the manual method and other tools. An article on chasing no-shows that never names the reminders feature is a traffic post.

**Apply anywhere:** In every how-to post about a task your product performs, write the manual method in full, name two other legitimate solutions, then show plainly what your product does that the manual method does not.

### 18. Never let AI supply the argumentation for an article  `174.1`
*core · best practices · source 174*

Devesh Khanal of Grow and Convert draws the line at argumentation. He accepts AI for drafting help, but says that if the process of producing great content starts with getting the argument on a topic from AI, you have already lost, because you end up with the same unoriginal content as everyone else. His reasoning is mechanical: a language model predicts the most likely next word, so its arguments are by construction the most average position already on the web. That makes AI usable for grammar, structure cleanup and summarizing what you already decided, but never for deciding what the piece claims. The rule is a sequencing rule, not a ban. Form the point of view first, from a human who knows the subject, then let AI help express it.

> "getting the argumentation on a topic from AI, you've already lost"

**Evidence:** Khanal's mechanism claim that AI writers are 'biased towards saying the most average thing because that's how they're built', following his 2016 Mirage Content analysis.

**How to do it**

1. Before any drafting, write the article's central claim in one sentence yourself, without opening an AI tool.
2. Name the specific competitor approach or common belief the claim disagrees with, so the piece has something to argue against.
3. Get the supporting detail from a person with hands-on knowledge, not from a model summary of the top 10 results.
4. Only then use AI, and only for tasks downstream of the argument: tightening sentences, checking structure, generating headline options.
5. Re-read the draft and delete any paragraph whose argument you could not have written before the AI touched it.
6. Reject the draft outright if the central claim would be uncontroversial to every competitor in your space.

**Pitfall:** Teams treat 'AI plus a human editor' as safe, but editing only tightens sentences inside an argument the model chose. The signal you have hit it: nobody on the team can say what the article argues that competitors would dispute.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau articles, David should fix the argument before any AI drafting: state in one sentence what the piece claims about aesthetic or healthcare practice operations, and which common practice advice it disagrees with. Put that sentence at the top of the brief. AI can then help with phrasing, but the claim comes from someone who has watched clinics actually run.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide your article's central claim yourself before opening an AI tool, and name what common belief it contradicts. Use AI only downstream of that claim, for phrasing and structure. If nobody on your team can say what the piece argues that a competitor would dispute, the argument came from the model and the article will read like everyone else's.

### 20. Only publish lifestyle content if you can do it journalistically  `112.13`
*core · best practices · source 112*

Grow and Convert do not reject brand-building lifestyle content outright, but they set a bar for it. The companies that do it well use a high-quality journalistic approach, and their example is M.M. Lafleur's blog The M Dash, which runs exclusive interviews with women celebrities and influencers. They point out you cannot easily get an interview with Padma Lakshmi, and that this is effectively running a digital magazine attached to an ecommerce brand. The test is therefore resource-based: if you cannot fund original reporting, do not run a lifestyle blog at all.

> "you can't easily get an interview with Padma Lakshmi"

**Evidence:** M.M. Lafleur's The M Dash runs exclusive celebrity interviews such as Padma Lakshmi, which Grow and Convert describe as a digital magazine attached to an ecommerce brand.

**How to do it**

1. Before approving any lifestyle topic, ask what in it could not be produced by a freelancer with a search engine.
2. Require an original input for each piece: a named interview, proprietary data, or first-hand access.
3. Cost the piece honestly, including the time to secure that access, and compare it to a bottom-of-funnel article.
4. If you cannot fund the original input, cut the piece rather than commissioning a cheaper version of it.
5. Hold lifestyle content to a brand metric such as subscriptions or shares, never to a conversion target.
6. Keep it as a separate line in the budget so it never eats the purchase-intent queue.

**Pitfall:** Farming lifestyle topics to freelancers gets the worst of both worlds: the topics do not relate to the product so they barely convert, and the writing does not impress anyone so there is no brand gain either.

**Apply anywhere:** Lifestyle content is worth running only at a journalistic standard, meaning original interviews, proprietary data or first-hand access. If you cannot fund that, cut the piece, because a freelance version of it neither converts nor builds the brand.

### 21. Open a bottom-funnel roundup with a what-to-look-for criteria section  `92.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 92*

Grow and Convert's client article ranking second for website translation plugin opens with a section titled five things you need from your translation plugin. They argue the existence of that section is itself a signal of good writing, because a writer who does not know which pain points matter would never think to create it. The section does two jobs. It gives the searcher, who is actively comparing products, the evaluation framework they came for. And it sets up every product review that follows, so each entry is judged against criteria the reader has already accepted. In their case automation was criterion one, accuracy two, and translated SEO meta information three, all drawn from client interviews. The criteria must be correct to work; a list of made-up criteria is worse than none. When the article later introduces the client's product, it leads with the three features that answer the first three criteria.

> "we have an entire section at the beginning that outlines what to look for"

**Evidence:** The client article ranking #2 for website translation plugin (a moderately high difficulty keyword per Ahrefs) opens with a five things you need section built from client interviews.

**How to do it**

1. Take the ranked pain points from your customer-facing interviews.
2. Convert the top three to five into buyer criteria phrased as things the reader needs, not features you sell.
3. Place that section immediately after the intro, before any product review.
4. Order the criteria by how often interviews raised them, most-raised first.
5. Explain under each criterion why it matters, using the concrete scenario from the interview, such as an ecommerce site adding products daily.
6. Review every product in the list against the same criteria in the same order.
7. When you reach your own product, lead its section with the features answering criteria one, two and three.
8. Cut any criterion you cannot justify from an interview or from data.

**Pitfall:** Inventing criteria to fill the section is the failure mode. If the criteria are guessed, every product review beneath them is judged on the wrong axis, and the article is confidently wrong instead of merely thin.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau software comparison should open with a criteria section drawn from what practice owners actually evaluate, then review each system against those criteria in the same order. Pabau's own section then leads with the features answering criteria one to three.

**Apply anywhere:** Open every software comparison with a criteria section drawn from what buyers actually evaluate, then review each product against those criteria in the same order. Your own product's section leads with the features answering the top criteria.

### 22. Open an entity description with a semantic triple and four answers  `73.7`
*core · best practices · source 73*

Asked how a Miami electrician should describe itself, Barnard walks through the sentence pattern he uses. Start with a semantic triple - subject, verb, object - naming the brand: 'Palm Wires is an electrical services company in Miami.' Because the brand name does not say what the business does, the sentence has to say it. Then narrow the audience, because 'electrical services' could mean industrial scale: 'serving households.' Then add history and a reason to trust: 'since 2004.' He compresses the whole rule to four questions he says he repeats constantly - who you are, what you do, who you serve, and why should they trust you. Get those into the executive summary and you are off to the races. The phrasing must carry zero ambiguity when read plainly, and the same text goes onto every platform.

> "who you are, what you do, who you serve, why should they trust you"

**Evidence:** Barnard built the example live in the interview, correcting the brand name to Palm Wires and reading the triple back as subject verb object.

**How to do it**

1. Write sentence one as a bare subject-verb-object triple: brand name, is, category noun.
2. Add the location or market to that first sentence if the business is geographically bound.
3. Write the audience explicitly in the next clause, narrowing the category (households, not just electrical services).
4. Add a founding year or a duration to give the reader and the machine a trust anchor.
5. Check the paragraph answers all four questions: who you are, what you do, who you serve, why trust you.
6. Read the paragraph back and remove any sentence a stranger could read two ways.
7. Use the brand name as the sentence subject rather than a pronoun each time the facts change.
8. Copy the finished paragraph to every platform description unchanged.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Palm Wires is an electrical services company in Miami serving households since 2004.
```

**Pitfall:** Starting the description with positioning or a value proposition. The machine gets no subject-verb-object anchor, and the category it files you under comes from whatever noun it can find instead.

**Apply at Pabau:** Rewrite Pabau's boilerplate first sentence as a plain triple: Pabau is practice management software for aesthetic and healthcare practices, then the audience, then the founding year. Use it identically in every directory and guest-post bio.

**Apply anywhere:** Open every entity description with a plain subject-verb-object sentence naming the brand and its category, then state the audience, then give a founding year. Answer who you are, what you do, who you serve, and why to trust you.

### 23. Open on the reader's real operational problem, found by user research  `127.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 127*

Grow and Convert name the winning CRM intro's method as the Specificity Strategy, the same strategy they use for blog posts in general. What it does concretely: it gets to the heart of a real, specific problem that real sales managers face in real life, namely that their reps are not using the CRM. They call that an actual problem sales managers have, and they add in a parenthesis that you discover these problems with extensive user research. Their claim is that the intro then hooks the target customer, who is nodding along as they read on. The rule they state flatly is that specific blog content is good blog content. The operational consequence is that a good intro is downstream of research, not of writing skill, so a team without customer interviews cannot write these openers no matter how good the writer is.

> "Specific blog content is good blog content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's winning CRM intro opens on reps not using the CRM, which they attribute to extensive user research rather than writing craft.

**How to do it**

1. Run customer or prospect interviews and log the operational failures they describe in their own words.
2. Keep a running list of these failures phrased as specific behaviors, for example 'my reps are not using the CRM', not 'poor adoption'.
3. Map each planned article to one item on that list before outlining it.
4. Open the article by naming that behavior in the first sentence or two.
5. Check the named problem is one the reader would recognize as happening in their own week, not an industry trend.
6. If no interview line matches the article's topic, treat that as a signal the topic may not be worth writing.
7. Refresh the list every quarter, since the operational failures shift with tooling.
8. Reuse the same phrasing in the title and meta so the SERP snippet matches the intro.

**Pitfall:** Without customer interviews, writers substitute an invented pain that sounds plausible but no reader recognizes, which reads exactly like the generic opener it was meant to replace. The tell is a pain stated as a trend rather than as a behavior.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's intros should open on a logged operational failure from real practices, for example front desk staff still writing consultations on paper after the software went live. That requires a maintained list of customer-interview lines David can pull from per article.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep a maintained list of operational failures customers describe in their own words, taken from interviews, and open each article on the one it addresses. A specific behavior the reader recognizes from their own week hooks them; an invented industry-level pain does not.
