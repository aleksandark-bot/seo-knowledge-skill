### 1. Report AI visibility as a count of topics by band, not one percentage  `85.2`
*core · best practices · source 85*

Grow and Convert's answer to the diluted percentage problem is to change what the brand-level number actually is. Instead of collapsing every prompt into one share of voice, report the number of topics sitting in low, medium and high visibility bands. That metric does not fall when you add topics, because a new topic increases the low band count rather than dragging an average down. It also stays legible to an executive: three high, four medium, nine low is a portfolio you can fund. They built this into Traqer, so treat it as a disclosed vendor position, but the metric is reproducible in a spreadsheet with any tool that exports per-prompt results.

> "those metrics show the total number of topics"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report this structure to clients and say adding new topics does not artificially reduce performance elsewhere.

**How to do it**

1. Define the visibility bands up front, for example low under 20%, medium 20 to 50%, high above 50% of prompts in the topic basket mentioning the brand.
2. Export per-prompt mention results from your tracking tool and group them by the topic each prompt belongs to.
3. Calculate each topic's own percentage, then assign it a band.
4. Report the three band counts as the headline brand-level number, with the topic names listed under each band.
5. Track band movement month to month: which topics crossed from low to medium is the story, not the aggregate.
6. Add new topics freely, and show them entering the low band as intended new work, not as a decline.

**Tools:** Traqer, Peec, Profound

**Pitfall:** Bands hide magnitude inside a band, so a topic sliding from 49% to 21% looks unchanged. Keep the raw per-topic percentage visible under the band label.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's monthly AI visibility report should lead with a band count across the topic map, for example clinic scheduling software, aesthetic practice EMR, patient booking, med spa CRM. That reframes a new topic Pabau starts tracking as a funded gap, not a regression.

**Apply anywhere:** Lead your monthly AI visibility report with a count of topics in low, medium and high bands across your topic map. That reframes a newly tracked topic as a funded gap rather than a regression.

### 2. Restrict AI visibility work to bottom-of-funnel buying prompts  `87.16`
*core · best practices · source 87*

Grow and Convert state their standing position up front in the methodology: bottom-of-funnel, product-centric prompts are the only ones worth worrying about for AI search, because top-of-funnel queries are typically answered by LLMs without mentioning or linking to any brand. Every one of their 100 test prompts had buying intent, with examples like best help desk software for small teams, top accounts payable automation tools, and best standing desk for home office. The study only measures behavior in that band, and their recommendations only claim to apply there. This matches the base's existing line that top-of-funnel material is what AI extraction takes first, and it sharpens it into a selection rule for which prompts to track and which pages to build.

> "bottom-of-funnel, product-centric prompts are the only ones"

**Evidence:** All 100 tested prompts were buying-intent across B2B, B2C, software, services and physical products.

**How to do it**

1. List the prompts a buyer would type when they are choosing a vendor, not when they are learning the topic.
2. Keep only prompts of the form best X for Y, top X tools, X alternatives, or X pricing.
3. Drop informational prompts from tracking; they return answers with no brand links.
4. Build or refresh the pages that answer those buying prompts: comparisons, listicles, pricing, product pages.
5. Publish genuinely top-of-funnel material somewhere other than the money blog if you still want it.
6. Review the prompt list quarterly as the category's vocabulary shifts.

**Pitfall:** Tracking informational prompts and concluding AI visibility is poor. Those answers rarely link to any brand, so there was nothing to win.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's AI tracking basket should be buying prompts about aesthetic and healthcare practice software, not prompts about how to run a clinic.

**Apply anywhere:** Track only buying-intent prompts for AI visibility. Informational prompts get answered without brand links, so there is nothing there to win.

### 3. Run citation outreach at the exact articles AI tools quote for your queries  `121.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 121*

Grow and Convert sell citation outreach as an extension of link building rather than a separate program. The method is to identify which articles and sources appear most frequently when AI tools answer questions relevant to the product, then approach those sources to get the product mentioned or included with accurate feature and use-case information. Their example is a specific 'best project management software' article that ChatGPT and Perplexity consistently cite for recommendation queries. Getting added to that one article directly changes how often the tools recommend you. They describe it as the same concept as listicle inclusion for SEO, with the extra benefit of shaping AI recommendations, and they run the source analysis through their own tool, traqer.ai. Their standing advice is that citation outreach amplifies pain-point SEO results but does not replace ranking in normal search.

> "identifying which articles and sources appear most frequently"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run this as a paid client service and analyze the most commonly cited domains, articles and brands for terms like 'best project management software' using their AI visibility tool traqer.ai.

**How to do it**

1. Write the list of product-recommendation prompts a buyer would actually type, one per bottom-funnel keyword you target.
2. Run each prompt across ChatGPT and Perplexity and log every domain and URL cited.
3. Rank the cited URLs by how often they appear across the prompt set rather than judging any single answer.
4. Filter to the listicles and roundups you could plausibly be added to, and check whether you are already listed.
5. Contact the author or site owner with the specific features, pricing and use cases they should list, written so it can be pasted in.
6. Offer an affiliate arrangement where the site monetizes that way, since it gives them a reason to add you and keep the entry current.
7. Re-run the same prompt set monthly and track the change in mention frequency, not a single-prompt check.
8. Keep ranking the underlying page in normal search, since citation outreach amplifies that rather than substituting for it.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Perplexity, traqer.ai

**Pitfall:** Checking one prompt once and declaring a result. Citations are sampled, so a basket of prompts tracked over time is the only reading that holds, and outreach with no supplied copy usually gets ignored.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build a prompt basket around practice management software recommendations for aesthetic and healthcare clinics, log which roundups get cited, and pitch inclusion with ready-to-paste feature and pricing copy.

**Apply anywhere:** Find the specific articles AI tools cite when they answer buying questions in your category, then get your product added to those articles with accurate details. Treat it as listicle outreach that also moves AI recommendations.

### 4. Run the four-step Prioritized GEO engagement in this exact order  `79.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 79*

Grow & Convert publish the four steps they run in every client engagement, in priority order. Step one is topic ideation with their Pain Point SEO framework: list the bottom-of-funnel topics an ideal customer searches when shopping for a solution, then find traditional SEO keywords that align with each. Step two is producing high-quality owned content that ranks for those keywords. Step three, optional, is link building plus citation outreach to the domains their tool shows are cited for the topic. Step four, also optional, is on-site tactics such as key takeaways and FAQs. Their stated rule is that no GEO tactic should get in the way of content that impresses real customers and converts. They give the example of client InnovationCast, whose foundational topic was 'innovation management software'.

> "Using our Pain Point SEO framework, we identify bottom-of-funnel topics"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert's client engagements; InnovationCast ranks in top spots for a spread of innovation management software keywords and appears in Traqer visibility for related prompts.

**How to do it**

1. List the bottom-of-funnel topics an ideal customer would search when shopping for your category, not topics about the problem in general.
2. Map each topic to traditional SEO keywords humans and LLMs would both use, for example 'innovation management software' and its variants.
3. Write the foundational article for the head term first, in-house or via interviews, not AI-generated or freelance-written.
4. Publish and wait for it to rank in Google organic, since that is the surface LLMs search.
5. Run traditional link building to contextually relevant domains for that article.
6. Pull the domains most cited for prompts on that topic and pitch them to include you.
7. Only then add key takeaways, FAQs or question headings, and only if they do not degrade the page.
8. Track visibility with several prompt phrasings per topic rather than one.

**Tools:** Traqer.ai

**Pitfall:** Teams invert the order and spend the quarter on llms.txt and FAQ formatting while the foundational bottom-of-funnel article does not exist. The signal is on-site work shipped with no ranking page behind it.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should order Pabau's GEO work the same way: first a genuinely good page for each bottom-of-funnel term such as clinic management software or aesthetic practice software, then links and trade-site citations, then any block-level AI formatting. Pabau's existing block contract already covers step four, so it should never be the bottleneck.

**Apply anywhere:** Order your GEO work the same way: first a genuinely good page for each bottom-of-funnel commercial term in your category, then links and trade-site citations, then any AI-friendly formatting. Formatting is the last 5%, not the plan.

### 5. Run the null test: check LLM visibility for keywords you do not rank for  `82.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 82*

Grow and Convert's Level AI case ends on a negative control that is more useful than the wins. Across four example keywords where they rank on page one, and specifically at position one for two of them, Level AI shows strong LLM visibility for related prompts. For keywords they have not yet earned rankings on, there is little to no visibility in LLMs. They use this pairing as the proof that Google ranking is the mechanism, not a correlation. The test is cheap to run on your own site and settles internal arguments about whether GEO needs a separate content type. Build the comparison deliberately: sample ranked and unranked target keywords from the same category, run matched prompt baskets for both, and show the gap.

> "Keywords we aren't yet ranking for have little to no visibility in LLMs."

**Evidence:** Level AI: four keywords ranking page one or #1 all showed LLM visibility for related prompts; keywords not yet ranking showed little to none.

**How to do it**

1. List ten target keywords where you rank in the top five on Google and ten in the same category where you rank below position 20 or not at all.
2. For each keyword write five buyer-phrased prompts, keeping the phrasing style identical across the ranked and unranked sets.
3. Run every prompt on ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews, logging brand mention and citation separately.
4. Score each keyword as the share of its five prompts that mention your brand.
5. Compare the mean score of the ranked set against the unranked set and record the gap.
6. Repeat the unranked prompts a month after any of those keywords reaches page one to confirm the visibility follows the ranking.
7. Use the gap to set the program's priority: fix rankings first, and only then chase off-site mentions.

**Tools:** Traqer, Ahrefs, ChatGPT, Perplexity

**Pitfall:** If you sample unranked keywords from a different topic, the comparison fails because brand familiarity, not ranking, explains the difference. Keep both sets inside one product category.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can run this in a week over practice management keywords. Pick ranked pages and unranked ones in the same aesthetic and healthcare category, then use the gap to justify spending on rankings rather than on schema and llms.txt tweaks.

**Apply anywhere:** Sample ranked and unranked keywords from the same category, run matched prompt baskets on both, and measure the visibility gap. Use it to decide whether ranking work or off-site work is your bottleneck.

### 6. Sample ChatGPT several times per query because it swaps one to three tools each ask  `89.3`
*core · best practices · source 89*

Grow and Convert note in their method that ChatGPT does not return the same list twice, even for an identical question. Each re-ask swaps in one to three new tools. For the study they simply used the first answer to keep the method simple, which they flag as a limitation. Anyone measuring their own visibility should not do that. A single answer tells you almost nothing about whether you are reliably in the recommendation set or occasionally lucky. Ask the same question five to ten times in fresh sessions and record presence as a rate, not a yes or no. This matches the base's wider point that AI answers are token-sampled probability rather than a position you occupy.

> "we just used the first answer from ChatGPT"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert observed one to three tools swapping in on every repeat ask of the same question, and used only the first answer as an acknowledged simplification.

**How to do it**

1. Write the category question in the natural language a buyer would use, not keyword form.
2. Open a fresh chat with memory and custom instructions off for each run so history cannot bias the answer.
3. Ask the same question ten times across ten separate sessions.
4. Record every product named in each run in a spreadsheet, one column per run.
5. Calculate each brand's appearance rate out of ten, and log your own.
6. Treat anything under 30 percent as unstable presence and anything over 70 percent as reliable.
7. Repeat monthly and track the rate over time rather than any single answer.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What are the best time clock software options?
```

**Pitfall:** Taking one screenshot of a good answer and reporting it as visibility. The next run may drop you entirely, and Grow and Convert say one to three names change every single time.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any Pabau AI-visibility report should quote an appearance rate over at least ten runs per prompt, never a single screenshot. Build the prompt basket around practice-management and clinic-software questions.

**Apply anywhere:** Any AI-visibility report should quote an appearance rate over at least ten runs per prompt, never a single screenshot. Build a prompt basket around your category's buying questions.

### 7. Score your category with a Page One Popularity count before chasing AI mentions  `89.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 89*

Grow and Convert built a metric they call the Page One Popularity number. For each category query they opened every result on Google's first page, recorded every product named inside those listicles, and counted how many times each product appeared across the whole page. A typical query produced around 100 product mentions from 8 to 10 results. They then compared that count against the 7 to 8 products ChatGPT named for the same question. The count tells you two things at once: which products the web consensus actually treats as leaders, and how far your brand sits from that consensus. It is a cheap manual audit that gives you a ranked target list of the products you need to be mentioned alongside, and a baseline you can re-measure later.

> "we counted how many times each product mentioned"

**Evidence:** Across six B2B SaaS queries, Google page one yielded roughly 100 product mentions per query; 'best heatmapping tools' returned 117 mentions covering 42 unique tools.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the bottom-funnel category query your buyers actually use, such as 'best CRM' or 'best time clock software'.
2. Open every organic result on Google page one, usually 8 to 10 listicles and review sites.
3. Copy every product name mentioned inside each result into one spreadsheet column, including obscure ones.
4. Count how many separate page-one results mention each product; that count is its Page One Popularity number.
5. Sort descending and colour-format the column so the leaders and the long tail are visible at a glance.
6. Ask ChatGPT the same question in natural language and record the 7 to 8 products it names.
7. Look up your own Page One Popularity number and compare it to the lowest number ChatGPT still named.
8. Re-run the whole count every quarter to see whether your mention count is moving.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Google Sheets

**Pitfall:** Counting only the products in the top three results understates the long tail and inflates your relative position. Grow and Convert found page-one listicles are stuffed with obscure products, and it is that tail that makes the average look low.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this count for the queries that matter to Pabau, such as 'best practice management software' and 'best clinic software', listing every product named across page one. That gives Pabau a real mention-count baseline and names the third-party listicles Pabau must appear in.

**Apply anywhere:** Run this count for your own category queries, listing every product named across page one. It gives you a real mention-count baseline and names the third-party listicles you need to appear in.

### 8. Score your passages against competitors' for synthetic queries  `20.9`
*core · ai workflows · source 20*

This is a competitive-gap diagnostic workflow: start from real prompts, map them to the synthetic sub-queries inside the query fan-out (using the open-source tool Qforia, built by the speaker, plus commercial monitoring like Profound), then identify which pages rank for those synthetic queries across Google, Bing, and Brave (Claude's exclusive search source). From there, extract the most relevant passage on your page for each synthetic query and score it against the equivalent passage on competitor pages — the worked example shows a page scoring 0.6 against a competitor's 0.8 across ten synthetic queries, explaining a visibility gap directly at the passage level rather than the whole-page level.

> "you have a passage that's a 0.6 for this synthetic query"

**How to do it**

1. Identify the real prompts your target audience is likely to ask, grounded in actual audience research rather than guessed keywords.
2. Run each prompt through a query fan-out research tool — Qforia (free, open-source, built by the speaker) is one option — to generate the underlying synthetic sub-queries.
3. Supplement with a commercial AI-visibility monitoring tool (e.g., Profound) that independently collects fan-out and citation data from repeated prompt analysis over time.
4. Consolidate the fan-out data down to the top five synthetic queries per prompt/topic you're prioritizing.
5. Check rankings for those synthetic queries on Google, Bing, and Brave (Brave specifically because Claude uses it exclusively as its search source).
6. For each synthetic query, identify which pages currently rank and extract the single most relevant passage from your page and from top competitor pages.
7. Score each extracted passage's relevance to its synthetic query on a 0-1 scale (e.g., 0.6 vs. a competitor's 0.8).
8. Where your passage score trails a competitor's across multiple synthetic queries, or you have no ranking passage at all, treat that as your specific, addressable visibility gap.
9. Rewrite or add passages specifically targeting the queries where you scored lowest or were entirely absent, then re-run the scoring check to confirm improvement. (inferred verification loop)

**Tools:** Qforia, Profound

### 9. Seed your brand name next to your category words, not just your brand name  `89.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 89*

Grow and Convert make a precise point that generic mention-building advice misses. Because it is a language model, what matters is not how often your name appears anywhere but how often it appears in the context of the category words you want to be recommended for, their examples being 'CRM' or 'small business accounting tools'. A thousand mentions of your brand in funding news does little for 'best CRM'. A handful of mentions where your name sits inside a sentence about CRM software builds the association the model reproduces. That turns mention building into a targeting exercise: pick the exact category phrase, then make sure every placement you earn puts your name and that phrase in the same passage.

> "the more your product is mentioned in the context of the category-related words"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert argue the language-model mechanism makes category-adjacent mentions the operative variable, citing 'CRM' and 'small business accounting tools' as the association targets.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the two or three exact category phrases buyers use, for example 'practice management software'.
2. Search each phrase and list the sites already writing about the category, not just high-authority sites.
3. For every placement you pitch, supply a one-sentence product description that contains your brand name and the category phrase in the same sentence.
4. Refuse or rewrite placements that mention your brand without the category phrase attached.
5. Audit existing coverage for brand-only mentions and ask publishers to add the category descriptor.
6. Repeat the same wording across your own About page, directory profiles and press releases so the association is consistent.
7. Re-run your ChatGPT sampling after each wave of placements to see if the appearance rate moves.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Chasing high-authority coverage that never names your category. A national business feature that calls you 'a software startup' teaches the model nothing about which category to recommend you in.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau mention Pabau places or earns should carry the phrase 'practice management software' next to the name, exactly as the house rule already requires on first mention. Apply the same rule to directory profiles and PR boilerplate.

**Apply anywhere:** Every mention you place or earn should carry your category phrase next to your brand name. Apply the same rule to directory profiles and PR boilerplate.

### 11. Self-promotional 'best X for Y' listicles need guardrails  `37.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 37*

Jotform runs a number of self-promotional 'best X for Y' listicles where its own product ranks #1 (visible via a site:jotform.com "best" search), and these get picked up in AI answer engines - Jotform shows up in ChatGPT's answer for 'best affiliate programs for beginners without a website' via exactly this kind of listicle. The tactic is described as currently risky but workable if kept balanced: Jotform's versions are readable, not dominated by huge AI-generated block paragraphs, cover competitors fairly, include a TL;DR at the top, and are confined to a handful of pages within their blog rather than mass-produced across important SEO subfolders.

> "it's best to do it in subfolders that don't have"

**How to do it**

1. If publishing self-promotional 'best X for Y' listicles featuring your own product prominently, limit it to a handful of pages rather than mass-producing them.
2. Place these listicles in a subfolder that doesn't also hold your other important, already-ranking SEO assets, so any de-indexing risk is contained.
3. Keep the writing genuinely balanced and readable - avoid huge AI-generated block paragraphs and avoid making most of the piece read as a straight advertisement for your own product.
4. Add a clear TL;DR summary at the top giving the direct answer immediately.
5. Cover competitors fairly within the listicle rather than dismissing them, so it reads as a genuine comparison rather than pure self-promotion.
6. Monitor these pages' indexing status and traffic specifically, since this is described as a currently risky, unresolved tactic.
7. Benchmark your approach against known collapse cases (e.g., ClickUp's fall from 1.19 million to 28,000 monthly traffic after spamming unbalanced, heavily AI-written self-promotional listicles) as a cautionary reference.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude

**Pitfall:** Self-promotional listicles spammed out without much thought - heavy AI-generated content, unbalanced coverage that reads as a straight advertisement - have caused some companies major traffic increases followed by rapid decreases they struggled to recover from (the video cites ClickUp's collapse from 1.19 million to 28,000 as the cautionary example); doing this at scale, or in a subfolder that also holds important SEO assets, risks that entire subfolder being de-indexed.

### 12. Skip llms.txt and heading rewrites, fix content specificity instead  `78.10`
*core · best practices · source 78*

Grow and Convert state plainly that Constitution Lending's AI visibility did not come from an llms.txt file or from restructured headings, calling those on-site hacks that do not help LLMs connect a brand to a user's pain points. What this source adds is the reason and the substitute: the model has to be able to match your published detail to a user's stated situation, and no markup change creates that detail. > "This didn't happen because of an llms.txt file"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert explicitly attribute Constitution Lending's results to detailed specific content, not to llms.txt or restructured headings.

**How to do it**

3. Redirect that budget into customer-facing interviews and scenario content production.
4. Before shipping any on-site GEO change, state which user pain point it helps the model match, and drop it if there is no answer.
5. Measure the outcome at topic-basket level so you can tell whether markup or content moved the number.
6. Keep normal technical SEO health separate from the GEO program so the two budgets are not confused.

**Pitfall:** Selling an llms.txt rollout as a GEO strategy. It is cheap and visible, so it gets approved, and it consumes the quarter without producing the specific content the models actually need.

> "don't prioritize top-of-funnel content for GEO"

**Pitfall:** Traffic dashboards still reward top-of-funnel volume, so teams keep publishing it and read flat AI referral numbers as a GEO measurement problem rather than a topic-selection problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog carries a lot of top-of-funnel explainer content that AI answers absorb. David should stop commissioning new ones and shift the budget to product-comparison and 'best software for X practice' pages, where being named in the answer is the win.

### 14. Sort every AI SEO tactic into the three-tier GEO priorities pyramid  `86.2`
*core · best practices · source 86*

Grow & Convert built a framework they call Prioritized GEO to stop teams from working a random tactic list. Every AI SEO tactic they have seen fits one of three tiers. Tier 1 is owned content you publish on your own site that ranks in traditional search. Tier 2 is off-site brand mentions: digital PR, citation outreach, guest posts, links. Tier 3 is on-site tactics that reformat your content for machines: llms.txt, question-shaped headings, FAQ sections, key takeaways blocks, AI-targeted schema. Tiers 1 and 2 work by the same mechanism, exposing your brand to the LLM when it searches the web to ground an answer. Tier 3 works by a different mechanism, helping an LLM digest content it has already found. Their argument is that digestion is worthless if the content is never retrieved, so Tier 3 is last, even though most published advice sits there because it is new and easy to sell.

> "Every AI SEO tactic we've seen fits into one of these three tiers"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert developed the framework from a year of testing across their client base and named it the GEO Priorities Pyramid, with owned content at the base and on-site tactics at the apex.

**How to do it**

1. List every AI SEO tactic currently on your backlog or in an agency proposal.
2. Label each one Tier 1 (owned content that ranks), Tier 2 (off-site mention) or Tier 3 (on-site formatting for machines).
3. Ask of each tactic: does it expose my brand to an LLM performing a web search, or only help it digest content it already has? Digestion-only means Tier 3.
4. Freeze all Tier 3 work until you have bottom-of-funnel content ranking on page one for your target commercial keywords.
5. Run Tier 1 and Tier 2 in parallel, with Tier 1 taking the larger share because you control the wording.
6. Only after Tiers 1 and 2 are running, test one Tier 3 tactic at a time and measure it against a control set of pages.

**Pitfall:** The pyramid gets inverted in practice because Tier 3 is cheap, fast and demo-able, so it fills retainers and sprint boards while the ranking work stalls. The signal is a completed AI-optimization checklist with no movement in AI mentions.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should audit Pabau's AI SEO backlog against these three tiers and park the on-site items. Ranking the comparison and alternatives pages, plus getting Pabau named on aesthetic-industry sites, outranks adding schema or llms.txt.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your AI SEO backlog against the three tiers and park the on-site formatting items. Ranking your commercial pages and getting named on industry sites outranks adding schema or an llms.txt file.

### 15. Start AEO at the bottom of the funnel, not the top  `76.3`
*core · best practices · source 76*

The paper calls this the most counterintuitive finding in AEO. Traditional SEO rewarded top-of-funnel investment, broad keyword pages built to capture awareness and volume. AEO inverts that, and the structural reason is query decomposition. When a user asks an agent a highly specific question, the engine breaks it into sub-queries targeting each attribute of the request. A brand that has published content addressing exactly that intersection of attributes dominates retrieval for the query regardless of its overall domain authority, because in the vector space of AI retrieval brands compete on proximity to the specific query embedding, not on aggregate reputation. The authors link this to Mike King's 'relevance engineering' and argue it structurally favors small specialists: a niche brand producing data-rich content for a narrow domain achieves higher vector-space proximity than a generalist whose content is broad but shallow.

> "brands should begin optimization at the bottom of the funnel rather than the top"

**Evidence:** The authors frame this as a structural advantage for smaller, specialized players and attribute the mechanism to Mike King of iPullRank's 'relevance engineering'.

**How to do it**

1. Write out the specific compound questions a buyer asks an agent, with all their qualifiers intact, not the head keyword.
2. Split each one into its attribute sub-queries, the way the engine's fan-out would.
3. Find the attribute intersections nobody has a dedicated passage for.
4. Build a page or section per intersection instead of one broad guide covering the topic.
5. Load each with the concrete specifics that only a practitioner in that niche would have.
6. Leave the broad awareness keywords to last: they are where large incumbents already have shallow coverage and where AI answers finish the job without a click.

**Pitfall:** Large sites assume their legacy SEO library covers this. The paper's warning is that libraries optimized for high-volume keywords produce few passages that survive chunking and pairwise comparison, because they cover topics without addressing precise attribute intersections.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build pages at the attribute intersections aesthetic clinic owners actually ask about, such as booking software for a two-room medspa with injectables consent forms, rather than another broad practice-management guide.

**Apply anywhere:** Build pages at the attribute intersections your buyers actually ask about, rather than another broad category guide.

### 16. Swap review platforms to flip AI Overview sentiment  `40.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 40*

When a brand's primary review platform gets overwhelmed with negative reviews, Kasra's fix is to stop sending new review-request traffic there and redirect it to a fresh, undamaged platform instead, since the platform actively receiving the most current review volume is the one that ends up ranking and being summarized in the AI Overview. His illustrating case: a friend's brand got hammered on Trustpilot, and a Google AI Overview literally warned searchers 'don't go with this brand' when they searched the brand plus 'reviews,' which nearly tripled the brand's cost-per-lead from £126 to £442 because it also hit paid ads. The fix was moving the brand's review-solicitation traffic from Trustpilot to Feefo, which changed what the AI Overview said about the brand. Kasra layers on an exact-match domain, such as '[Brand] Reviews,' or an owned '/reviews' page, populated with curated video, image, and text reviews, as additional consensus-building assets that also rank in their own right.

> "all you need to do is send more traffic somewhere else"

**How to do it**

1. Audit your current primary review platform: pull the total review count, average star rating, and percentage of negative (1-2 star) reviews.
2. Identify a fresh alternative review platform that is not already 'burnt' with historical negative reviews, e.g. Reviews.io or Feefo in the UK, or an equivalent competitor to your current platform.
3. Set up a business profile on the new platform.
4. Change your post-service review-request email or workflow so it links to the NEW platform instead of the damaged one, immediately stopping new traffic to the old platform.
5. Optionally register an exact-match domain such as yourbrand plus 'reviews' (e.g. edwardsturmreviews.com), or build a /reviews subpage on your existing domain.
6. Populate the owned reviews asset by curating your best reviews in three formats: video, image, and text.
7. Link to the new review platform and/or your owned reviews page from your main website and from the review-request emails so both customers and search engines discover the new consensus source.
8. If you use 'sameAs' schema pointing at your old review platform, update it to point at the new one, since this appears to help Google reassess and re-rank the new platform faster.
9. Continue slowly rebuilding the damaged platform's rating in parallel as a long-term fix while the new platform absorbs review volume and AI Overview visibility short-term.
10. Re-check the brand plus 'reviews' query in Google AI Overview and ChatGPT every few weeks to confirm the sentiment characterization has shifted toward the new platform.

**Pitfall:** This only works because you control where you send post-service customers to leave reviews - if you keep directing traffic to the damaged platform out of habit, it will keep dominating regardless of how good your new platform looks, since the swap requires actually redirecting the traffic source, not just creating a new profile.

### 17. Swap superlative claims for number-based factual statements  `69.3`
*core · best practices · source 69*

Smarty draws a direct contrast: LLMs may ignore bold marketing statements such as 'We are the best in XX' but will likely pick up on factual, number-based messages, her example being 'trusted by XXX consumers since XXXX'. The mechanism is that a superlative has no external corroboration, so a model that answers from consensus has nothing to match it against. A number attached to a date is a specific claim that can appear in the same form in a press mention, a directory listing or a review profile, and that repetition is what makes it quotable. This applies to the About page first but generalizes to every page where you make a trust claim.

> "will likely pick up on factual, number-based messages"

**Evidence:** Smarty's stated contrast between 'We are the best in XX' being ignored and 'trusted by XXX consumers for since XXXX' being picked up.

**How to do it**

1. List every claim on the About page and mark each as superlative or factual.
2. Delete or rewrite each superlative; a claim survives only if a number or date can be attached.
3. Convert claims to the pattern 'trusted by N customers since YEAR' or 'N practices in M countries'.
4. Attach a source or an outbound link to each number where one exists.
5. Repeat the same figures verbatim in press materials, directory listings and review profiles so the claim corroborates itself off-site.
6. Set a review date on every figure so an outdated number never sits on the page.

**Pitfall:** Rounding or restating the same figure differently in different places. Two conflicting customer counts across your own properties make the claim less likely to be repeated, not more.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every superlative on pabau.com should be replaced by a dated figure: the number of practices using Pabau, the year founded, the countries served. Then the same figures go into the directory listings and press boilerplate unchanged.

**Apply anywhere:** Replace superlatives with dated, numeric claims, then repeat those exact figures across your off-site profiles so the claim is corroborated wherever a model looks.

### 18. Target every query fan-out branch, not just one  `40.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 40*

Kasra's model for winning an AI product recommendation is to reverse-engineer the 'query fan-out': when a user asks an LLM a compound question, the system breaks it into several narrower sub-queries and pulls candidate answers for each, and only the entities appearing across multiple sub-queries make it into the final synthesized recommendation. His example: a $600 laptop pitched at students for video editing would get fanned out into sub-queries like 'best laptops for students,' 'best laptops under $700,' and 'best laptops for video editing' - and whichever product shows up in all three is the one likely to get cited in the final answer. The practical task is mapping out the likely fan-out branches for your own product and making sure you have dedicated content ranking for each individual branch, not just the head query.

> "The laptop that shows up in all three fan-out queries"

**How to do it**

1. Pick a specific product, plan, or feature to optimize for AI recommendation.
2. Write out 2-3 realistic, natural-language buyer queries a prospect might type into ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude when looking for that solution.
3. For each natural query, brainstorm the 3-5 sub-queries an LLM's fan-out would likely break it into, such as splitting by audience, price, and use case.
4. Check via manual searches or an AI-visibility tracking tool whether your product or page currently ranks or gets cited for each sub-query individually, not just the head term.
5. For every sub-query where you're absent, create or optimize a specific page or section explicitly targeting that exact angle.
6. Prioritize the sub-queries that overlap across multiple fan-outs, since appearing in all of the fan-out branches is what gets an entity into the final synthesized answer.
7. Re-test the full query set periodically as LLM fan-out behavior and your own content coverage change.

### 19. The "source signal stack": distance from brand equals more LLM trust  `19.4`
*core · content insights · source 19*

Kaleigh's named framework, the "source signal stack," holds that the further a piece of content is from a brand's direct control, the more large language models trust and cite it. Tier one is content the brand fully owns and publishes itself, such as the company blog, website, or the brand's own YouTube and Instagram. Tier two is named employees or experts posting under their own identity, such as a product manager describing how they solved a technical problem on LinkedIn, still brand-connected but one step removed. Tier three is B2B creators who can be paid to post about the product but don't work at the company and aren't directly connected to it. Tier four, the highest-trust tier, is content totally outside brand control, such as organic Reddit mentions, earned media, or someone referencing the company by name on a podcast without any paid placement, signal that "just authentically happened."

> "Layer four is totally out of brand control"

**Evidence:** Kaleigh cites Red Bull's domain authority of 93 out of 100 as an example of a brand whose extreme earned-media volume, generated by stunts that get "crazy earned media," sits at the top of the trust tier.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau's GEO strategy, don't treat all content sources as equal even when the words are similar: prioritize, in ascending order of AI trust, genuine customer and analyst mentions on Reddit and in earned press, named Pabau employees posting expertise under their own identity on LinkedIn, and creator or B2B partnerships, while treating owned blog content as the necessary foundation rather than the highest-trust layer for AI citation.

**Apply anywhere:** For your GEO strategy, don't treat all content sources as equal even when the words are similar: prioritize, in ascending order of AI trust, genuine customer and analyst mentions on Reddit and in earned press, named your employees posting expertise under their own identity on LinkedIn, and creator or B2B partnerships, while treating owned blog content as the necessary foundation rather than the highest-trust layer for AI citation.

> "we track multiple related prompts for a topic"

**Evidence:** InnovationCast tracked on 'best innovation management software' versus 'innovation portfolio management software' showed very different visibility on the same underlying page.

4. Report the topic as a share across the whole basket, not as a yes or no on one prompt.
5. Keep the basket fixed between checks so movement is comparable over time.
6. Add a new phrasing only when a genuinely new buyer question appears, and note the date you added it.
