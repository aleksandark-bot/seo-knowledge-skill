### 1. Publish a how-to that does your product's job by hand  `136.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 136*

In June 2018 Grow and Convert published a post for Leadfeeder describing how to do manually, without the tool, what Leadfeeder does automatically. It generated over 100 signups by the time the case study was written, making it one of the highest-performing single posts of the engagement. They call it out as an aside they think every SaaS business should try: teach your audience to accomplish what your tool accomplishes, without your tool. The mechanism they give is qualification. Someone who chooses to read a long manual workaround has the problem right now and is willing to work on it, so the tool becomes a natural upsell rather than a pitch. Note the contrast with their earlier month: two May 2018 posts on marketing automation stacks and integrations produced almost no long-term signups, so the manual how-to was not just a good month.

> "which has generated over 100 signups to date"

**Evidence:** Leadfeeder's manual how-to post, published June 2018, produced over 100 signups; two marketing automation posts published the month before produced almost none long term.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the job your product automates in one sentence, using the customer's words.
2. Document the full manual method for that job: the spreadsheet, the exports, the reports, the copy-paste steps.
3. Publish it as a genuinely complete how-to that works if the reader never buys anything.
4. Target the query someone searches when trying to do it by hand, not your product category term.
5. Place the product mention at the point in the process where the manual method becomes painful, not in the intro.
6. Add a short comparison of time spent manually versus with the tool, using real figures from the walkthrough.
7. Track signups attributed to that single post separately, since it should outperform the rest of the mid-funnel set.
8. Repeat for every distinct job the product automates rather than combining them into one long guide.

**Pitfall:** Writers hold back the useful detail so the reader has to buy. That kills the post's ranking and its credibility; the signups come from readers who trust the walkthrough and then decide the manual route is not worth it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish complete manual walkthroughs of the jobs it automates: running clinic recalls from a spreadsheet, tracking consent forms on paper, chasing no-shows by hand, reconciling stock manually. Each becomes its own article with the Pabau section placed where the manual method breaks down.

**Apply anywhere:** Write a complete how-to teaching readers to do your product's job manually, holding nothing back. Readers who work through it are highly qualified, and the tool reads as an obvious upgrade rather than a pitch.

### 2. Publish short accurate news posts the day a vendor changes its rates  `168.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 168*

CPC Strategy's blog grew largely because it functioned as a trade news desk for comparison-shopping advertising. Nii Ahene gives the concrete example: when PriceGrabber moved its rate card from 20 cents a click to 30 cents a click, CPC Strategy was first to report it. When Shopzilla changed its ranking algorithm or its ad-serving network, they reported that too. These posts were deliberately short. Nii says they focused on quality over quantity and treated speed of accurate publication as the primary job, not length. The mechanism is that in a narrow niche the vendor changes affect every practitioner's budget immediately, so being the first credible source makes the blog a destination people check rather than a page they find once through search.

> "we would be the first to report it"

**Evidence:** Nii Ahene cites PriceGrabber moving from 20 to 30 cents a click and Shopzilla algorithm changes as posts CPC Strategy broke first.

**How to do it**

1. List the 10 to 20 vendors, platforms or regulators whose changes directly affect your readers' costs or workflow.
2. Subscribe to each one's changelog, pricing page, developer blog and support forum, and set a change-detection alert on the pricing URL.
3. Assign one person to check the alert feed daily and to publish within hours of a confirmed change.
4. Keep each news post short: what changed, the old number, the new number, the effective date, and what readers must do.
5. Verify the change against the vendor's own page or a direct statement before publishing, since accuracy is the whole asset.
6. Link every news post back to your deeper evergreen guide on that vendor so the news traffic feeds the ranking page.
7. Track which vendors' changes drive the most repeat visits and narrow your monitoring list to those.

**Pitfall:** Rewriting news you read on another site adds nothing and arrives late. If your post is not first or does not add a number the vendor published, skip it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can run this on aesthetic and healthcare practice regulation and software changes: CQC guidance updates, insurer coding changes, Apple or Stripe payment rule changes that hit practices. Short, dated posts published the same week, each linking into the relevant template or code-reference page.

**Apply anywhere:** Pick the vendors and regulators whose changes hit your readers' costs, monitor their pricing and changelog pages daily, and publish a short dated post the same day a change lands. Link each one into your evergreen guide on that vendor.

### 3. Pull the top five ranking pages in as the source material  `64.3`
*core · ai workflows · source 64*

The step he treats as the whole reason the output ranks. He searches the validated keyword, takes the top-ranking listings - about five of them - and uses all of the content from those pages as the source material for the article. His stated reasoning, repeated across his videos: we already know this content ranks, and because it ranks right now, Google is telling us it's a good answer for that query. Supplying it as source material sets the constraints - these are the things most likely to rank on page one - which creates a stronger likelihood that the new article ranks for the keyword too. It's the difference between asking a model what it knows and asking it to write within the boundary of what Google has already validated.

> "find the top ranking listings"

**How to do it**

1. Search your validated target keyword.
2. Take the top five ranking results, ignoring ads and irrelevant result types.
3. Extract the full body content of each page.
4. Supply all of it as labelled source material rather than summarising it.
5. Instruct the model to write within that material rather than from general knowledge.
6. Check the finished piece covers everything the ranking set covers before publishing.

**Pitfall:** Consolidating five ranking pages produces a page with no original contribution - it will rank, but it adds nothing, which is why every other version of this workflow adds an interview transcript on top.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat the page-one scrape as the coverage floor, not the article - the piece only becomes worth publishing once Pabau's own data, product knowledge or clinic experience is layered on top of it.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat the page-one scrape as the coverage floor, not the article - the piece only becomes worth publishing once your own data, product knowledge or first-hand experience is layered on top of it.

### 4. Put a Write for Us link on your own site to source writers  `164.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 164*

Grow and Convert say a writer job link on their own website has been their most effective sourcing method, ahead of job boards and marketplaces. Most of their best team members heard about the agency through something it published or a podcast appearance, then found the Write for Us link. They acknowledge being a content agency makes writers more likely to browse their site, but argue the tactic still works for product companies outside marketing: writers are people who check out companies they find interesting, and a writer already on your site for another reason is a qualified candidate because they already know and possibly like what you do. The instruction is to make it easy for those visitors to see that you are hiring, and specifically that you are hiring writers.

> "this has been the most effective method of finding good writers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report the on-site Write for Us link outperformed job boards and marketplace sites, and that most of their best team members arrived through it.

**How to do it**

1. Publish a Write for Us page on your own domain describing the work, the pay and the process.
2. Link it from the site footer and from the bottom of blog articles, not only from a careers page.
3. Say the per-article rate on the page so unqualified applicants self-select out.
4. Embed the application form on the page, including the on-the-fly mini sample question.
5. Mention the page whenever you appear on a podcast or publish a piece with your byline.
6. Track the source of every applicant so you can compare this channel against boards and referrals.

**Pitfall:** Burying the writer listing inside a generic careers page. The mechanism is a writer already browsing your content noticing you hire writers, and a careers link labeled only 'Careers' does not trigger that.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should add a Write for Us page linked from the blog footer, aimed at writers who know aesthetics or healthcare practice operations, since those visitors are already reading Pabau's articles.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish a Write for Us page on your own site and link it from the blog footer and article ends. Writers who are already reading your content are pre-qualified candidates, and this often beats job boards.

### 6. Put the exact keyword in title, URL, H1, and opening sentence  `42.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 42*

The host's own repeatable on-page formula for fast rankings is to place the exact target keyword phrase in the page title, the URL slug, the H1, the beginning of the first sentence of body copy, and typically the meta description too. He gives a specific case study: he wanted to rank for a keyword where The New York Times was ranking number one and Forbes number two, but neither had actually targeted the keyword this precisely, since they were ranking for it only incidentally. After applying this exact five-spot placement formula to his own page, and because he already had what he calls "adequate authority" built up, he says he outranked both of them and reached position one within hours.

> "the page title, URL slug, H1, beginning of the first sentence"

**How to do it**

1. Confirm the exact target keyword phrase you intend to rank for before writing anything else on the page.
2. Place that exact phrase in the page's title tag.
3. Place that exact phrase in the page's URL slug.
4. Place that exact phrase in the page's H1 heading.
5. Place that exact phrase at the very beginning of the first sentence of the page's body copy.
6. Place that exact phrase in the page's meta description as well.
7. Publish the page only once your site already has some baseline topical authority built up via the earlier link-building and foundational-page steps, since the case study attributes the hours-long ranking speed to pre-existing authority, not the on-page formula alone.
8. Check rankings for the target keyword within the following 24-48 hours to see how quickly the page moves, given this combination of on-page optimization and existing authority (inferred verification timeframe).

**Pitfall:** This speed of ranking, hours, beating The New York Times and Forbes, is explicitly attributed to already having "adequate authority" built up beforehand — applying the same exact-match placement formula on a brand-new site with no authority would not be expected to produce the same result.

### 7. Put the reader's actual search term in the title instead of a vague company framing  `74.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 74*

David Quaid picks out the starter guide's 'expect your readers' search terms' line as vital, and gives a failure example he sees constantly: a post titled 'how we made our industry better.' He asks which industry, and points out that Google has no way to translate it. His prediction is exact. Write that title and you will only rank for the word 'industry', not for your actual industry in place of that word. He extends this to the belief that LLMs and Google understand everything from site context: they do not, and if the query works in an LLM it is because the model already knows your brand, not because the title was clear. He is openly skeptical of Google's own claim that its matching systems are sophisticated enough to handle vague phrasing.

> "you're only going to rank for the word industry and not your industry"

**Evidence:** David's worked example: a post titled 'how we made our industry better' ranks only for 'industry'.

**How to do it**

1. Before writing, name the exact phrase a reader would type, in their words not the company's.
2. Put that phrase in the title, the H1, the URL slug and the first sentence.
3. Replace every category word (industry, sector, solution, platform) with the specific named entity it stands for.
4. Read the title cold and ask whether a stranger could tell what topic it is about without your brand context.
5. Check the phrase against a live SERP to confirm the pages ranking for it match what you are about to write.
6. After publishing, check GSC queries for the page and rewrite the title if the queries coming in are the generic word rather than the specific one.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Clever, self-referential headlines on company blogs. They pull no queries, and the failure looks like a traffic problem when it is a naming problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog titles should always name the specific procedure, code or workflow rather than saying 'the aesthetics industry' or 'practice management'. On /diagnostic-codes/ and /procedure-codes/ pages the code itself must appear in the title, since that is the literal search term.

**Apply anywhere:** Name the specific entity in every title instead of a category word, and confirm the phrase against a live SERP before writing.

### 8. Put your target keyword first in LinkedIn posts/articles  `19.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 19*

LinkedIn's own domain has very high authority, and a LinkedIn post's keyword placement flows straight into ranking factors: if you put your target keyword at the very beginning of a LinkedIn post or article, that keyword also lands at the start of the post's URL slug, its page title, and its meta description — three of the highest-value on-page ranking spots — all on a site Google already prioritizes heavily because it's user-generated content. Kaleigh reports LinkedIn articles built this way have hit the number-one organic ranking spot within 24 hours, something she says she could never achieve that fast otherwise, and have been picked up into Google's AI Overview and featured snippet. She calls this an underused hack she expects more people to catch onto within six to twelve months, on top of running it as a genuine multi-platform, not just owned-domain, SEO strategy.

> "you have your keyword right at the beginning of the"

**How to do it**

1. Pick the target keyword phrase for the piece of content exactly as you would for a normal blog post.
2. Open LinkedIn and start a new LinkedIn Article, not just a short status post, so the platform generates a dedicated URL and title.
3. Type the target keyword as the literal first words of the article's headline/title field.
4. Confirm the keyword also appears at the very start of the article body's opening sentence.
5. Publish the article and check LinkedIn's generated URL slug to confirm the keyword landed at the start of it (inferred verification step, since slug generation is automatic).
6. Search the keyword in Google in an incognito window over the next 24-48 hours to check whether the LinkedIn article is ranking and whether it appears in the AI Overview or a featured snippet.
7. Repeat this as a standing practice for high-priority keywords across LinkedIn, alongside your normal owned-domain content, as part of a deliberate multi-platform strategy.

**Tools:** LinkedIn

**Pitfall:** This only works if the keyword genuinely opens the post — burying it lower in the body loses the URL-slug, title, and meta-description alignment that lets LinkedIn's own domain authority carry the ranking.

### 9. Ramp the agent from 1 article a day to 3, then to 10  `77.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 77*

Schneider is explicit that the pipeline did not start at full throughput. It began at one article a day, then incrementally increased to three per day, then ten. The ramp is the safety mechanism. Starting at ten means you find out the agent's research stage is weak, or its intent match is wrong, only after 300 bad pages are indexed. Starting at one gives you a month of pages to check before the volume makes checking impossible. It also matches the pattern this base records elsewhere: scale in cycles, get a few pages ranking, then add more. Note the tension with the ceiling reported in source 57, which puts the sustainable limit at 50 to 100 articles a month regardless of how the content is humanized. Ten a day is roughly 300 a month, three times that ceiling.

> "Started 1 article a day, then incrementally increase to 3"

**Evidence:** Schneider: started at one article a day, then three per day, then ten. Source 57 puts the sustainable ceiling at 50 to 100 articles a month, which ten a day exceeds threefold.

**How to do it**

1. Run the agent at one article per day for the first 30 days and read every output before it publishes.
2. Log each article's keyword, publish date and first-month impressions in a single sheet.
3. After 30 days, check what share of the first batch has any impressions at all and what share produced a signup.
4. Only raise the cadence to three per day if the batch is gaining impressions and no page has been flagged as thin or duplicative.
5. Hold at three per day for another 30 days and repeat the same check.
6. Raise to ten per day only if the quality checks still pass, and be aware this exceeds the 50 to 100 per month ceiling other practitioners report.
7. Drop the cadence back a step immediately if impressions per new page start falling as volume rises.
8. Keep a human review pass in place at every cadence; the review is what has to scale with the volume, not just the writing.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Ramping on a calendar rather than on evidence is how the fast-rank-then-crash curve happens. The signal to stop is impressions per new page falling as the daily count rises, which means you are adding pages faster than the site's authority can carry them.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau automates article generation, start at one draft a day and hold there until a month of output has been fact-checked and is gaining impressions. Do not plan capacity around Schneider's ten a day; the review pass, not the writing, is Pabau's real constraint.

**Apply anywhere:** Start any automated publishing program at one article a day and raise the cadence only after a month of output shows impressions and no quality flags. Treat ten a day as a claim to verify on your own site, not a target.

### 10. Re-enter every article at 30 days with GSC, analytics and a gap check  `77.5`
*core · ai workflows · source 77*

The stage Schneider treats as the differentiator is the refresh: every article is revisited on a 30-day cycle and rewritten against three live inputs. First, Google Search Console, for what the page is actually ranking and getting impressions for versus what it was written to target. Second, a product analytics source — he names Google Analytics 4 or PostHog — for what the page does with the traffic it gets. Third, a content gap analysis against competing content that has appeared or changed since publication. This matches the wider finding in this base that refreshing declining pages beats publishing new ones, and it is the reason his pipeline is a maintenance system rather than a page mill. The refresh is scheduled, not triggered by a drop, so decay is corrected before it compounds.

> "refreshes the article every 30 days based on live data"

**Evidence:** Schneider specifies a 30-day cycle driven by Search Console, GA4 or PostHog, and a content gap analysis of competing content.

**How to do it**

1. Store each published article's URL and publish date so a scheduler can queue it 30 days later.
2. On the due date, pull that URL's queries from Google Search Console for the last 28 days, with impressions, clicks and average position.
3. Flag every query in positions 2 to 15 that the article does not already address with a heading or a direct answer.
4. Pull the page's behaviour from GA4 or PostHog: entrances, scroll depth and signup events attributed to it.
5. Re-scrape the current top 10 for the target keyword and diff their headings against the article's headings.
6. Have the agent write the additions: a section per unaddressed striking-distance query, plus any subtopic every competitor now covers and you do not.
7. PUT the revised article back to the same CMS record, updating the modified date, and leave the URL unchanged.
8. Re-queue the article for another 30 days and log what was changed so you can tell refreshes from rewrites later.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics 4, PostHog

**Pitfall:** Refreshing on a fixed clock without reading GSC first turns into cosmetic rewriting that changes the modified date and nothing else. The signal is a refresh log full of edits and a flat impression curve.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should add a 30-day re-entry queue for /blog/ and /templates/ articles, driven by the GSC query pull the /SEO flow already runs. The gap check gives those pages a maintenance path instead of leaving them to decay after publication.

**Apply anywhere:** Put every published article on a 30-day refresh queue driven by three inputs: Search Console queries in positions 2 to 15, on-page behaviour from your analytics, and a fresh diff against the current top 10. Update the same URL rather than publishing a replacement.

### 11. Re-record a weak clip as a scripted hook-insight piece using Claude  `68.4`
*core · ai workflows · source 68*

Cody's fix for the common problem where an episode contains a great idea but the clip itself is badly formatted. Instead of shipping the clip, he re-records the idea in the format that actually performs: hook first, then deliver on the promise of the hook. He drops the whole transcript into Claude, asks for ten insights on the topic, then asks Claude to write each one in hook-insight format, producing ten scripts. Then he records himself delivering those scripts to camera. His framing is that this is derivative content off the good ideas that occurred inside the show, so the show becomes an idea generator rather than the only asset. This is distinct from clipping: it separates the value of the insight from the accident of how it happened to be said on the day.

> "list 10 insights related to growth marketing"

**Evidence:** Cody uses this on his own show, prompting for insights related to growth marketing.

**How to do it**

1. After each episode, note which ideas were strong but poorly delivered on camera.
2. Paste the full transcript into Claude.
3. Prompt: list 10 insights related to [your topic].
4. Review the list and cut any insight you would not defend publicly.
5. Prompt Claude to write each surviving insight in hook-insight format as a short script.
6. Record yourself delivering each script to camera in one sitting.
7. Schedule the re-recorded clips alongside the raw clips and compare performance.

**Tools:** Claude

**Prompt / template:**

```text
List 10 insights related to growth marketing.

Now write a hook-insight format script for each of these 10 insights.
```

**Pitfall:** Shipping the raw clip because it exists wastes the idea. A good insight with a weak hook underperforms a mediocre insight with a strong one, so the format failure is the real cost.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's recorded practitioner interviews should be mined for insight lists this way, and the strongest ones re-recorded to camera rather than clipped verbatim.

**Apply anywhere:** When an episode contains a great idea delivered badly, extract insights from the transcript with an AI model, have it script them hook-first, and re-record. The idea is the asset, not the original take.

### 12. Read the first 1,000 words of the top 10 results, not just the headings  `71.2`
*core · ai workflows · source 71*

Devesh's brief tool Googles the target keyword and reads the first thousand words of each of the first ten organic listings, then reports the patterns it found. In the demo it reported that every ranking result was a free tool landing page with an interactive input box, that all of them defined what a content brief is, and that all were generic. He is blunt about the alternative: skimming headlines is not the same as reading the content and knowing the arguments the top ten results make. He calls a brief that ignores what is currently ranking borderline useless, because it cannot tell you what Google is already favoring for that keyword. This is the step almost every free brief generator skips, and it became the article's main attack line.

> "skimming headlines is not the same as actually reading the content"

**Evidence:** The tool read the first 1,000 words of the top 10 organic results for 'AI content brief generator' and surfaced that every result was a free tool page and none of them analyzed the SERP, which became the article's core positioning.

**How to do it**

1. Run the target keyword in Google and capture the top 10 organic URLs.
2. Pull the first 1,000 words of body copy from each of those 10 pages, not just their H2s.
3. Write down the page type each result is, for example free tool landing page, listicle, product page or blog post.
4. List the patterns every result shares, such as a shared definition, a shared framework or a shared interactive element.
5. List the weaknesses common to all of them, and mark each one as an angle you can take.
6. Decide the format from that read: match the dominant page type, or deliberately break from it and say why.
7. Put the pattern list and the weakness list into the brief so the writer inherits the research.

**Tools:** Wave Writer, Google

**Pitfall:** Heading-scraping tools produce a brief that looks researched but carries no argument-level intelligence. You find out when the draft comes back saying the same things as page one in a different order.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO and /generate briefs should include an argument-level read of the top ten results, not just entity and heading extraction, so articles on crowded terms like practice management software attack a specific weakness rather than restating the SERP.

**Apply anywhere:** Have whoever writes your briefs read the opening 1,000 words of each of the top ten results and record the shared weaknesses. That weakness list is your angle, and heading scrapers never produce it.

### 13. Read the ranking SERP yourself before drafting a bottom-funnel post  `72.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 72*

Devesh Khanal's first step on any bottom-of-funnel listicle is to open the SERP and read what already ranks, on the reasoning that Google is tipping its hand about what it values for that query. He does this manually before touching any tool or brief. He reads the organic results rather than the AI Overview citations, and notes he may be doing that out of habit rather than principle. He then reads the ranking pages for themes and pain points, not for structure alone. He is explicit that you do not need his tool to get the value: the process is read the SERP, work out search intent, note the pain points, and only then outline. His agency ran this same manual process for eight years before AI tools existed.

> "you got to read what Google's already ranking"

**Evidence:** Khanal says his agency ran this exact manual SERP-reading process for eight years before AI tools existed.

**How to do it**

1. Search the target keyword and list every organic result on page one, ignoring the AI Overview citations for now.
2. Open each result and note the page type: listicle roundup, vendor landing page, forum thread, review site.
3. Write down which page type dominates, since that is the format Google has decided to rank.
4. For each page, record the pain points and themes the writer chose to address, not just the headings.
5. Note the common patterns across results, for example whether nearly all of them define the term first.
6. Note the shared weaknesses, for example evaluation criteria that are all surface level.
7. Turn the pain points and the weaknesses into your outline before writing a single sentence.

**Tools:** Google

**Pitfall:** Skipping straight from keyword to outline. Khanal says a tool or writer that goes straight from keyword to article has by definition skipped a critical part of quality SEO writing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before David optimizes or generates any Pabau comparison or 'best software for' page, read the ten ranking URLs manually and log page type, pain points and shared weaknesses into the brief. That log, not the keyword, should drive the outline.

**Apply anywhere:** Before you write any bottom-of-funnel comparison page, read the ten ranking URLs by hand and log the page type, the pain points each covers and the weaknesses they share. Build the outline from that log rather than from the keyword.

### 14. Refuse a keyword-list-and-briefs deliverable without implementation attached  `179.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 179*

Grow and Convert describe a common agency deliverable that looks generous and produces nothing: keyword research plus a giant list of recommendations and content briefs, with the actual page creation pushed onto the client or priced as an expensive add-on. They give three failure modes. The lists are usually hundreds of keywords the company could have pulled from any SEO tool itself, rarely categorized or prioritized by buying intent, and skewed toward high-volume low-intent terms because that is what the tools suggest. The briefs leave the client wondering how to produce 200 or 500 articles good enough to rank. The result is that most of the recommendations never get implemented, which makes the whole deliverable useless. Their rule is to hire only agencies that create and publish the pages.

> "put the onus of actually creating the pages"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say most of the keyword and content recommendations companies receive from agencies never get implemented, rendering the deliverable basically useless.

**How to do it**

1. Ask whether writing, editing and publishing the pages is inside the retainer or an add-on, and get the answer in writing.
2. If it is an add-on, price it before signing anything, because the base engagement is worthless without it.
3. Ask how many pages per month the retainer actually produces, and compare that to the size of the keyword list they plan to deliver.
4. Refuse an uncategorized keyword list; require every keyword tagged by buying intent and ranked, not just exported.
5. Ask who publishes to the CMS and who owns the internal linking, images and metadata on each page.
6. Cap the brief count at what your team plus theirs can actually publish in the contract term.
7. Score month three on pages published and ranking, not on briefs delivered.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Do you create and publish the pages, or do you hand over briefs? If you hand over briefs, how many articles per month do you expect us to produce, and who writes them?
```

**Pitfall:** Judging a proposal by the size of the keyword list. A 500-keyword deliverable looks like more value than a 20-page publishing plan, but it converts to zero published pages and leaves the client overwhelmed and unsure where to begin.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any content vendor Pabau engages should be scoped in published pages per month on pabau.com, with the WordPress publishing step included. A brief-only deliverable would sit unused because the in-house team is already the bottleneck.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not buy keyword research and content briefs on their own. Confirm in writing that the agency writes, edits and publishes the pages, find out how many pages a month that actually means, and require every keyword to arrive tagged and ranked by buying intent. Judge month three on pages live and ranking, not on briefs delivered.

### 15. Refuse to outsource content unless the vendor has a product-expertise process  `121.9`
*core · best practices · source 121*

Grow and Convert's rule follows from their step three, selling the product inside every post. If the content has to discuss product features, differentiators and how they beat other solutions, then the person writing it must know those things. Their conclusion is that software companies should not fully outsource content to freelance writers or agencies unless that vendor has a process for developing deep expertise in the product and its differentiators, and they say most do not. Their own solution is to interview experts at the client company for every piece of content. They contrast this with what they call Google research papers: quickly learning a topic by Googling it and regurgitating what everyone else says, which they attribute to many digital marketing agencies and freelancers. The test is not whether the writer is internal or external, it is whether a repeatable expertise-extraction step exists.

> "unless those freelance writers or agencies have a process"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert interview experts at their clients' companies for every piece of content, and say most freelancers and agencies have no equivalent process.

**How to do it**

1. Ask any prospective writer or agency to describe the specific step in their process that gets product knowledge into a draft.
2. Reject an answer that is a kickoff call plus a brand guide, since that is a one-time transfer and topics change.
3. Require a recorded expert interview per article, not per engagement.
4. Name the internal expert for each planned article before the article is commissioned.
5. Ask for a sample where the draft names features and differentiators a competitor's writer could not have known.
6. Score every delivered draft on whether it explains how the product solves the problem, and reject drafts that only summarize the SERP.
7. Keep the interview recordings and transcripts so later articles can reuse them without rebooking time.

**Pitfall:** Judging a writer on writing quality alone. A well-written article with no product specifics reads fine and converts like a top-of-funnel post, and the deficit only shows up in the conversion report months later.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any writer producing Pabau bottom-funnel pages needs a booked interview with product or customer success per article, not a single onboarding session. Drafts that describe practice management generically should be sent back.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not hand content fully to an outside writer unless they have a repeatable way of extracting product expertise, such as a recorded expert interview per article. Most do not, and the gap shows up as generic drafts.

### 16. Reject all three common briefing models for pushing argument formation onto writers  `126.2`
*core · best practices · source 126*

Grow and Convert name the three approaches most companies use: let the writer self-research from a keyword list, hand the writer a content brief and let them do the rest, or have the writer source outside experts. They argue all three share one flaw, which is that they put the onus on the freelance writer to formulate the detailed arguments for the piece and to find supporting information for those arguments. Their position is that this should not be the writer's responsibility because the writer is not the subject matter expert. The fix is not a better writer or a longer brief; it is moving argument formation inside the company and leaving the writer to structure and write. They add that asking writers to just get it is itself the smoke signal of a broken process.

> "They put the onus on the freelance writer"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite Peep Laja's complaint about hundreds of mediocre applicants as the symptom they are re-diagnosing.

**How to do it**

1. Write down which of the three models you currently use for each content stream.
2. For each stream, name who decides the actual arguments inside the piece.
3. If the answer is the writer, move that decision to a named internal expert.
4. Restate the writer's job as structuring, phrasing and evidencing the expert's arguments.
5. Stop scoring writer applications on industry knowledge you are no longer asking them to supply.
6. Re-scope the brief so it carries the arguments, not just the keyword and the goal.

**Pitfall:** Believing the problem is writer quality. Grow and Convert say that with a broken process even really good writers produce bad content, which makes it look like the writer just does not get it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's writing process should never hand a keyword and a Clearscope doc to a writer and expect a differentiated aesthetic-practice article. David should make argument formation a Pabau-side job, captured in an interview, and treat the writer as the person who executes it.

**Apply anywhere:** Stop expecting freelance writers to invent your arguments. Whichever briefing model you use, the arguments inside each section have to come from someone in your company who lives the topic; the writer's job is to structure and evidence them.

### 17. Reject any post idea that is really a whole blog, not a post  `98.2`
*core · best practices · source 98*

Benji Hyam's test for a generic idea. Reviewing worksheets from nine companies in the Customers From Content program, he found most submitted ideas like 'How To Get More Customers For Your Startup' from an app referral SaaS, and 'So you just realized your business needs an app. Where to start from?' from a dev shop. His diagnosis is that these are not blog posts, they are entire blogs: whole sites exist covering each one. He caught himself doing it too, drafting 'How To Create Valuable Content' before Devesh Khanal killed it as too broad. The practical test he applies is whether you can name where the post starts. For the app idea he asks: do you talk about internal decisions, user stories, design, or budget? Too many valid answers means the topic is too broad and the post will be average at best.

> "These ideas are entire blogs."

**Evidence:** Nine companies in Grow and Convert's Customers From Content program submitted ideas and most came back too generic, across case study, narrative, how-to, opinion, mega guide and data post formats.

**How to do it**

1. For each proposed title, ask whether an existing site or a whole site section is dedicated to that subject; if yes, reject it.
2. Ask where the post would start, and list every legitimate opening angle.
3. Reject the idea if more than two opening angles are equally valid, because the writer will end up surveying rather than answering.
4. Ask whether the finished piece would be a book rather than an article; if yes, reject it.
5. Rewrite the rejected idea as one of its sub-angles, chosen because it matches a pain point you have evidence for.
6. Keep the rejected broad idea as the name of the cluster the narrow posts will sit under.
7. Run this check at the idea stage, before a brief is written or a writer is briefed.

**Pitfall:** The broad idea passes review because it genuinely solves a pain point and genuinely matches the audience. Those two checks are not enough; specificity is the third and it is the one that gets skipped.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should add a 'is this a post or a blog?' gate to Pabau's article commissioning step. Titles like 'How to run a successful aesthetic clinic' fail it; 'How to cut no-shows with automated SMS reminders' passes.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a specificity gate to your idea review. If a whole site already covers the topic, or if you can name three equally valid places the article could start, it is a content cluster and not an article. Split it before briefing a writer.

### 18. Reject both common freelance briefing models as insufficient  `163.7`
*core · best practices · source 163*

Grow and Convert name the two ways SEOs usually work with freelance writers, and reject both. The first is handing over a topic or keyword and letting the writer self-research. They call the output a Google Research Paper: the writer searches the term and regurgitates what everyone else says, producing generic content unlikely to rank for valuable keywords or convert anyone. The second is a slightly more prescriptive content brief with a light outline of subheadings and topics, which still leaves the writer to work out the key arguments alone. Both fail for the same two reasons: no process for gaining and expressing product and domain expertise, and freelance writers who are often untrained in on-page SEO and cannot turn a brief into a piece capable of ranking for a competitive term. The fix is a brief that supplies the arguments and an interview that supplies the expertise.

> "We call these "Google Research Papers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's assessment from working with dozens of clients and observing their prior vendors' writer workflows.

**How to do it**

1. Stop issuing topic-only assignments to writers; they produce a summary of page one and nothing else.
2. Upgrade the brief past a subheading outline: state the specific arguments and claims the piece must make.
3. Attach a recorded interview or transcript with an internal expert as a required source for every bottom-funnel piece.
4. Name the target keyword, the intent, and the on-page requirements explicitly rather than assuming the writer knows them.
5. Have someone SEO-trained do the on-page pass after the draft, rather than expecting the freelancer to handle it.
6. Spot-check drafts against the top three ranking pages: if your piece says the same things, send it back.
7. Track which writers can carry the argument-level brief and concentrate work with them.

**Pitfall:** A light outline feels like a real brief, so the gap is invisible until the piece publishes and stalls on page three. The tell is a draft where every claim could appear on a competitor's blog unchanged.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs must carry the arguments and the source facts, not just headings. The /generate and /SEO flows already harvest source facts, so the gap to watch is whether the brief states the differentiating claim the article should make.

**Apply anywhere:** Make your briefs carry the arguments and source facts, not just headings. The gap to watch is whether the brief states the differentiating claim the article should make.

### 19. Replace the logistics content brief with an audience-and-SERP questionnaire  `124.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert argue the standard freelance brief guides nothing that matters. Their example brief lists keyword, audience job title, a 2,000-2,500 word count, stats no older than three years, an image every 250-300 words, paragraphs under four sentences, h3 and h4 but no h5, exact-match keyword in title and first heading, Grammarly 90+, and Hemingway at seventh-grade reading level. All of it is formatting and basic on-page work that can be done at the end of drafting. They replace it with a questionnaire the writer fills in after interviewing the client, covering two things: the specific pain point that made the reader search, and what the ranking SERP says about intent. Only after those two sections does the writer propose a title, intro type and structure in a deliberately blank outline field.

> "the brief is ineffective for guiding the writer to produce great content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite the real brief the author received as a freelance content writer, and eight years of building bottom-of-funnel content for clients.

**How to do it**

1. Delete word count, image cadence, Grammarly and Hemingway targets from the brief and move them to a pre-publish checklist.
2. Replace section one with: name the specific pain point that drove this reader to search this keyword, in one paragraph.
3. Reject answers at the level of the audience is a startup looking for accounting software as surface-level.
4. Replace section two with: what does the current ranking SERP say about intent, and which recurring angle appears across results.
5. Ask the writer to note any repeated hook in the SERP, for example every result pushing free tools, and decide whether to cover or counter it.
6. Leave section three as a blank outline field where the writer proposes title, intro type and heading structure.
7. Require the pain point to come from a recorded client interview, not from the client website.
8. Approve the questionnaire before drafting, and review the draft against it rather than against formatting rules.

**Tools:** Grammarly, Hemingway

**Pitfall:** Teams keep the logistics brief and bolt the questionnaire on top, so writers optimize the measurable parts and skip the argument. The signal is a draft that hits every formatting rule and still says nothing a competitor page does not.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should rewrite the Pabau article brief so pain point and SERP intent come first and word count moves to a pre-publish check. For an aesthetic-practice keyword the brief must name what made the practice owner search, not just say the audience is a clinic owner.

**Apply anywhere:** Rewrite your content brief so it opens with the reader's specific pain point and what the SERP says about intent, then leaves the outline open for the writer. Push word count, image cadence and readability scores to a pre-publish checklist instead.

### 20. Research the product and its rivals, not the industry, before kickoff  `134.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 134*

Grow and Convert's pre-kickoff routine for an industry they know nothing about is deliberately product-shaped, not industry-shaped. They read everything on the client's website about the product, search the product name on YouTube for demos and explainers, repeat both steps for the top three competitors, then read reviews of the client and of those competitors looking for named benefits and named frustrations. Anything they do not understand goes straight onto a question list for the kickoff call rather than being googled to death. They stress the focus is products, not general industry how-to material, because the target output is bottom-of-funnel content. The whole point is that it is far more efficient to ask a subject matter expert to correct what you already learned than to ask them to teach the industry from scratch.

> "Read reviews of the client's product/service online"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert used this process for Rainforest QA, a no-code software testing platform in a niche with its own lingo, and produced ranking content from day one.

**How to do it**

1. Ask for the top three competitors before the engagement starts, so the research list is fixed.
2. Read every product, feature and pricing page on the client's site end to end.
3. Search the product name on YouTube and watch any demo, explainer or walkthrough video.
4. Repeat the site-plus-YouTube pass for each of the three competitors.
5. Read G2, Capterra or Google Reviews for the client and each competitor, marking every named benefit and named frustration.
6. Keep a running question list of everything you could not resolve online.
7. Cap the whole research phase at what you can hand off as questions, not at industry mastery.

**Tools:** YouTube, Google Reviews

**Pitfall:** Teams substitute a general industry education for product research, so they arrive able to discuss the sector and still unable to explain what the product does differently. The signal is a question list full of definitions rather than differentiators.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before writing any pabau.com comparison or 'best software' page, David should run this pass on Pabau plus the three named competitors: their sites, their demo videos, and their G2 review complaints. The frustrations in competitor reviews are the raw material for the 'what to look for' section.

**Apply anywhere:** Before writing a comparison or category page, read your own product pages, watch product demo videos, then do the same for your three closest competitors and read reviews of all four. Competitor review complaints are the raw material for buying-guide criteria.

### 21. Restore an expired domain by exporting its Wayback content into a new CMS  `67.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 67*

Dirk's own successful revival, from 2017 or 2018, was a travel domain on a country-specific TLD rather than a .com. He chose it because the backlink profile was genuinely clean, it had a handful of links from authoritative tourism sites and some were still live, and Wayback Machine held the full content. He exported all the text and images out of Wayback using an export tool, built a new CMS on the domain, and reinjected the old content, then cleaned it up and rebuilt the internal link structure by reading it end to end. Within a few months it was generating just under 1,000 euros a month for very little effort, and it still generates similar numbers today. It has moved only slightly through core updates, and he now keeps it as a test bed where he deliberately overloads the homepage with content to see which way updates push it.

> "I reverted the whole content from Wayback Machine"

**Evidence:** Just under 1,000 euros a month within a few months of restoration, sustained at similar levels from 2017 or 2018 through to today with only minor movement across core updates.

**How to do it**

1. Pick a candidate with a clean backlink profile where some of the old links are still live, and check them individually rather than trusting a total.
2. Prefer a country-specific TLD in the geo you want, which Dirk credits as part of why this one worked.
3. Use a Wayback export tool to pull every archived page's text and images, not just the homepage.
4. Stand up a fresh CMS on the domain rather than restoring the old platform.
5. Reinject the archived content at the original URLs so the surviving backlinks still resolve.
6. Read the whole restored site and rebuild the internal linking structure by hand.
7. Clean up the copy rather than republishing it raw, since Dirk credits a little care on content for the reindexation.
8. Give it a few months before judging, and then leave it largely untouched through core updates.

**Tools:** Wayback Machine

**Pitfall:** Restoring content that never had live links behind it. The revenue here came from surviving authoritative tourism backlinks pointing at real archived pages, not from the age of the domain by itself.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau acquires a lapsed aesthetics or clinic-industry domain, restore its original URLs and content from Wayback first so the surviving links resolve, before layering any Pabau content on top.

**Apply anywhere:** When you revive an expired domain, restore its archived pages at their original URLs from Wayback first so surviving backlinks still resolve, then rebuild internal linking and clean the copy.

### 22. Retire top-of-funnel definition posts because AI answers them without citing  `80.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 80*

Grow and Convert argue top-of-funnel content is now dead as a traffic driver, and give the mechanism: information is commoditized at the top of the funnel, so an LLM has no reason to send a user anywhere. They work the example 'what is content marketing' — Ahrefs shows 8,000+ searches a month and a keyword difficulty of 85, yet ChatGPT answers it with no links at all, and Google's own SERP puts a dictionary definition above the fold so the first organic result is below it. Their conclusion is that commoditization fades as you move down the funnel, so effort should move with it. The base already says move top-funnel material off the blog; what this source adds is the test for which pages qualify and the two-engine check that proves it.

> "information commoditization fades as you move down the funnel"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: 'what is content marketing' has 8,000+ monthly searches and KD 85 in Ahrefs, but ChatGPT returns no links and Google shows a dictionary definition above the first organic result.

**How to do it**

1. List every blog URL whose primary query is a definition, a 'what is', or a broad category explainer.
2. Run each query in ChatGPT and record whether the answer carries any source links at all.
3. Run the same query in Google and note whether the first organic result sits above the fold or under a definition box or AI Overview.
4. Mark any page that fails both checks as a non-acquisition asset and stop refreshing it on the normal schedule.
5. Reassign that production budget to bottom-of-funnel pages: jobs-to-be-done, category and alternative queries.
6. Keep a small number of definition pages only where they support internal linking or ad landing pages, and say so in the content plan.
7. Re-run the two-engine check quarterly, since Google keeps expanding what it answers itself.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, ChatGPT, Google

**Pitfall:** Keyword tools still show fat volume and tempting difficulty scores for these terms, so a volume-driven plan keeps commissioning them. The signal you have made this mistake is high impressions with falling clicks and no leads attributed to the page.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should stop commissioning generic aesthetics explainers for the Pabau blog and audit the ones already live with the ChatGPT-plus-Google check. The hours move to pages a practice owner searches when choosing software, such as comparison, pricing and workflow pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your blog for definition-style pages, test each query in both ChatGPT and Google, and stop investing in the ones neither engine sends traffic for. Move that budget to bottom-of-funnel pages where the user is choosing a product.

### 23. Route healthcare drafts through medical review and a compliance sign-off  `117.5`
*core · best practices · source 117*

Grow and Convert note that healthcare content carries compliance requirements that do not exist in other industries, and that the workflow needs two separate gates rather than one. Patient testimonials need explicit consent and often de-identification. Claims about treatment or outcomes may need legal review, particularly for medical device and pharmaceutical companies subject to FDA regulation. Anything referencing patient information has to comply with HIPAA. Their point is that medical accuracy review and legal or compliance sign-off are different checks by different people, and healthcare publishing workflows commonly need both before a piece goes live. The cost of skipping them is not just a correction: they list diminished credibility, compliance exposure and legal consequences.

> "require sign-off from compliance or legal teams before publishing, not just medical review"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite HIPAA, FDA regulation and patient-information handling as the three recurring compliance surfaces across their healthcare clients.

**How to do it**

1. Add two named gates to the publishing workflow: clinical accuracy review, then compliance or legal sign-off.
2. Before using any patient testimonial, get written consent and de-identify details that could identify the person.
3. Flag every sentence that claims a treatment outcome and route those specifically to legal review.
4. Check any content referencing patient information against HIPAA before it leaves the draft stage.
5. Record who signed off and when in the CMS, so a later challenge can be answered.
6. Do not let a deadline collapse the two gates into one reviewer.

**Pitfall:** Teams treat a doctor's read-through as full clearance. A clinician checks accuracy, not consent, advertising claims or FDA exposure, and the gap is where the legal risk sits.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau publishes clinical and procedure content for aesthetics practices. Add a named reviewer step for any article stating a treatment outcome, and never publish a customer story without written consent on file.

**Apply anywhere:** Run healthcare drafts through two separate gates: a clinician for accuracy and a compliance or legal reviewer for consent, claims and regulatory exposure. One reviewer cannot cover both.

### 24. Route surviving gap keywords to re-optimization before new content  `118.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 118*

Grow and Convert close the process with a routing step rather than a writing brief. For each surviving keyword they ask first whether an existing page can be updated and re-optimized to rank for it, because that recovers value from content already paid for and frees budget. Second, they look specifically at the overlapping keywords where both you and the competitor already rank, find the ones where the competitor outranks you, and schedule updates to those pages. Only what is left after both passes becomes new content. This ordering matters because a gap report naturally reads as a list of things to write, and most teams commission all of it, then discover they have published a near-duplicate of a page they already had.

> "are there any opportunities to update and re-optimize current content"

**How to do it**

1. Split the surviving keyword list into three buckets before briefing anything: re-optimize, update-to-outrank, and build new.
2. For each keyword, search your own site for an existing page covering the same intent, using a site: search and your CMS search.
3. Assign to re-optimize where an existing page covers the intent but does not target the phrase in title, H1, URL and opening sentence.
4. Pull the overlap set where both you and the competitor rank, and sort by the gap between your position and theirs.
5. Assign to update-to-outrank every overlap row where the competitor outranks you, and schedule those updates first.
6. Assign to build-new only the keywords with no existing page and no overlap row.
7. Work the three buckets in that order, since re-optimizations ship faster and return sooner than new articles.
8. Re-check positions four to six weeks after each update before commissioning the new-build bucket.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Briefing a new article for a keyword an existing page half-covers creates cannibalization instead of a second ranking, and both pages then sit outside the top ten.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a gap report lands for pabau.com, David should check every row against existing /blog/ and /templates/ pages first, and send the overlap rows where competitors outrank Pabau to /SEO as refreshes before any new article is commissioned.

**Apply anywhere:** Sort surviving gap keywords into re-optimize, update-to-outrank and build-new, and work them in that order so paid-for pages earn before new production starts.

### 25. Run Facebook content ads in three phases: audiences, then articles, then creative  `128.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 128*

Grow and Convert's paid distribution has a fixed order, and the order is the point. Phase one, lasting the first month or two of an engagement, tests audiences only: several cold interest and demographic sets built from the client's customer research, lookalikes built from an uploaded customer list or the client's pixel, and a retargeting set of all site visitors. Nothing else is optimized yet. Phase two holds the winning audiences steady and rotates articles through them, looking for the pieces with the highest engagement, the lowest cost, and any sign of conversions. Phase three, only once both are known, optimizes creative such as headlines and images to drive cost down further. Testing creative before you know the audience confounds both variables and wastes the first month of spend.

> "The first month or two is spent testing various audiences"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's standing three-pronged Facebook process across agency clients, with audience testing occupying the first one to two months.

**How to do it**

1. Run a customer research session with the client and build three to five cold interest and demographic sets from what it surfaces.
2. Upload the client's best-customer list or connect their pixel and build a lookalike from converters only.
3. Add one retargeting set covering all website visitors.
4. Spend the first one to two months on audience tests alone, with the same article and the same creative in every set.
5. Keep only the audiences that engage, then rotate your published articles through those audiences one at a time.
6. Track engagement, cost per landing page view and conversions per article, not just clicks.
7. Move the winning articles into an ongoing always-on campaign and keep feeding new articles into the test slot.
8. Only after audiences and articles are settled, test new headlines and images to push cost down.

**Tools:** Facebook

**Pitfall:** Testing creative, audience and article at the same time. Costs move but you cannot say which variable did it, so nothing carries over to the next campaign.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run any Pabau content ads in this order: first find which aesthetic and healthcare practice-owner audiences engage, then rotate template pages and comparison articles through them, and leave creative testing until last.

**Apply anywhere:** Sequence paid content promotion as audiences first, then articles, then creative. Spend the first month or two finding which cold, lookalike and retargeting audiences engage at all, then hold those fixed while you rotate content through them, and only optimize headlines and images once both are known.
