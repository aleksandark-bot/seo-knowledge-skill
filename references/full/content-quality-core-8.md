### 1. Stack multiple layers of detail behind a single load-bearing claim  `99.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 99*

Khanal dissects Benji's Specificity Strategy post to show what fully backing one claim looks like. The claim is 'Content marketing hasn't become harder, readers have just become smarter.' Khanal says the claim on its own is not impressive, has nothing memorable in it, and shows no expertise, even though it contains a contrast. What makes it work is that Benji spends an entire section explaining the underlying mechanism, that content is too broad in topic, then backs that with actual examples from students' proposed blog topics, then goes further and lists specific student post titles, then dissects those. Khanal calls this multiple layers of details, all to back up one claim. He links the result to link acquisition: per Ahrefs the article picked up 124 backlinks from 27 referring domains since publishing, with no explicit link building and no paid promotion.

> "This is _multiple layers_ of details"

**Evidence:** The Specificity Strategy post earned 124 backlinks from 27 referring domains per Ahrefs, with no explicit link building or paid promotion to the article.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the one claim the article's premise rests on and treat it as the piece's load-bearing claim.
2. Layer one: write a full section explaining the mechanism behind the claim, not a paragraph.
3. Layer two: give a real example drawn from your own work, clients or students.
4. Layer three: add several more examples so the reader sees the pattern, not a single anecdote.
5. Layer four: quote or screenshot the actual artifacts, such as the exact titles or lines involved.
6. Layer five: dissect one artifact in detail, saying what is wrong with it and why.
7. Do not spread this effort evenly; other claims can carry one layer, this one carries five.
8. Check the section afterwards in Ahrefs after six months to see whether it is the page attracting links.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Spreading detail thinly across every claim produces an even but forgettable article. The linkable asset comes from one claim being taken far further than a competitor would bother to take it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Each Pabau article should nominate one load-bearing claim and take it five layers deep, with real practice examples and named artifacts. That section is also the natural home for the article's original visual.

**Apply anywhere:** Nominate one load-bearing claim per article and take it five layers deep with real examples and named artifacts. That section is what earns links.

### 2. Start writing by naming the question page one still leaves unanswered  `134.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 134*

Grow and Convert start every article with a SERP analysis that hunts for holes, framed as one question: what would a searcher still want to know after reading the top two or three articles. For the Rainforest listicle they found none of the ranking pages helped the reader understand the ecosystem of software testing tools or where each tool fit in it, because the listicles took the longer-is-always-better approach and dumped in as many tools as possible without categorizing them or making recommendations. Their verdict is that most of those articles left the reader more confused than when they started. So the first thing their article did was define the categories in plain English, say which category the client's product belonged to, and classify every other tool on the list accurately. That single structural decision is what they credit for the article outperforming longer competitors.

> "What questions would a searcher still have after reading the top two or three articles?"

**Evidence:** The Rainforest QA listicle published 15 July 2021 ranked #1 for 17 keywords by 22 July 2022 and converted at 1.8% of unique page views.

**How to do it**

1. Read the top two or three ranking pages in full and write down every question they leave open.
2. Check specifically whether they organize the options or just list them.
3. Pick the one unanswered question that a buyer must resolve before choosing.
4. Build the article's structure around answering it, not around beating the competitors' item count.
5. If the gap is organization, define the categories in plain English before the list starts.
6. Say explicitly which category your own product sits in.
7. Classify every other item on the list into those same categories, accurately, including rivals.

**Pitfall:** Competing on length. Grow and Convert say the dump-everything listicles left readers more confused than when they arrived, which is why a shorter categorized article beat them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's listicles should open by naming the categories of practice management or aesthetics software, state which one Pabau is, and sort every reviewed vendor into them. That beats adding a twenty-first tool to the list.

**Apply anywhere:** Before writing, list what the top three ranking pages fail to answer. If they only enumerate options, categorize them instead, say where your product sits, and classify every competitor honestly.

### 3. State who you serve, what you are best at, and why you beat rivals  `80.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 80*

Grow and Convert reduce AI recommendation to three things a company must have published: who they are best for, what they are best at, and why they are better than the competition, each backed by case studies and examples. They tested this on themselves. When ChatGPT was asked for the best SaaS marketing agencies it described them as specializing in content that drives demos and signups rather than traffic, with transparent reporting and many case studies. They then show the 'Problems We Solve' section directly under their homepage hero and note the model's description is eerily similar to that copy. The warning attached is that if your site's copy, benefits and use cases look like every competitor's, you stay lost in the shuffle.

> "who they're best for, what they're best at"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show ChatGPT's description of them mirroring the 'Problems We Solve' block under their homepage hero.

**How to do it**

1. Write one sentence each for who you are best for, what you are best at, and why you beat named alternatives.
2. Put those three statements in plain language high on the homepage, near the hero, not buried on an About page.
3. Attach a case study or a concrete example to each claim rather than leaving it as an assertion.
4. Open three competitor homepages side by side and cut any sentence of yours that could appear on theirs unchanged.
5. Repeat the same three statements consistently across service pages, case studies and blog posts so the model sees one story.
6. Ask ChatGPT to describe your company and compare its answer line by line against your copy.
7. Where the model's description is vaguer than your copy, add specifics; where it is wrong, find and fix the page giving it that idea.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Generic benefit copy that matches every competitor gives the model nothing to discriminate on, so it defaults to the better-known brand. You detect it by asking the model to describe you and getting a description that would fit any vendor in the category.

**Apply at Pabau:** The Pabau homepage and product pages should say plainly which practice types Pabau is best for, which jobs it does better than the general-purpose alternatives, and link a case study to each claim. Then check what ChatGPT says back.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish three explicit statements — who you are best for, what you are best at, why you are better — high on the homepage, back each with a case study, and verify by asking a model to describe your company.

### 4. Stop chasing word counts; vary vocabulary to match more queries instead  `74.14`
*core · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid reads the starter guide's line that content length alone does not matter and that there is no magical minimum or maximum word count, joking that you probably want at least one word. He goes further than the guide: thin content is not a real thing, you do not have to write a lot, and a lot of content does not tell Google you did more research. What he does say helps is variation. If you vary the words, writing naturally rather than repetitively, you have more chances of showing up in search simply because you are using more words, meaning more distinct terms to match against queries. That reframes length as a side effect of covering more vocabulary, not a target in itself. He also connects the word-count obsession to tools like RankMath and its keyword density score, which he calls nonsense.

> "you have more chances of showing up in search simply because you are using more words"

**Evidence:** Google's starter guide states there is no magical word count minimum or maximum; David extends this to say thin content is not a real thing.

**How to do it**

1. Delete any minimum word count from your content briefs.
2. Replace it with a vocabulary target: list the distinct terms, synonyms and entity names a reader might search for.
3. Write each section to cover its point completely, then stop, rather than padding to a number.
4. Sweep the draft for repeated phrases and swap them for the genuine alternative wordings readers use.
5. Check GSC's query report after a few weeks and add the wordings you are getting impressions for but did not use.
6. Turn off keyword-density scoring in your SEO plugin so it stops pushing repetition.

**Tools:** RankMath, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Padding an article to hit a word count. It adds repetition rather than vocabulary, which David says is the opposite of what earns extra query matches.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should specify the entities and phrasings to cover rather than a length. On /diagnostic-codes/ and /procedure-codes/ pages, this means covering the alternative names clinicians use for a code rather than expanding the page to hit a length.

**Apply anywhere:** Replace word-count targets in briefs with a list of distinct terms and entity names to cover, and let length fall out of that.

### 5. Stop commissioning Google research papers from writers without topic expertise  `96.8`
*core · content insights · source 96*

Grow and Convert name the core failure of outsourced blog writing. Freelancers without experience in the topic produce what they call 'Google research papers': they research what other companies say and regurgitate a generic, high-level piece. Benji's 2016 term for the result is 'Mirage Content', articles filled with elementary advice that does not impress a potential customer. Three consequences follow. Prospects are underwhelmed or turned off by the brand. The writer does not understand the company or its customers well enough to craft arguments that sell the product inside the post. And the business spends real time and money and never sees ROI. Their bottom line: whoever does the strategy and the writing must have expertise in the topic, or the ability to tap the company's expertise through interviews, plus a real grasp of customer pain points and how the product solves them differently.

> "they do what we call "Google research papers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's agency experience across dozens of clients, and Benji's 2016 Mirage Content article.

**Pitfall:** The failure is invisible in a keyword audit. The article covers every subtopic, scores well on a grader, and still reads as generic to the buyer, so it neither impresses nor sells.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any Pabau article about clinic workflows needs input from someone who has run one, or from a recorded interview with Pabau customer-facing staff. Desk research about aesthetics practices will read as generic to a practice owner.

**Apply anywhere:** Never hand a topic to a writer with no experience in it and no access to your experts. Desk research produces generic content that impresses nobody.

### 6. Stop rewriting explainers — add original value or consolidate them  `66.24`
*core · concrete actions · source 66*

Content that repackages common advice or existing sources without first-hand testing, expert contribution, original data, tools or distinctive media. The framework is blunt about the usual remedy: changing the length or title doesn't fix the problem, because AI can summarize the same consensus and the page gives users no extra reason to visit. It covers broad how-tos, expanded rewrites, exhaustive introductions and 'everything you need to know' guides assembled from public sources without original evidence or practical utility. It scores Low on all five value dimensions at Medium effort — the worst return-to-cost ratio of any type in the worksheet other than programmatic pages from public data. The list of five things that would fix it is the actionable part: first-hand testing, expert contribution, original data, a tool, or distinctive media. Any one of them moves the page into a prioritized type; none of them is a rewrite.

> "repackages common advice or existing sources without first-hand testing"

**How to do it**

1. Identify the site's explainer and 'complete guide' pages, and for each ask what a reader gets here that they cannot get from an AI answer. Write the answer down; if you cannot, it is this type.
2. Rank them by current clicks and conversions to decide which get investment and which get consolidated.
3. For the ones worth keeping, pick one of the five additions and commit to it: run a first-hand test, bring in a named expert, publish original data, build a tool, or produce distinctive media.
4. Schedule the addition as real work with an owner and a date, since this is the step teams substitute a rewrite for.
5. Consolidate the rest into the strongest relevant page — usually a hub, a documentation page or a prioritized content type — and redirect.
6. Explicitly ban the length-and-title refresh as a remedy, and remove it from the content calendar as a task type.
7. Where several explainers cover the same consensus at different lengths, merge them into one and redirect the others, since they were competing for the same intent.
8. Change the commissioning brief so no new explainer is approved without one of the five additions named up front.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Treating a refresh as the fix. Expanding the word count or changing the title leaves the page in exactly the same position — it still contains only what the consensus already contains — while consuming the budget that an original addition needed.

**Apply at Pabau:** This is the biggest single item in Pabau's blog library. For each explainer, either attach something original — Pabau platform data, a named practitioner, a first-hand test, a tool — or consolidate it into the relevant template or feature hub. A rewrite with a new title changes nothing the framework scores.

**Apply anywhere:** This is usually the biggest single item in a blog library. For each explainer, either attach something original — platform data, a named practitioner, a first-hand test, a tool — or consolidate it into the relevant hub. A rewrite with a new title changes nothing the framework scores.

### 7. Structure every JTBD post as help first, then the switch argument  `93.3`
*core · best practices · source 93*

Goolding gives the single template behind both of Grow and Convert's biggest JTBD wins: 'You can do it. Here's how. But it's not ideal, and here's why. We have a better option for you.' The post genuinely serves the search intent first, walking through the manual method step by step with no information withheld. Only after the reader can do the job does the article explain the limits of that method and introduce the product as the easier long-term route. He is explicit that the product argument is made openly and not coyly, and that content-marketing advice about not talking about your product does not apply here, because JTBD keywords carry buying intent. Withholding the answer to force a product pitch breaks the intent match and the page loses the ranking that made it worth writing.

> "'You can do it. Here's how. But it's not ideal, and here's why."

**Evidence:** The Timetastic Outlook post produced 20 free trial signups in its first 6 months; the Circuit Google Maps post has produced thousands of free trial signups since 2020.

**How to do it**

1. Open the article by confirming the job is doable with the tool the reader already has.
2. Give the complete step-by-step for the manual or incumbent method, including screenshots, with nothing held back.
3. State the concrete limits of that method: the fiddly steps, the hard caps, the time cost.
4. Name the specific numeric limit where one exists, as with Google Maps capping at 10 stops.
5. Introduce your product as the alternative for the long term, and say plainly that it is yours.
6. Explain the exact mechanism that removes the pain, for example a calendar integration that syncs automatically.
7. Show that the product works alongside the incumbent rather than replacing the reader's whole setup, where that is true.
8. Close with a trial or demo link tied to the job the article was about, not a generic CTA.

**Pitfall:** Writers hedge the product section to sound objective, and the reader never gets a reason to switch. The signal is a JTBD post that ranks well but produces no signups.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's how-to blog posts should follow this shape exactly: teach the manual method for the clinic task in full, then state its limits, then explain how Pabau removes them and link to /book-demo/. No coyness about it being our product.

**Apply anywhere:** Write every jobs-to-be-done post as help first and pitch second: complete the manual walkthrough, name its limits, then make the case for your product openly.

### 8. Switch into editor mode with three deliberate distancing tricks  `124.5`
*core · best practices · source 124*

Grow and Convert's diagnosis is that most drafts are written to help the writer understand the topic rather than to serve the reader, and that the fix is a genuine role switch before submission. Because that is hard to do on your own work, they give three mechanical ways to create distance. Step away from the piece for a full day so you return with fresh eyes on the logic, not just the typos. Change the font and color to something absurd, their example being purple Comic Sans at size 16, so it feels like a new piece written by someone else. Have a text-to-speech feature read the piece to you, so your ears edit instead of your eyes. They note the audio pass catches structural problems too: as you listen you form an expectation of what comes next, and when it does not arrive, the structure needs work.

> "three different ways to help you switch into the editor role"

**Evidence:** The author reports the day-away method works best for him, and that the text-to-speech pass surfaces structural problems as well as awkward phrasing.

**How to do it**

1. Finish the draft, then stop and put it down for a full day before editing.
2. On return, read for whether the piece serves the reader or only documents your own learning.
3. Change the whole document to an absurd font and color, for example purple Comic Sans at 16pt, and read it again.
4. Fix the typos and styling issues the font change surfaces, then revert the formatting.
5. Run a text-to-speech pass and listen to the whole piece end to end.
6. While listening, note every point where what came next was not what you expected, and mark it as a structure fix.
7. Rewrite the marked sections in the order the listening pass implied, not the order you drafted.
8. Only then run the mechanical checks such as spelling and readability.

**Tools:** Microsoft Word, Google Docs

**Pitfall:** Editing immediately after drafting and calling it a round of edits. You catch typos and miss that the first two paragraphs state the obvious to a reader who already knows the problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's writing process should require a day between draft and edit, and a text-to-speech listen before any article goes to review. That single listen catches the meandering sections a screen read forgives.

**Apply anywhere:** Force a role switch before submitting: sleep on the draft, then reread it in an absurd font, then have text-to-speech read it aloud. The listening pass catches structural gaps a screen read forgives.

### 9. Take a contrarian position against a SERP made of software vendors  `140.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 140*

Lewis found the competitors ranking for her bottom-of-funnel accessibility terms were mostly software companies. Rather than write a better version of their content she built the articles on a position none of them could take: ADA Compliance Pros does the same thing the software does but adds a human touch, and doing part of the audit manually means a higher chance of catching every error, which lowers the chance of being sued by serial litigators. That is a differentiator with a consequence attached, not a claim of quality. The general move is to look at what the ranking pages sell and identify the argument their business model forbids them from making. A tool vendor cannot argue that automation misses things. A pure agency cannot argue for scale. Whichever side of that line you are on is your angle for every article in the set.

> "they were mostly software companies"

**Evidence:** ADA Compliance Pros positioned manual auditing against software-only competitors on lawsuit risk from serial litigators, and reached eight first-page rankings in about four months.

**How to do it**

1. List who owns each ranking page for your target terms and what they sell.
2. Identify the shared assumption their product forces them to defend.
3. Write the opposite claim as one sentence, and check it is true of your own delivery.
4. Attach the consequence to the claim, in the buyer's terms such as legal exposure, refunds or downtime.
5. Put that claim in the article as a named tradeoff section rather than a passing line.
6. Support it with a concrete failure case the automated or rival approach produces.
7. Reuse the same position across every article in the set so the site reads as one argument.
8. Drop the angle if a competitor could truthfully say the same thing.

**Pitfall:** A contrarian angle that your own delivery does not actually support reads as marketing and collapses on the sales call. It also fails if the ranking pages are mixed, because there is no single assumption to argue against.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau comparison pages, David should note what the ranking pages sell. Where they are generic scheduling tools, the argument Pabau can make and they cannot is clinical record keeping and compliance inside the same system as bookings.

**Apply anywhere:** Check who owns the pages ranking for your bottom-of-funnel terms and what they sell. Find the claim their business model prevents them from making, verify it is true of your delivery, attach the buyer-level consequence, and build every article in the set on that same position.

### 10. Take the contrarian angle when every page-one result agrees  `100.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 100*

Grow and Convert classify the contrarian viewpoint as light originality because most topics have easily identifiable opposing angles. Their example: every post about hiring marketers listed things to consider, compared options and gave tips. Nobody had written 'hiring marketers isn't easy and here are the reasons why.' Benji wrote that angle and the piece ranks #4 for 'hiring marketing' plus a spread of long-tail keywords around hiring marketers. They argue that taking a contrarian view to what everyone else says in the search results demonstrates expertise on the subject and, in their experience, helps you rank. The work is small: read what the SERP agrees on, then argue the other side honestly. It only fails if you have no real basis for the disagreement.

> "take an opposing viewpoint to what everyone else is writing about"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's hiring-marketers post ranks #4 for 'hiring marketing' and for various long-tail keywords around hiring marketers, on an angle no competing result had taken.

**How to do it**

1. List the shared assumption running through all 10 page-one results in one sentence.
2. Ask whether your own experience contradicts that assumption in any way.
3. Write the contrarian claim as a plain sentence and check you can support it with at least two real examples.
4. Put the contrarian claim in the title and the first paragraph.
5. Devote a section to why the common advice fails, naming the situations where it does.
6. Concede where the consensus is right so the piece does not read as contrarian for its own sake.
7. Track long-tail rankings around the topic, not just the head term, since the angle usually pulls those first.

**Pitfall:** Contrarianism without evidence reads as a hot take and gets no links. The signal is that your supporting sections are opinion restated rather than examples, data or first-hand accounts.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's aesthetic-practice content has obvious consensus positions worth testing, such as advice to discount to fill the diary. David can commission one contrarian piece per quarter, supported by real practice data, rather than another agreeing guide.

**Apply anywhere:** When every page-one result repeats the same advice, write the honest opposing case instead. Support it with two or more concrete examples, and concede where the consensus holds.

### 11. Test a proposed title against whether the named decision-maker would read it  `182.5`
*core · content insights · source 182*

Grow and Convert give a specific test for whether a B2B topic is too basic, using the example of a company selling an IT product to CTOs and CIOs and a proposed piece titled '10 tips to improve your B2B tech stack'. They ask what veteran CTO reads that, then give four reasons: no time for generic pieces, they already know more than the writer, their feeds and inboxes are already full of titles like it, and the beginner-level topic forces beginner-level content inside. The last reason is the load-bearing one. A title pitched at a beginner constrains what the article can contain, so even a reader who clicks is not impressed enough to start a trial or request a demo. The failure is not the writing quality; it is the level the topic sets.

> "what veteran CTO is going to read that"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert draw the example from B2B SaaS clients selling to chief technology and information officers.

**How to do it**

1. Name the exact job title that has to sign off on your purchase, not a persona label.
2. Read each proposed title aloud as if that person received it in a newsletter, and cut any that they would already know.
3. Check whether the title implies beginner content; if it does, the article cannot recover later.
4. Rewrite survivors so the specificity in the title is something the decision-maker could not already have.
5. Confirm the topic touches the product decision, not just the reader's general interests.
6. Kill any 'X tips to improve your Y' formulation aimed at a senior buyer.

**Pitfall:** The article can be well written, well researched and still fail this test, because the level was fixed by the topic before drafting. The signal is high pageviews with no demo requests from the piece.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's buyer is a practice owner or clinic manager who runs the business daily. Any title along the lines of basic tips for running a clinic fails this test; the topic has to assume they already do the job.

**Apply anywhere:** Before approving a title, read it as the specific job title that signs your contracts and ask whether they would learn anything. Beginner titles lock the article into beginner content.

### 12. Test a suspect sentence by deleting it and rereading the passage  `72.11`
*core · best practices · source 72*

Khanal names this as a technique rather than an instinct. When a sentence gives him a nagging feeling, the first thing he tests is not having it at all: remove it, read the surrounding passage as a whole, and see how it feels. If the passage reads more focused without it, the sentence stays deleted. If something is lost, undo and put it straight back. His phrasing is that if you read it back and think 'oh, this kind of whatever', it means you did not need it. He applies it live to a line he had just written, 'it's a matter of substance, not style', decides it is too cheesy and repeats the previous sentence, and cuts it even though he liked writing it.

> "just remove it and read it together"

**Evidence:** Khanal cut his own line 'it's a matter of substance, not style' on air after applying the test, judging it a restatement of the sentence before it.

**How to do it**

1. When a sentence nags at you, delete it before trying to rewrite it.
2. Read the paragraph from the start without it, out loud if possible.
3. Ask whether the passage feels more focused or whether something is genuinely missing.
4. If nothing is missing, leave it deleted; if it is, undo immediately and keep the original.
5. Then ask separately whether the sentence repeats a point you already made.
6. Allow a deliberate repeat only where you want emphasis, and cut it otherwise.
7. Repeat the test on every sentence you wrote because it sounded good rather than because it added something.

**Pitfall:** Trying to rewrite a sentence you are unsure about. Khanal's point is that rewriting keeps a line you did not need; deletion is the faster test and usually the answer.

**Apply at Pabau:** Build the delete-and-reread test into the Pabau editorial pass. On any article where sentences feel padded, remove the suspect line first and only restore it if the paragraph loses something.

**Apply anywhere:** When a sentence nags at you, delete it and reread the paragraph before trying to rewrite it, and restore it only if the passage actually lost something.

### 13. Test added personality by asking whether it adds a detail or repeats  `71.7`
*core · best practices · source 71*

Devesh says something unexpected about writing in the AI era. Because Claude and ChatGPT have pushed everyone toward the same clipped style, he is now less shy about being a little more wordy, since that wordiness is where color and personality live. But he sets a hard test to stop it becoming padding. When you add color to AI writing, ask yourself whether you are saying the same thing in other words, or adding a useful detail or example. Only the second earns the words. He points at Grow and Convert's own post, 'The detail principle for writing good blog posts', as the fuller argument. During the session he applied the test on himself, checking mid-edit whether he was adding personality or just being wordy, and concluded he was on the right side of the line because the addition carried a new specific.

> "am I just saying the same thing in other words"

**Evidence:** Devesh cites Grow and Convert's 'The detail principle for writing good blog posts' as the underlying argument and applied the test live mid-edit to a sentence he had just expanded.

**How to do it**

1. Draft with the AI, then edit for personality rather than accepting the model's clipped default style.
2. For every sentence you add or lengthen, ask whether it repeats an existing claim in different words.
3. Keep the addition only if it introduces a new detail, a concrete example, or a named comparison.
4. Delete the addition if it only re-emphasizes, no matter how well it reads.
5. Apply the same test to human drafts, since Devesh says human writing fails it more often than AI writing does.
6. Accept that some sentences will stay imperfect, and stop editing rather than rewriting every line from scratch.

**Pitfall:** Deciding to 'add human-ness' without the test produces padding. Devesh's warning is that you cannot just add a bunch of nonsense and call it voice; you have to be good at it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's rule against AI-flat prose should be paired with this test, so writers adding color to a draft on a clinical or software topic add a named example or a specific figure rather than another adjective.

**Apply anywhere:** When you add voice to an AI draft, keep only the additions that carry a new detail or example. Everything else is padding, and the same test catches padding in human drafts.

### 14. Test writers on three product-copywriting abilities, not general writing skill  `179.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 179*

Grow and Convert argue that ranking for buying-intent keywords forces you to write about products and services in depth, covering features, use cases and differentiators. They point at the SERP for a category term like "accounting software", which is filled with product lists and comparisons rather than general accounting advice, so competing there means discussing and comparing products. They then name the three specific abilities this requires: discussing your unique features and benefits in terms of the customer pain points they solve, communicating competitive advantages and differentiators without being aggressive or trashing competitors, and selling without being salesy. They are blunt that this is a distinct skill set that is hard to find, and that even strong writers often lack it. Most agencies substitute a brief that dictates subheaders copied from ranking pages and expect the freelancer to self-research the rest.

> "Communicate your competitive advantages and differentiators"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run an extensive writer training process built over years to produce a team of product copywriters, because they could not hire the skill off the shelf.

**How to do it**

1. Search your top category keyword and note whether page one is product lists and comparisons or general advice; if it is products, product copywriting is a hard requirement.
2. Give every writer candidate a paid test on one real comparison or category keyword, not a general topic.
3. Score the draft on ability one: does each feature appear alongside the customer pain point it solves, rather than as a spec list.
4. Score ability two: does it state a differentiator against a named competitor without disparaging that competitor.
5. Score ability three: does it sell without reading as sales copy, judged by whether you would send it to a prospect.
6. Reject drafts whose subheaders are the ranking pages' subheaders in a different order.
7. Give writers who pass the interview recordings and support tickets that supply the differentiator detail, and train the ones you keep.

**Pitfall:** Hiring on writing samples alone. Grow and Convert say even great writers often lack these abilities, so a strong portfolio of general articles predicts nothing about whether the writer can sell a product without sounding salesy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's writers and any freelancers should be tested on a live comparison keyword, writing about a named competitor without disparaging it and tying each Pabau feature to a practice's actual pain point. That is the exact skill the alternatives and comparison pages need.

**Apply anywhere:** Check whether page one for your category keyword is product lists and comparisons. If it is, hire for product copywriting specifically. Test candidates on a real comparison keyword and score three things: features tied to the pain points they solve, differentiators stated against a named competitor without trashing them, and selling that does not read as sales copy. General writing samples do not predict any of these.

### 16. The publishing volume ceiling is 50-100 articles a month, humanised or not  `57.10`
*core · content insights · source 57*

Jackie's most concrete finding from running sites deliberately into penalties: the sites where he pushes fewer articles are less likely to get hit. He says you can no longer do thousands of articles a month, and puts the current sweet spot at 100-200 a month maximum, with 50-100 a month being safe. The important qualifier is that humanising doesn't change it - 'if you humanise a thousand articles a month you still get hit', where humanising means a real person rewriting, editing and formatting. His diagnosis is that it isn't about detectability, it's that the volume itself is too spammy. He timestamps the finding and warns that it's subject to change. Cody's process is compatible: take what's ranking, put it in the context window, write on top of it, have a human polish, then publish and index.

> "sites where I push fewer articles are less likely to get hit"

**How to do it**

1. Set a monthly publishing cap and treat it as a constraint rather than a target. His safe band is 50-100 articles a month; 100-200 is the maximum he'd attempt.
2. Don't buy extra volume with human editing. He is specific: humanise a thousand articles a month - a real person rewriting, editing, formatting - and you still get hit, because the volume itself is the signal.
3. Ramp rather than step. If you are moving from 10 to 80 a month, get there over a quarter.
4. Keep every piece inside the orbit of the product, since relevance is what carries volume in both practitioners' accounts.
5. Date-stamp every cadence change in a log so any traffic movement can be attributed to it rather than guessed at.
6. Prune as you publish: no-index anything that no longer fits, so the indexed count grows more slowly than the published count.
7. Re-test the ceiling every few months rather than treating any number as permanent - he explicitly timestamps the finding and expects it to move.

**Pitfall:** He explicitly dates the finding and says don't message him if you get hit at 50 articles a month - it's an observed threshold from his own portfolio, not a rule.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should stay far below any of these thresholds: at Pabau's publishing volume the risk isn't velocity, and the useful takeaway is the inverse - improving existing pages is the safer way to add coverage.

**Apply anywhere:** Most brand publishers sit far below any of these thresholds, so velocity isn't the risk - the useful takeaway is the inverse: improving existing pages is the safer way to add coverage.

### 17. Treat a claim you cannot back as a signal to cut or re-report it  `99.7`
*core · best practices · source 99*

Khanal gives a clean decision rule for the moment a writer cannot support a claim. If you have no supporting information for a claim, that means you likely do not understand it well enough to be writing about it. The escalation is ordered. If the piece was built on subject matter expert interviews, flag the claim as a follow-up question for that expert. If that is not possible, question whether the claim belongs in the article at all. If neither resolves it, treat it as evidence of a more fundamental problem with the premise: maybe the claim is not valid, maybe the premise does not make sense, maybe nobody involved knows enough about the topic to be writing about it. He extends the same rule to the writer level. If you do not know the answers to the multiple why and how questions behind a step, it means you should not be writing the post.

> "That means you likely don't understand it enough to be writing about it"

**Evidence:** Khanal's escalation rule from editing client pieces and evaluating freelance test drafts.

**How to do it**

1. When a claim has no backup, write it on a separate follow-up list rather than papering over it.
2. Send that list back to the subject matter expert as specific follow-up questions, not as a general review request.
3. If no expert is available, delete the claim and check whether the section still holds together without it.
4. If deleting the claim breaks the section, treat the premise itself as suspect.
5. Count how many claims in the piece landed on the follow-up list.
6. If several core claims are unbackable, stop the draft and re-scope or re-source the article rather than shipping it.
7. Record the unbackable claims so the same topic is not commissioned again from the same thin source.

**Pitfall:** The default response is to hedge the claim with softeners so it survives review. That keeps a claim in the article that nobody in the process can defend, and a knowledgeable reader spots it immediately.

**Apply at Pabau:** In the Pabau fact-check pass, an unsupported claim should route to a follow-up question for the internal expert or get cut, rather than being softened. If several claims in one article fail, re-scope the article instead of publishing.

**Apply anywhere:** In your fact-check pass, route an unsupported claim to a follow-up question for your internal expert or cut it. If several fail in one article, re-scope the piece rather than softening the claims.

### 18. Treat a stalled ranking and a slipping ranking as two different problems  `119.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 119*

Grow and Convert separate two monitoring signals that most teams lump together. A stalled ranking is a page that climbed page one and then stopped, for example one that will not push past position #5. A slipping ranking is a page losing position over many weeks. Their read is that stalling is not automatically an intent problem: the content may already match the SERP and the missing ingredient is authority, so link building is as likely a fix as a rewrite. A steady decline, by contrast, they treat as a near-certain sign the content no longer matches search intent, because the SERP itself has moved. Backlink loss can contribute, but their experience points to intent first. The practical consequence is that you diagnose before you rewrite, otherwise you spend a full content update on a page that needed links.

> "isn't pushing past position #5"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's monitoring rules, drawn from running content programs where a page plateaued around positions #9 and #10 before dipping.

**How to do it**

1. Tag every tracked page in Ahrefs Rank Tracker as climbing, stalled or slipping, using the rank history dropdown rather than the current position.
2. Call a page stalled when it has held the same band, for example positions 4 to 6, for four weeks or more with no downward trend.
3. Call a page slipping only when the decline runs across many weeks or months, not a single week.
4. For a slipping page, run SERP analysis first and plan a content update against the new intent.
5. For a stalled page, compare referring domains to the pages ranking above it before touching the copy.
6. If the pages above have materially more referring domains, route the page to link building instead of a rewrite.
7. If referring domains are comparable, treat the stall as a depth or format gap and update the content.
8. Re-check the page four weeks after whichever action you chose and record which lever moved it.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Rewriting a stalled page that already matches intent. You burn a writer's week, the position does not move, and you conclude updates do not work when the real constraint was authority.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should split Pabau's tracked-keyword list into stalled and slipping before commissioning any refresh. Aesthetic-practice guides parked at position 4 or 5 are usually a link problem, while older feature-led posts sliding down are intent problems worth a rewrite.

**Apply anywhere:** Split your tracked pages into stalled and slipping before you commission any refresh. A page frozen mid-page-one usually needs links, while a page in steady multi-week decline usually needs a rewrite against the current SERP.

### 19. Treat content aimed at the wrong customer segment as mirage too  `97.7`
*core · content insights · source 97*

In a January 2017 update, Grow and Convert widen the definition. Mirage content also comes from not understanding your customers in depth. Companies jump to brainstorming ideas they think will interest their audience without first working out who their best customers are. The result looks like it will attract the right people but slightly misses the mark. Their example is an in-app referral software company writing about the benefits of referral marketing for growing mobile app downloads. After the customer work, the ideal customer turned out to be enterprise companies with 50,000-plus downloads who already knew the benefits and simply lacked the resources to build a referral solution in-house. The content was a mirage for that buyer. It can still pull traffic, because it serves an audience, just not yours, and the complaint that follows is always about lead quality.

> "but that slightly misses the mark"

**Evidence:** An in-app referral software client whose benefits-of-referral-marketing content missed an enterprise ICP with 50,000-plus app downloads.

**How to do it**

1. Define your best customers by revenue and retention before planning any topics, not by who currently reads the blog.
2. Write down what that segment already knows, so you stop publishing benefits-of-the-category posts to people past that stage.
3. Write down what that segment lacks, which is usually resources or a specific capability, and build topics around that gap.
4. Test every planned topic with one question: would our best customer find this valuable, not would anyone in this market find it valuable.
5. Segment traffic reports by whether visitors match the ICP, rather than reading total sessions.
6. Kill or repoint topics that only serve the smaller, cheaper end of the market.

**Pitfall:** This failure is invisible in traffic reports because the posts do get traffic. The signal is a marketing team reporting growth while sales complains the leads are the wrong size of company.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's best customers are established aesthetic and healthcare practices, not people deciding whether to open one. David should cut or repoint articles explaining why practices need software and write for operators who already accept that.

**Apply anywhere:** Define your best customer segment before planning topics, then kill any post that explains benefits that segment already accepts, even if it attracts traffic.

### 20. Treat page 2-4 rankings as zero and rebuild with dedicated pages  `112.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 112*

Grow and Convert are blunt about partial rankings in the Truwild example. Looking at the terms where the hydration product page ranks on pages 2 to 4 of search results, they note in passing that you get essentially zero conversions ranking on pages 2-4, and that it is not much different from not ranking at all. The important move is what they do with that list. Rather than treating those terms as nearly-won and trying to nudge the product page up, they treat them as evidence that the product page is the wrong page for those queries. Terms like 'hydration powder', 'hydration drink mix' and 'hydrate supplement' all describe exactly what the company sells, but a general product page may never rank for the variations, so each gets its own dedicated or list-style page instead.

> "you will get essentially zero conversions when ranking on pages 2-4"

**Evidence:** Truwild's hydration product page ranked on pages 2-4 for hydration powder, hydration drink mix and hydrate supplement, all terms Grow and Convert say the page may never win.

**How to do it**

1. Export the queries where a product or category page ranks between positions 11 and 40.
2. Filter to the ones that clearly describe a product you sell, discarding informational strays.
3. For each, look at the top 10 and note whether the ranking pages are dedicated pages or general product pages.
4. Where the winners are dedicated pages, stop optimizing the product page and schedule a new page for that query.
5. Where the query wants options rather than one product, build a list-style post instead of a single-product page.
6. Interlink the new page from the product page so the existing authority reaches it.
7. Recheck positions after two to three months and retire any duplicate targeting you have created.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Treating page-two rankings as almost-there wastes months on a page that structurally cannot satisfy the query. The signal is repeated on-page edits that move the position a few places and never past ten.

**Apply at Pabau:** This contradicts the common striking-distance rule of optimizing positions 11-20 first. For Pabau, David should split the striking-distance list in two: queries where the right page is already ranking get an on-page refresh, and queries where the SERP is full of dedicated pages get a new page instead of another edit.

**Apply anywhere:** Positions 11 to 40 produce close to nothing, so do not treat them as nearly won. Check whether the SERP is held by dedicated pages; if it is, your general product page will not get there and the answer is a new page, not another round of on-page edits.

### 21. Treat the unique angle as the thing the whole outreach depends on  `162.14`
*core · best practices · source 162*

Peralta closes by saying the process hinges on step two, finding a unique angle, and flags it mid-article as the one section to read if you read nothing else. He gives two failure modes if you skip it. First, the pitch goes generic, so the expert is far less likely to agree. Second, and less obvious, even when you do land the interview the story itself is generic and produces poor results. That makes the angle the load-bearing part of both the outreach and the content, not an outreach trick. He also notes it costs about five minutes per target, so the excuse for skipping it is weak. The rule follows: no angle, no pitch, and no interview booked on a topic you cannot already describe as a specific unanswered question.

> "it all hinges on Step 2: finding a unique angle"

**Evidence:** Peralta names step two as the make-or-break of a process with a 60% positive response rate, and says a generic angle produces a generic story even when the interview happens.

**How to do it**

1. Write the angle down as a single specific question before contacting anyone.
2. Search that exact question and confirm no existing article answers it.
3. Reject any angle you could ask of five different companies without changing a word.
4. Budget five minutes of research per target for this and do not batch it.
5. If no angle emerges for a target, drop the target rather than sending a generic pitch.
6. Carry the same angle wording into the tweet, the email, the follow-ups and the article headline.
7. Judge the finished article by whether the angle still drives it, not by word count.

**Pitfall:** Landing the interview with a generic angle and assuming the writing stage will save it. It will not; the piece reads like everything else on the SERP and gets no traction.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau interview request should carry a one-line angle that no competing article answers, and the same line should become the article's H1 promise so the piece is not just another treatment overview.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the angle as one specific unanswered question before you pitch, confirm nothing already answers it, and drop the target rather than sending a generic ask.

### 22. Trigger every content update from a rank monitor, not a calendar  `119.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 119*

Grow and Convert argue there is no magic interval for updating website content. The trigger should be slipping or stalled rankings, found by monitoring, not an arbitrary schedule. They run rank tracking as a standing process: keywords from the content plan go into Ahrefs Rank Tracker, which shows the ranking page, the current position, and the full rank history in a dropdown. A decline or a plateau is the signal to open the page. If nothing has moved down, they leave the post alone. This differs from the base's usual refresh advice in that the monitoring layer, not judgment about staleness, decides the queue. They note both patterns matter: a page climbing then stalling, and a page steadily losing position over weeks or months.

> "the trigger should be slipping/stalled rankings"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish and maintain SEO content for clients as their business, and say monitoring is the most efficient approach they have found.

**How to do it**

1. List every target keyword from the content plan with the URL meant to rank for it.
2. Load that keyword set into Ahrefs Rank Tracker and let it collect a baseline for a few weeks.
3. Open the Trends tab and read each keyword's rank history, not just today's position.
4. Flag any keyword that has fallen over several consecutive weeks, not a single week's wobble.
5. Flag any keyword that climbed onto page one and then plateaued for weeks.
6. Leave every keyword that is stable or still climbing untouched.
7. Queue only the flagged URLs for a SERP intent check before writing anything.
8. Re-read the tracker a month after each update to confirm the page recovered.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Reacting to one bad week. Rankings fluctuate normally, and Grow and Convert stress the decline must run over many weeks or months before it counts as a signal.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should track every target keyword for /blog/, /templates/ and the code-reference pages in a rank tracker and let that list generate the refresh queue. David should stop scheduling library-wide refreshes by publication age.

**Apply anywhere:** Put your target keywords into a rank tracker and let sustained declines or plateaus decide which pages get rewritten. Age alone is not a reason to touch a page that still ranks.

### 23. Turn a how-to keyword into a tool that delivers the outcome  `75.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 75*

Working the keyword "retention rate formula", the author argues the searcher does not want the formula, they want their retention rate. So the winning page explains the formula briefly to build confidence, then hands over an interactive calculator or a downloadable Excel template that produces the number. He cites Qualtrics' article on calculating Net Promoter Score as the model, because its embedded calculator explains the formula while delivering the outcome in the same place. He also reads expertise from the query: someone who has to look up the formula does not work in retention, so the page must stay simple and reassuring rather than exhaustive. The structure he recommends is a digestible landing page covering credentials, the inputs to the formula and the common questions, with the calculator underneath.

> "A problem statement removes the solution (the formula)"

**Evidence:** Qualtrics' NPS article, which the author names as particularly good because its calculator explains the formula while delivering the outcome.

**How to do it**

1. List your keywords whose intent ends in a number, a score or a rate the reader must compute.
2. For each one write the outcome, for example know our retention rate, not know the formula.
3. Explain the formula in plain terms in the first screen so the reader trusts the output.
4. Name the inputs the formula needs, one line each, so the reader can gather them before starting.
5. Build an interactive calculator on the page, or ship a downloadable spreadsheet template if you cannot build one.
6. Put the calculator directly below the explanation rather than at the end of a long article.
7. Keep the reading level low, assuming the reader does not work in this discipline day to day.
8. Answer the two or three follow-up questions a first-time calculator gets, such as what counts as a churned customer.

**Tools:** Microsoft Excel

**Pitfall:** Publishing the formula and stopping there. The reader leaves to find a calculator elsewhere, and the page loses to any competitor that completes the job on the page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has several calculable metrics worth a tool page: no-show rate, average revenue per patient, rebooking rate, chair utilization. Build one calculator page per metric, with the formula, the inputs and an embedded calculator, rather than another formula explainer in /blog/.

**Apply anywhere:** For any keyword whose intent ends in a computed number, build the calculator rather than only the explanation. Explain the formula briefly, name the inputs, then embed a calculator or ship a spreadsheet template on the same page.
