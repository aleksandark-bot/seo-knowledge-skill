### 1. Treat Clearscope as the last practical step, after strategy and sourcing  `128.13`
*useful · best practices · source 128*

Grow and Convert's on-page process is short and deliberately positioned. After the keyword decision, the specificity angle, the expert interview and the detail work, they say that finally, on a practical level, they make sure their on-page SEO is sound using Clearscope. The ordering carries the argument. On-page tooling is a check for coverage and term usage at the end, not an input to what the article should be. Nothing about their three content strategies comes from a Clearscope report, and the report cannot supply an angle, an expert or a claim's supporting detail. The practical use is to catch terms the ranking pages all cover that your interview-led piece happened to skip, which is a real gap and a cheap fix at that stage.

> "we make sure our on-page SEO is sound using Clearscope"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list Clearscope last, after keyword strategy, specificity, expert interviews and the detail principle, as the practical on-page check.

**How to do it**

1. Choose the keyword and the specific angle before opening any on-page tool.
2. Run the expert interview and write the draft from it.
3. Only then paste the finished draft into Clearscope for the target keyword.
4. Read the report for terms and subtopics the ranking pages cover that your draft genuinely omits.
5. Add the missing subtopics as real sections with real content, not as term mentions.
6. Ignore suggested terms that would force you off your specific angle back toward an overview.
7. Recheck the grade after edits and stop once coverage is sound rather than chasing the top grade.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Writing from the on-page report instead of checking against it. The report describes what already ranks, so drafting from it reproduces the average of page one and erases the angle that would have won.

**Apply at Pabau:** In Pabau's optimization flow, the on-page term check should run on the finished draft only, and any suggested term that pulls the article toward a generic overview should be ignored.

**Apply anywhere:** Run on-page term tools on the finished draft, never as the drafting brief. Use the report only to catch subtopics the ranking pages cover that your piece genuinely skipped, and ignore suggestions that would drag a specific angle back toward a generic overview.

### 2. Treat department disagreements about the customer as the best topic source  `114.13`
*useful · best practices · source 114*

In the cross-department research session, Grow and Convert take notes on every answer, then look for two things: commonalities and disconnects. Commonalities are the obvious output, the repeated use cases and questions that become articles. The disconnects are the part most teams skip. When sales names a different ideal customer than support, or product names a most-used feature that sales never mentions, they push the departments to explain why they see it differently. That conversation usually exposes either a positioning problem or a segment nobody is writing for, and both are content opportunities. Their own example of the output is a set of three plain problem statements about their clients: blog post ideas that do not attract the right customer, no strategy for driving traffic, and a blog that converts nobody. Each of those became a body of content on their site.

> "look for commonalities and disconnects in the responses"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert reduce their own session output to three client problem statements, each of which anchors a body of content on their site.

**How to do it**

1. After the session, build one table with a row per question and a column per department.
2. Mark every row where two departments gave materially different answers.
3. Bring the disagreeing people back together and ask each to explain what they are seeing that the other is not.
4. Write down which customer segment or use case each side is describing.
5. Check whether either segment has any content aimed at it today.
6. Turn each unwritten segment into a dedicated page rather than a section in a general article.
7. Reduce the commonalities to three or four plain problem statements written in customer language.
8. Map each problem statement to a keyword and check the SERP intent before commissioning.

**Pitfall:** Teams resolve disconnects by picking whoever is most senior, which buries the segment that nobody serves. The signal is a session output that agrees with the existing positioning deck on every point.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau's sales, onboarding and support describe different ideal practices, that gap is likely a page that does not exist yet, such as content for multi-location groups or for injectable-only practices. David should build that page rather than reconciling the views on paper.

**Apply anywhere:** Tabulate the research session by question and department, mark every disagreement, and make the disagreeing people explain what the other is missing. Each unserved segment that falls out becomes its own page, not a paragraph in a general one.

### 3. Treat marketplaces and job boards as unfiltered pools needing your own screen  `164.10`
*useful · best practices · source 164*

Grow and Convert rank their writer sourcing channels from six years of trying all of them. Marketplaces such as Upwork, Fiverr and Crowd Content may contain a small number of high quality writers, but those are harder to find and the platforms apply no strict filter of their own, so you must supply the whole screen. General job boards such as Remote OK, Craigslist and Wellfound, formerly AngelList Talent, are hit and miss; dedicated writing boards such as ProBlogger worked better for them. Social media postings on LinkedIn, Facebook or Twitter can produce useful referrals, but the yield depends entirely on whether your own network contains writers, which for a content agency it does and for a product company often does not. Their closing rule is that the sourcing channel matters less than the evaluation process.

> "they don't have a strict filter for freelancers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert tried every popular sourcing route over six years and found dedicated writing boards outperformed general boards, with marketplaces weakest.

**How to do it**

1. Rank your channels by yield rather than by convenience: on-site link, referrals, dedicated writing boards, general boards, marketplaces.
2. Post to ProBlogger or an equivalent writing-specific board before a general job board.
3. On marketplaces, assume zero platform filtering and apply your full three-filter process to every applicant.
4. Post on social media only if your own following plausibly contains writers or people who work with them.
5. Ask for referrals explicitly in the social post rather than only for applicants.
6. Tag every applicant with its source and review channel yield after each hiring round.

**Tools:** Upwork, Fiverr, ProBlogger, Wellfound

**Pitfall:** Relying on marketplace ratings or badges as a quality filter. Those platforms do not screen for the kind of writing you need, so a five-star profile tells you about responsiveness, not about product copywriting.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau posts a writer listing, use ProBlogger and the on-site page first. Treat Upwork as a last resort and run the full mini-sample screen on anyone from there.

**Apply anywhere:** Rank sourcing channels by yield: your own site link and team referrals first, dedicated writing boards next, general boards after that, marketplaces last. Marketplaces apply no quality filter, so you supply all of it.

### 4. Use a blog post, not a landing page, for high-intent keywords  `143.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 143*

Grow and Convert close by saying they have three examples of outranking landing pages with blog posts, and they argue the blog post is usually the better format for a high buying intent keyword. Their reasoning is twofold. Ranking is easier because the format matches what already ranks on comparison and best-of style SERPs, which are dominated by article-shaped pages. And they believe the blog post may convert better even in the scenario where the landing page outranks it, because the article can do the comparison work the searcher came for while still making the product argument. The practical implication is that when a commercial term is contested, the answer is not always a new landing page in the product section of the site.

> "3 examples of us outranking landing pages with blog posts"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite three of their own examples of blog posts outranking landing pages for high-intent keywords.

**How to do it**

1. Search the target commercial keyword and count how many of the top 10 are landing pages versus articles.
2. If articles dominate, brief the target as a blog post even though the term is commercial.
3. Give the post the comparison structure the SERP shows: options, criteria, who each suits.
4. Argue your own product inside that structure at the same depth as the alternatives.
5. Keep the SEO title, H1 and URL on the exact keyword, as with any dedicated page.
6. If a landing page already targets the term and sits below page one, publish the article and link the two rather than competing.
7. Track conversions on the article separately so you can compare it against the landing page's rate.

**Pitfall:** Publishing an article for a term whose SERP is entirely product and pricing pages wastes the slot. Check the format mix before choosing, not after.

**Apply at Pabau:** For contested terms like practice management software comparisons, David should check whether the SERP is article-shaped before commissioning a new Pabau landing page. Where articles dominate, a blog post that argues the product is the stronger bet.

**Apply anywhere:** Check the format mix on a commercial SERP before deciding between a landing page and an article. Where articles dominate, publish a blog post that carries the comparison and makes the product argument inside it.

### 5. Use all three promotion channels, and let SEO carry the long term  `102.17`
*useful · best practices · source 102*

Grow and Convert divide promotion into three categories and say the most effective strategies use all three: promoting to your existing audience, promoting through paid channels, and promoting through SEO. Each does a different job. Audience promotion is fast and cheap but produces a spike with no tail. Paid buys conversions during the wait for rankings but costs per click forever. SEO is the only one that pulls traffic and conversions over the long term without an incremental cost per click, and they split it into two parts: optimizing the content itself, and link building, which they define as outreach or paid outreach services to acquire backlinks from other domains. The framing is useful because it stops teams treating promotion as a single publish-day task.

> "The most effective content strategies leverage all 3"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say the most effective content strategies use all three categories, with SEO as the only one producing long-term traffic without per-click cost.

**How to do it**

1. Write the three promotion channels as separate lines in the content plan, each with its own owner and budget.
2. On publish day, run only the audience promotion, and log the date.
3. Start the paid campaign for high-intent pieces in the same week, sized to a small test budget.
4. Run the SEO work as two tracks: the on-page optimization at publish, and link acquisition over the following months.
5. For link building, decide whether outreach is done in-house or bought as a service, and record the cost per link.
6. Review each channel separately at 90 days rather than judging the piece on a single blended traffic number.

**Pitfall:** Collapsing promotion into publish-day sharing. Sites that do this get a launch spike, no links, and pages that never reach page one for competitive terms.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat link acquisition for its strongest bottom-funnel articles as a standing line in the content plan, not something that happens only when a piece stalls.

**Apply anywhere:** Plan promotion as three separate lines with their own owners: audience promotion at publish, paid ads during the ranking wait, and SEO with an ongoing link acquisition track. Review each at 90 days rather than blending them.

### 6. Use the Clearscope word count as the gap size for a thin post  `139.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 139*

Brandon used Clearscope to decide both what a rewritten page should cover and how long it should be. Clearscope scans the top ten results for the target keyword, calculates which terms appear and how important each is, and reports an estimated word count. On the receipts post, the existing page was about 800 words and Clearscope recommended 1,900, so he added 1,100 words of topically relevant material rather than padding. He is explicit that the term list is not a stuffing checklist. It is a list of topics the ranking pages cover, and the job is to satisfy the search intent by covering them. His framing of the mechanism is that Google no longer scores strictly exact keywords; it reads a page holistically and uses the knowledge graph to judge topical relevance, so synonyms and related concepts carry weight.

> "Since Clearscope recommended 1,900 words"

**Evidence:** Keeper Tax receipts post: 800 words expanded to roughly 1,900 on Clearscope's recommendation.

**How to do it**

1. Run the target keyword through Clearscope and note the recommended word count and the ranked term list.
2. Count the current page's words and treat the difference as the size of the content gap.
3. Read the term list top-down and convert the highest-importance terms into subheadings or paragraphs.
4. Write the missing sections as real answers, not sentences built to contain a phrase.
5. Re-run the Clearscope grade after editing and stop once the grade lifts, not once every term is used.
6. Keep exact-match placement in the URL, title, H1 and headers, since those still matter.
7. Reject any term the topic does not genuinely need rather than forcing it in.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Treating the Clearscope list as a checklist produces keyword-stuffed prose that reads badly and does not lift the grade meaningfully. The list is a topic map, not a word quota.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau refreshes a thin /blog/ post, run the target keyword through a SERP term tool first and treat the recommended length gap as the brief for what is missing, not as a word count to pad to.

**Apply anywhere:** Run the target keyword through a SERP term tool, treat the gap between its recommended word count and your page's as the content gap, and fill it with the topics the ranking pages cover.

### 7. Use your own brief, not a borrowed hiring process, in the test  `164.19`
*useful · best practices · source 164*

Grow and Convert repeatedly warn against copying their process verbatim. They say not to imitate their exact process, and that you need to give applicants the exact same prompt and level of information you give the writers you currently work with, because you want to see how the candidate would perform in real life. The same logic applies to their screening criteria: they screen for bottom-of-funnel product content because that is what they publish, and they say if you write top-of-funnel or thought leadership, or need a particular voice imitated, you will have your own criteria. The structural parts to copy are multiple filters, an unedited sample, a paid replica test and a feedback round. The contents of each filter must come from your own work.

> "But don't imitate our exact process."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert explicitly tell readers not to imitate their exact process and to use their own prompt and information level instead.

**How to do it**

1. Copy the structure of the funnel: portfolio, unedited mini sample, paid replica test, feedback round, real pieces.
2. Replace their screening questions with the two or three qualities your own published content requires.
3. Use the actual brief format your current writers receive as the test brief, unchanged.
4. Include the same supporting material a working writer would get, no more and no less.
5. Write your own pass and fail examples from your own archive rather than borrowing theirs.
6. Re-derive the criteria whenever your content type changes, for example moving from top-funnel to product content.

**Pitfall:** Adopting a published hiring process wholesale. A test built on someone else's brief measures fit to their content, and the writer you hire will then underperform against your actual working conditions.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's writer test should hand over the same brief format used internally, including the factcheck-flow block requirements, so the test measures fit to how Pabau articles actually get made.

**Apply anywhere:** Copy the structure of a hiring funnel, not its contents. Use your own working brief, your own supporting material and your own pass and fail examples, so the test measures fit to your actual conditions.

### 8. Watch content optimization scores as an early warning ahead of rank drops  `119.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 119*

Grow and Convert offer this as an advanced addition to rank monitoring. Content optimization tools such as Clearscope grade a page on how well it aligns with search intent and with what currently ranks for the keyword. Some of them include a content tracker that monitors those scores over time. Because the grade is computed against the live SERP, it can fall before your ranking does: the top results change, your coverage no longer matches, and the score drops while your position has not yet moved. They present this as a way to get ahead of updates and make changes before performance dips. They mark it as theoretical rather than proven, which is worth keeping in the entry.

> "they may start to decline before rankings"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this as theoretically letting you get ahead of updates; they do not attach a case result to it.

**How to do it**

1. Add every key SEO page to a content tracker in Clearscope or an equivalent on-page tool.
2. Record the grade each page held on the day it last ranked well as its baseline.
3. Set the tracker to re-grade on its regular cadence so the score is measured against the current SERP.
4. Review the tracker monthly and list any page that has dropped a full letter grade from baseline.
5. Cross-check each dropped page against rank data to see whether position has followed yet.
6. Where the grade fell but rank has not, run the SERP intent check early and refresh before the drop lands.
7. Treat a grade fall with no SERP change as a false alarm and leave the page alone.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Chasing the grade as a target in itself. The score moves when the tool's term list shifts, so a fall with no visible change in the ranking results is not a reason to rewrite.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau adopts an on-page scoring tool, put the highest-value /blog/ and /templates/ pages in its tracker so a slipping grade queues a review before Search Console shows the loss.

**Apply anywhere:** Track on-page optimization scores for your most valuable pages. A grade falling before rankings do gives you a head start on the rewrite.

### 9. Why an agency still sells human content - and where it uses AI  `60.11`
*useful · content insights · source 60*

Joe's position on AI content is commercial rather than ideological. FATJOE stays heavily on human writers not because they're against AI content but because the general consensus from clients is that if they're paying for content, they want it human - they'll use tools for their own AI content, but for premium tiers they want a human to look over it. He acknowledges his writers are probably using AI tools substantially, that it's getting harder to detect, and that as long as the output is really good they don't mind. Where they do lean heavily on AI is planning: a content plan service that clusters 100-200 blog posts and shows which are most likely to rank or to have good intent for the brand. The market signal in his answer is the most useful part: sales of their lower-end content have dropped because everyone uses tools now, so the premium tier has to be able to say human content - and he has 40-50 full-time writers to keep in work. His closing view is that it doesn't matter whether an AI wrote it or someone in Birmingham wrote it, as long as the content is good.

> "still very heavily on the human writers"

**How to do it**

1. Separate the two decisions: who writes the draft, and who is accountable for quality.
2. Use AI where it's uncontroversially better - clustering, planning, prioritising 100-200 topics by rank likelihood and intent.
3. Keep human review on anything premium or client-facing, because that's what buyers are actually paying for.
4. Judge output on quality rather than provenance, and stop trying to detect authorship.
5. Expect the low-end content market to keep collapsing as buyers use tools directly.

**Pitfall:** The demand for human content here is a market preference, not a ranking factor - Joe is explicit that quality is what matters, and both hosts optimise for the reader rather than for provenance.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the split is the useful bit: use AI for the planning and clustering layer where it's clearly better, and keep named human accountability on anything that carries clinical or compliance claims.

**Apply anywhere:** The split is the useful bit: use AI for the planning and clustering layer where it's clearly better, and keep named human accountability on anything carrying regulated, clinical or financial claims.

### 10. Write alt text for accessibility, and stop counting it as SEO work  `74.20`
*useful · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid calls image optimization massively overrated and singles out what he calls checklist SEOs who insist every image must be a good image with alt text. His position is that alt text is genuinely valuable for accessibility, for people with visual impairments, and for screen readers. It might help if you are trying to rank images, though he questions how many people actually pursue image rankings. From an SEO point of view he says he simply does not do it. From an accessibility point of view, absolutely do it. He makes the same split on video: you can embed videos from YouTube and then not worry about video optimization at all, since Google will not index the embed as the video source.

> "I really from an SEO point of view just don't do"

**Evidence:** David: alt text is good for accessibility and screen readers, and he does not do it for SEO reasons at all.

**How to do it**

1. Keep writing alt text on every image, and write it describing what the image shows for someone who cannot see it.
2. Move alt text from the SEO checklist to the accessibility checklist so it is judged on the right criteria.
3. Stop keyword-loading alt text; describe the image instead.
4. Do not budget SEO time for image file naming and compression beyond what page speed needs.
5. Only invest in image SEO if image search is a real traffic source for your vertical, checked in GSC's image search filter.
6. Embed video from YouTube rather than self-hosting, since the embed will not be indexed as your video source anyway.

**Tools:** YouTube, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Treating an alt-text audit as an SEO deliverable and reporting completion as a ranking improvement. The value is real but it is accessibility value, and it will not show up in rankings.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's image captions and alt text stay mandatory as a house rule for accessibility and reader comprehension. David should just not count that work as an SEO improvement or expect ranking movement from it.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep alt text mandatory as an accessibility requirement, describe the image rather than keyword-loading it, and stop reporting it as SEO progress.

### 11. Write posts that tie back to the product before monetizing them  `148.11`
*useful · best practices · source 148*

Shour's precondition for the whole tactic is that a solid content strategy produces posts that tie back to the product in some way. SnackNation sells healthy snack delivery to offices, and its top performing post is a large list of employee engagement ideas, because the company believes snacks raise engagement. One of the experts quoted in that post makes the same connection and backs it with data, which gives the argument third-party support the offer page can lean on. That editorial choice is what makes message match possible later. Where the connection is thin, as with the employee recognition post, the offer page underperforms and no amount of page design fixes it. So the product link is decided at the brief stage, not at the conversion stage.

> "most of what you write ties back to your product in some way"

**Evidence:** SnackNation's employee engagement post, with a contributing expert supporting the snacks-engagement link with data, feeds the 10.52% page; the weaker recognition link feeds the 7.59% page.

**How to do it**

1. At the brief stage, write the one sentence connecting the planned article to what you sell.
2. Reject or reframe the topic if that sentence needs a stretch to be true.
3. Plan an expert contribution or a data point that supports the connection inside the article.
4. Quote that expert in the article body so the claim is not only yours.
5. Reuse the same connection sentence later as the bridge on the offer page.
6. Keep a list of topics with strong product links and prioritize those for lead magnets.
7. Send genuinely unconnected awareness topics to the catch-all offer page instead.
8. Review the connection sentence at refresh time and update it if the product has changed.

**Pitfall:** Choosing topics purely on search volume produces traffic that cannot be bridged to an offer. You find out months later when the offer page converts a third below your best one.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau brief should carry a one-line statement of how the topic connects to practice management software. Topics that need a stretch belong in the awareness bucket with no bespoke demo page.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide at the brief stage how each article ties back to what you sell, and get an expert quote supporting that link inside the article. That sentence becomes the bridge on the offer page later.

### 12. Write rough half-finished section notes and let the model fill them  `71.24`
*useful · ai workflows · source 71*

One of the arguments Devesh had written into his own tool and then forgotten is that the writer can give it rough, half-written ideas, even with typos, and it fills in the blanks based on its understanding of the business. The counterpart is that the tool is trained to follow outlines faithfully, so the notes are treated as instructions rather than suggestions. Together those two behaviors define a working mode: you do not write prose and you do not write a vague topic label, you write the argument you want made in whatever shorthand comes out, and the model expands it using the business context it already holds. During the session this is visibly what happened, with body-section ideas plus the reasoning behind each one carried straight through into the draft.

> "You give it rough, half-written ideas, even with typos"

**Evidence:** Devesh's own product copy, which he had forgotten writing, states the tool follows outlines faithfully and expands rough half-written ideas with typos into sections using its understanding of the business.

**How to do it**

1. For each body section, write the actual argument you want made in shorthand, not a heading or a topic label.
2. Include the reasoning behind the section, so the model knows why the point belongs there.
3. Do not clean up typos or grammar in these notes, since the model resolves them.
4. Confirm the tool is configured or prompted to follow the outline faithfully rather than restructure it.
5. Generate the draft and check each section against the note that produced it.
6. Where a section drifted from the note, tighten the note rather than editing the prose, and regenerate that section.

**Tools:** Wave Writer

**Pitfall:** This only works if the model already holds real business context. Give rough notes to a model with no source material and it fills the blanks with generic filler instead of your arguments.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau outlines approved in /generate should carry the argument and its reasoning per section in shorthand, not just H2 labels, since the generator holds the Pabau context needed to expand them.

**Apply anywhere:** Write outline notes as the shorthand argument plus the reasoning for it, typos included, and let a model that already holds your business context expand them. Topic labels alone produce filler.

### 13. Write the content marketing job description around eight named duties  `165.2`
*useful · concrete actions · source 165*

Hyam publishes the job description he uses for a first content marketing manager, and it is specific enough to copy. The responsibilities are: own all content marketing including identifying which customers to target, producing content, and driving traffic and leads; identify and hire freelance writers; analyze the current customer base and talk to customers to find pain points, questions and topics; identify promotion channels and proactively share content there; understand which content targets top, middle and bottom of funnel; optimize posts around high impact keywords; manage the content calendar; measure content performance and report on traffic, conversions and SEO. The experience bar is one to three years managing a blog, editorial team or website, plus case studies or data proving traffic and business growth, working knowledge of WordPress or another CMS, working knowledge of Google Analytics with the ability to report on a blog, and the ability to make decisions off data.

> "Identify and hire freelance writers to produce content"

**Evidence:** Hyam wrote this description from running content operations at three companies plus his own agency.

**How to do it**

1. Open the responsibilities list with ownership of the full funnel, not with 'write articles'.
2. Name hiring and managing freelance writers as an explicit duty so candidates self-select.
3. Include customer interviews as a duty, phrased as finding pain points, questions and topics.
4. Include identifying promotion channels and proactively posting in them.
5. Require the candidate to distinguish top, middle and bottom of funnel content.
6. Set the experience bar at one to three years managing a blog, editorial team or website.
7. Demand case studies or data showing traffic and business metric growth, not writing samples alone.
8. Require working knowledge of your CMS and of Google Analytics reporting on a blog.

**Tools:** WordPress, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** A job description that lists writing first attracts writers, and Hyam's whole argument is that hiring a writer to run content marketing is the number one mistake companies make.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau posts a content role, reuse these eight duties and require case-study evidence of traffic and lead growth. Add one Pabau-specific duty: interviewing practice owners and Pabau's own product and support staff.

**Apply anywhere:** Build the job ad from eight duties — funnel ownership, hiring freelancers, customer interviews, promotion channels, funnel-stage judgement, keyword optimization, calendar, and reporting — and require case-study proof of growth rather than writing samples.

### 14. Write the customer story first, then attach a low-volume keyword  `117.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 117*

Grow and Convert treat patient stories as a third content type alongside pain-point posts and direct service posts, and they invert the usual order for them. The story is conceptualized first, purely as a story, and a search keyword is paired to it afterwards. Telling a great story takes precedence over what search engines want. They still bother with the keyword because some stories do rank: Cognitive FX's piece on a former figure skater whose 14 years of unexplained chronic illness turned out to be concussion-related sits at position #2 for 'figure skating concussion'. Their point is that these pieces are not SEO-motivated, so it is not essential they rank, but a low-volume specific term costs nothing to attach and gives the story a second life in search as well as social.

> "Telling a great story takes precedence over what search engines want"

**Evidence:** 'How 14 Years of Unexplained Chronic Illness Turned Out to Be Concussion-Related' ranks #2 for 'figure skating concussion'.

**How to do it**

1. Collect the customer stories that are genuinely remarkable, not the routine satisfied-customer ones.
2. Write or outline the story first, on its own terms, with no keyword constraint.
3. Once the story exists, find the specific niche term it naturally matches, however low the volume.
4. Work that term into the title and headings without distorting the narrative.
5. Publish it as a story piece and use it in social as well as organic.
6. Do not judge these pieces on rankings; judge them on whether they get shared and cited.

**Pitfall:** Building the story around a keyword from the start flattens it into another SEO post and loses the emotional pull that made it worth publishing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's customer stories should be written as stories, then matched to a narrow term like the practice type or the specific workflow problem, rather than commissioned against a keyword brief.

**Apply anywhere:** Conceptualize customer stories first and attach a keyword afterwards. Pick a specific low-volume term that fits the story, and accept that ranking is a bonus, not the goal.

### 15. Write the meta description last, as a click pitch not a ranking factor  `96.12`
*useful · best practices · source 96*

Grow and Convert leave the meta description to the very end, once the draft is complete. Their stated reason is honest about the uncertainty: there is debate around whether meta descriptions help pages rank, but at the very least they are an SEO copywriting opportunity to pitch potential readers on why they should click the post. So they treat it as ad copy for the SERP rather than as an optimization input. Writing it last also means it can reflect the angle the finished piece actually took, rather than the angle the brief assumed. This sits slightly against other practitioners in the base who argue against investing in meta descriptions at all because Google rewrites them; Grow and Convert accept the low ranking value but keep the effort for the click.

> "they're an SEO copywriting opportunity to pitch potential readers"

**How to do it**

1. Finish and edit the draft before writing any meta description.
2. Reread the finished angle and differentiator, not the original brief.
3. Write the description as a pitch for the click, naming the differentiator.
4. Include the target keyword so it can bold in the SERP, without stuffing.
5. Keep it short enough to survive truncation on mobile.
6. Accept that Google may rewrite it and do not spend more than a few minutes.

**Pitfall:** Writing the description from the brief before the draft exists, so it promises an angle the finished article does not deliver, which raises bounce from the click it wins.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep meta descriptions in the workflow but write them after the article is final, and cap the time spent given Google frequently rewrites them.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the meta description after the draft is done, treat it as click copy rather than a ranking input, and keep the time spent small.

### 16. Write the setup text first, then place the product screenshot  `71.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 71*

When Devesh reached the search-intent section he wanted a screenshot of the tool output, but he stopped himself from dropping it in cold. His instruction was that he wanted there to be some text first, and specifically not a generic lead-in. He wrote the pain-point framing, then placed the screenshot, then continued with more screenshots showing the categorization. He also moved a paragraph that had drifted below the screenshot up above it, because the sequence had broken. The pattern is that each screenshot sits inside an argument: a sentence saying why this part of the output matters, the image, then the consequence. He states the consequence explicitly for the search-intent screenshot, that it lets the writer open the piece inside the reader's mindset.

> "I want there to be some text first"

**Evidence:** Devesh refused to place the search-intent screenshot until he had written specific pain-point text above it, then moved a stray paragraph back above the image to fix the order.

**How to do it**

1. For each product screenshot, write one or two sentences of setup explaining what the reader is about to see and why it matters.
2. Make the setup specific to that screen, not a generic 'here is what it looks like'.
3. Place the screenshot immediately after the setup text.
4. Follow the screenshot with the practical consequence, for example what the reader can now do that they could not before.
5. Reorder any paragraph that ended up below its screenshot back above it, so the sequence reads setup, image, consequence.
6. Use several screenshots through a walkthrough section rather than one composite image.

**Pitfall:** A screenshot dropped in without setup makes the reader work out what they are looking at, and a generic lead-in wastes the slot. The signal is a caption or sentence that would fit any screenshot in the article.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau images already require a caption that says how the feature helps the reader. Pair that with a setup sentence above each screenshot so the sequence runs setup, image, caption stating the benefit.

**Apply anywhere:** Give every product screenshot a specific setup sentence above it and a consequence sentence below it. A generic lead-in wastes the slot and leaves the reader decoding the image.

### 17. Write the test brief to expose fluff openings versus specific positioning  `164.18`
*useful · concrete actions · source 164*

Grow and Convert give the exact contrast they look for in a test project. On a piece about best accounting software for a specific client, a passing answer reads along the lines of most accounting software has X issues which fail to solve Y pain points, and our software solves those pain points in Z way. A failing answer reads like running a business is hard, after you have done HR and sold to customers you sit down after a long day and now you have got to do the books. The first is specific, the second is general fluff. Because they ask for pain points, an introduction and an outline rather than a full article, the introduction is where this contrast shows up fastest, which is why the introduction is one of the three deliverables.

> "The first response is specific. The second is general "fluff" content."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish paired pass and fail introductions for a 'best accounting software' test brief.

**How to do it**

1. Ask for a full introduction as one of the three test deliverables, because openings expose fluff fastest.
2. Write down your pass and fail example openings for the test topic before scoring anyone.
3. Require the pain-points list to name concrete product shortcomings, not emotional states of the reader.
4. Score an introduction as failing if it opens with the reader's day rather than with the problem the product solves.
5. Require the outline to place the product's differentiator in a named section, not in a closing paragraph.
6. Give the same fluff-versus-specific contrast as the feedback in the revision round and re-score.

**Pitfall:** Accepting an atmospheric opening because it reads nicely. Grow and Convert's failing example is competent prose; the defect is that it contains no information about the product or the problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's test brief should use a real Pabau topic and reject introductions that open with how busy a clinic day is. The passing shape names what competing software fails to do and how Pabau does it.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask for a full introduction in the test project and score it on specificity. A passing opening names what competing products fail to solve and how yours does; a failing one describes the reader's busy day.

### 18. Write use-case pages that describe exactly what the product does  `95.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 95*

Alongside comparisons, Grow and Convert targeted use-case keywords for Circuit that are a precise description of the product's functionality. The example is 'how to create a delivery route', which drove 12 free trial signups in the 4 months after publication. The logic is that when the search phrase is a literal description of the job your software performs, the reader arriving is already trying to do that job, so the product recommendation is the natural conclusion of the article rather than an interruption. Goolding pairs this with niche how-to keywords, like starting a medical delivery service, which was sub-20 when chosen and produced 31 signups in 6 months of page-1 ranking. Both categories are small in volume and short in the gap between reading and signing up.

> "which drove 12 free trial signups in the 4 months"

**Evidence:** Circuit's 'how to create a delivery route' page: 12 free trial signups in 4 months. Its medical delivery service post: 31 signups in 6 months on page 1.

**How to do it**

1. List the tasks your software performs, written in the words a user would use, one per line.
2. Convert each into a 'how to [task]' keyword and check it in Ahrefs, keeping sub-20 rows.
3. Check the SERP for each: if the results are manual step-by-step guides with no tool built for the job, that is the opening.
4. Write the article as the genuine manual method first, in full, so it earns the ranking.
5. Introduce the product at the point in the process where the manual method becomes tedious.
6. Name the specific feature that removes that step, and link to the feature page.
7. Put a trial or demo CTA at that point in the article, not only at the end.
8. Measure signups per use-case page and expand the ones producing conversions into adjacent tasks.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Writing the how-to as a thin lead-in to a product pitch loses the ranking, because the SERP is full of genuinely useful manual guides. The manual method has to be complete before the product appears.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build use-case articles named after tasks the software performs, such as how to run a consent form workflow or how to schedule a course of treatments, and introduce Pabau at the point the manual method breaks down. These convert far better than general clinic marketing guides.

**Apply anywhere:** Write articles named after the exact tasks your product performs. Explain the manual method properly, then introduce the product at the point the manual method becomes tedious, and put the CTA there.

### 20. Include a retargeting audience for cheap engagement, not just conversions  `128.21`
*context · best practices · source 128*

Grow and Convert run retargeting as one of their three audience types, and they give two reasons rather than one. The obvious reason is reaching prospects who have visited the site but not yet converted. The second is less obvious and is about the ad itself: existing customers and returning visitors are more likely to drive positive engagement toward the content. That engagement matters because Facebook prices delivery partly on engagement, so a set that reliably likes, comments and shares subsidizes the cost of the campaign it sits in and gives the article visible social proof to cold visitors. Retargeting is also the cheapest of the three sets by audience size, so the cost of running it as a standing engagement set is small. It is a minor optimization but it explains why the audience mix is three sets rather than two.

> "customers who are more likely to drive positive engagement towards our content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite two purposes for their retargeting set: reaching unconverted prospects, and customers who drive positive engagement toward the content.

**How to do it**

1. Build a retargeting audience of all website visitors, not only pricing or demo page visitors.
2. Add existing customers as a separate included source, since they engage most readily.
3. Run it as a standing low-budget set alongside cold and lookalike rather than only during launches.
4. Point it at articles rather than at product pages, matching the rest of the content promotion.
5. Check the engagement rate on the retargeting set against the cold set to confirm the subsidy is real.
6. Use the same ad post ID across audiences so likes and comments accumulate on one piece of social proof.
7. Keep the budget small, since the audience is small and will fatigue fast.

**Tools:** Facebook

**Pitfall:** Running retargeting on separate ad creatives per audience. Engagement then fragments across several posts and the cold audience sees an ad with no social proof, which removes the reason for including the set.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any Pabau content promotion should include a small standing retargeting set of site visitors and existing practice customers, using the same ad post across all audiences so engagement collects in one place.

**Apply anywhere:** Include a small standing retargeting set of all site visitors plus existing customers in content promotion. It reaches unconverted prospects and, because those people engage most readily, it lowers delivery cost and gives the ad visible social proof. Use one shared ad post across audiences so engagement accumulates.

### 22. Treat ghostwriting as ordinary writing with a voice-match requirement  `164.17`
*context · general insights · source 164*

Grow and Convert argue there is no such thing as a ghostwriter: a ghostwriter is just a writer who does not put their own name on the byline. At their agency, sometimes the writer's own name is used on a client article and sometimes the client founder's name, someone else at the company, or the brand itself, depending on client preference or the context of the piece. The practical consequence is that the hiring dynamics are identical to hiring any writer, with one added requirement, that the writer's style is a natural fit for your voice. That extra requirement is what makes their multi-filter process more important, not less, because voice fit is exactly what a client-edited portfolio piece hides and what the unedited mini sample reveals.

> "But the reality is there is no such thing"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish client articles under writer bylines, founder bylines, other employees' names and the brand name, depending on the client.

**How to do it**

1. Stop searching for ghostwriters as a separate category and source writers generally.
2. Decide the byline per article, not per writer: writer's name, a founder's name, another employee, or the brand.
3. Add one criterion to your normal writer screen: how naturally the writer's style fits your voice.
4. Test voice fit on the unedited mini sample, since portfolio pieces may carry a client's editing.
5. Give the writer two or three published pieces of yours as the voice reference during the test project.
6. Coach voice explicitly in the feedback round rather than expecting it from day one.

**Pitfall:** Paying a ghostwriting premium for what is ordinary writing work. The only genuine difference is voice matching, which you should test rather than pay a category rate for.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles carry various bylines already, so recruit writers rather than ghostwriters and test voice fit against two published Pabau pieces during the test project.

**Apply anywhere:** Hire writers, not ghostwriters. The only added requirement when the byline is someone else's is voice fit, and you test that with an unedited sample rather than paying a category premium.
