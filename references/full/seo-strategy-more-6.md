### 1. Treat brand authority as the variable that flips the format decision  `146.14`
*useful · general insights · source 146*

Grow and Convert are clear that there is no universal answer to landing page versus blog post, and that the variable deciding it is the client's authority in their space. A super well known brand that generally has an easy time getting product pages to rank for competitive bottom-of-funnel keywords should consider the landing page when the SERP is mostly landing and product pages, because that composition suggests searchers want a product rather than a wordy blog post. A brand search engines do not recognize as an authority has low chances of ranking a product page, which has less space for relevant keywords and for the on-page signals Google weighs. That brand should take the blog post. The same SERP therefore produces opposite recommendations for two different companies.

> "we take into consideration our client's authority in their space"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert apply this authority test as the tie-breaker across dozens of agency clients when the SERP mixes product landing pages with blog posts.

**Pitfall:** Copying a market leader's page-type strategy without their authority. The SERP looks like it rewards product pages, but it rewards those specific brands' product pages.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should assume Pabau is in the lower-authority case on most category terms and default to articles, then revisit the rule for individual terms where Pabau's own pages already rank on page one.

**Apply anywhere:** Assume you are in the lower-authority case on most category terms and default to articles, then revisit the rule for individual terms where your own pages already rank on page one.

### 2. Treat content-customer fit as a named stage before scaling output  `122.11`
*useful · general insights · source 122*

Grow and Convert formalize the misalignment they kept seeing into a concept they call content-customer fit, explicitly modeled on product-market fit, which they credit to some combination of Marc Andreessen, Sean Ellis and Steve Blank. The analogy carries a sequence: research, then a minimum output, then iteration toward fit, and only then scale. They map the research phase onto Steve Blank's line to get out of the building, meaning founders should talk to customers repeatedly before building. Their equivalent is that a content team should talk to customers repeatedly before publishing. The practical value of naming it is that it gives a team a reason to pause production, which is otherwise hard to justify when the calendar is the metric.

> "a nod to "product-market fit"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert coined the term after seeing the problem across many industries and clients, and frame it directly against the product-market fit sequence.

**How to do it**

1. Name the current stage honestly: pre-fit if the blog produces few or no useful leads.
2. Freeze net-new publishing volume while pre-fit rather than adding more of the same.
3. Run the research phase: session, CRM sort, profile, pain points.
4. Publish a small batch, three to five pieces, as the content equivalent of an MVP.
5. Measure those pieces on lead quality against the best-customer profile, not on traffic.
6. Only raise publishing volume once that batch produces leads matching the profile.

**Pitfall:** Scaling output before fit multiplies the mismatch. More posts for the wrong reader produce more traffic, more work and the same lead count, which is the state Grow and Convert's clients arrive in.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau increases its publishing rate, David should confirm that the current batch aimed at practice owners produces demo requests. If it does not, volume is the wrong lever.

**Apply anywhere:** Name content-customer fit as a stage you either have or do not. Pre-fit, freeze volume, do the research, ship a small batch, and only scale once that batch produces the right leads.

### 3. Treat high domain ratings as a defended position, not a barrier to entry  `94.10`
*useful · general insights · source 94*

Grow and Convert open by naming three assumptions behind the defeatist view of SEO: that bigger budgets mean better results, that it is nearly impossible to compete with sites that have tons of backlinks and high domain ratings, and that because a previous SEO attempt produced little, SEO does not work for the company. They argue the conclusion is false, and their client work has ranked smaller and newer sites for keywords that generate qualified leads. The useful reframe is what a high domain rating actually buys: it defends the terms the incumbent has spent money and years defending, usually the one to three category head terms. It does not defend the qualified variants they never built a page for, which is where the underdog wins.

> "it's nearly impossible to compete with sites that have tons of backlinks"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say they have ranked many smaller, newer sites for valuable keywords that generate qualified leads in their client work.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** The opposite error is treating a low DR as irrelevant. It is not; it just changes the sequence, since under about DR 30 site-level authority work comes first.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should read a competitor's high DR as a signal about which specific terms are defended, not as a verdict on the whole category. Check whether a tailored page exists for each qualified variant before writing the term off.

**Apply anywhere:** A competitor's high domain rating defends the terms they have spent years and money on, usually the category head terms. Check each qualified variant for a genuinely tailored page before assuming you cannot compete.

### 4. Treat paid search intent discipline as stricter than organic, because clicks cost  `157.17`
*useful · general insights · source 157*

Grow and Convert make an explicit comparison between the two channels. They say the finding that search intent is the most important factor matches what they have seen in content and SEO, but the problem is actually worse in paid search because the clicks are not free. Every bad search term your ad gets clicked on is money gone. They also note the asymmetry in how teams behave: in SEO, targeting top-of-funnel terms for brand awareness and traffic metrics is a deliberate choice, whereas nobody sets out to bid on low-converting keywords in paid. It happens accidentally, through volume chasing, cheap CPC chasing and broad match. The strategic conclusion is that a keyword list good enough for organic is not automatically good enough for paid.

> "the problem is actually worse with paid search because the clicks aren't free"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert draw the comparison from running both channels for SaaS clients.

**Pitfall:** Porting the organic keyword map straight into a paid account. The top-of-funnel terms that justify themselves in organic through brand awareness burn budget in paid with no offsetting benefit.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog keyword list and any paid keyword list should be maintained separately. Informational aesthetic-practice topics earn their place organically but should never be lifted into a paid campaign.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep your paid and organic keyword lists separate. Top-of-funnel terms can justify themselves organically through awareness, but in paid every low-intent click is a direct cost with no offsetting benefit.

### 5. Treat post-breakeven rankings as an asset you can stop paying for  `129.15`
*useful · content insights · source 129*

Grow and Convert describe an option most channels do not offer. Once you have crossed breakeven, the rankings you acquired have already been paid for, so the business has a real choice. Keep spending the same incremental monthly cost to produce more content and win more rankings, or stop SEO investment entirely and let the rankings you already hold keep producing results with spend cut off. This is the mechanism behind their claim that SEO results are evergreen. They are careful about the word free: getting and maintaining rankings takes people and resources, so it is not free, but there is no incremental cost per visitor, so at a certain point the rankings do begin generating traffic and leads for nothing. The caveat they attach is that this holds only while the rankings are maintained, which in their experience can be years if the pages deeply match the search intent of the keyword.

> "stopping further SEO investment and letting the rankings you acquired continue producing results"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say rankings can be maintained for years when pages are crafted to deeply match the search intent of the keyword, with no incremental cost per visitor.

**How to do it**

1. Identify the month cumulative value passed cumulative spend on your ROI sheet.
2. List the pages that produce the leads, with their ranking keywords and current positions.
3. Decide explicitly between two modes: keep spending to add rankings, or stop and hold what you have.
4. If you choose to hold, keep a minimum maintenance budget for refreshes rather than cutting to zero.
5. Set a monitoring cadence in Search Console for those specific pages so a slide is caught before the leads stop.
6. Refresh any page whose position or clicks decline, since unmaintained pages do not hold indefinitely.
7. Reopen the spend decision if a competitor starts taking positions on your money keywords.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Reading evergreen as maintenance-free and cutting the budget to nothing. The rankings persist only while maintained; the pages that hold for years are the ones that deeply match the keyword's intent and get refreshed when they slip.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's already-ranking pages are the paid-for asset. Fund a standing refresh cadence for them out of the SEO budget before funding new page families, because a slipped page costs demo requests that no new page recovers.

**Apply anywhere:** After breakeven, the rankings you hold are a paid-for asset with no incremental cost per visitor, so you can either keep spending to add more or stop and hold. If you hold, keep a maintenance budget: monitor those specific pages in Search Console and refresh any that slip, because unmaintained rankings do not last.

### 6. Treat pre-fit content as an asset, and set no conversion target  `160.8`
*useful · best practices · source 160*

Grow and Convert say early stage companies should absolutely do content marketing, but with realistic goals attached. Until product/market fit and positioning are settled and cold-audience selling is proven, you cannot assume any number of conversions. Their framing is to approach the channel as an investment in a long-term asset. The traffic still has value while you iterate on product and messaging, because it gives you an audience to test offers against and to learn from. Once the core pieces are in place, the upfront investment pays off, as it did for them when the same site became their only lead source. The mistake they name is chasing more traffic and obsessing over article details while the real gap is the product.

> "you're investing in a long-term asset"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's pre-product site later became their sole lead source, supplying consistent leads from content alone with no other marketing.

**How to do it**

1. Decide up front whether the program is a lead channel or an asset build, based on whether cold-audience sales are proven.
2. For an asset build, set the reporting metrics to traffic from the right audience, email signups and qualitative replies, not conversions.
3. Use the audience as a test bed: put each new offer in front of them and record the result.
4. Keep publishing on the topics your target buyer cares about even while the offer changes.
5. Do not increase spend on traffic when conversions are flat; investigate the product and positioning instead.
6. Re-set the goal to leads and revenue only once a cold-channel sale is proven, then reuse the same content without rewriting it.

**Pitfall:** Reporting an early-stage content program against conversion targets. The team concludes content does not work, cuts it, and loses the compounding asset just before the product catches up.

**Apply at Pabau:** For content Pabau publishes around features that are not yet sold standalone, David should report traffic and email capture rather than demo requests, so those pages are not cut before the offer exists.

**Apply anywhere:** Where the offer is unproven, run content as an asset build and report traffic, signups and audience replies instead of conversions. Switch to revenue reporting once a cold-channel sale is proven, using the same content you already published.

### 7. Treat specificity and JTBD keywords as a portfolio of bets, not forecasts  `142.14`
*useful · content insights · source 142*

Two of the six groups in Grow and Convert's data behave as portfolios rather than as predictable averages. On category keywords with an added layer of specificity, they say it is a game of taking bets where you have to guess which variants hit with your audience and product, and that most will not hit; the group averages 2.96% while a single hit reached 15.2%. On alternatives keywords the same shape appears: an 8.43% average with most posts under 4%. Their conclusion is not to stop, but that even the misses at 1 to 4% beat top-of-funnel posts converting at a fraction of a percent, so taking a variety of bets is worth it. The planning consequence is that these programs must be sized as a batch. Publishing three specificity posts and judging the average will usually look like failure.

> "a game of taking bets"

**Evidence:** Specificity group: 13 posts averaging 2.96% with one at 15.2%. Alternatives group: 23 keywords averaging 8.43% with most under 4%.

**Pitfall:** Cancelling the program after a handful of posts land near 2%. The distribution is skewed, so a small sample almost never contains the outlier that justifies it.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should commission specificity and alternatives articles for Pabau in batches of at least eight to ten and review the batch after two quarters, rather than judging each article as it lands.

**Apply anywhere:** Commission specificity and alternatives articles in batches of at least eight to ten and review the batch after two quarters, rather than judging each article as it lands.

### 8. Treat the best-tool-for-X question as a sign of a missing strategy  `175.3`
*useful · general insights · source 175*

Grow and Convert opens by naming the questions they are tired of hearing: what is the best marketing stack for my company, what is the best tool for content promotion, what is the best tool for blog conversions. Their verdict is that asking those questions means you are going about the job the wrong way. The pattern matters for SEO because the same question shape shows up constantly: what is the best rank tracker, the best AI writing tool, the best GEO tracker. The tool question is a proxy for an unanswered strategy question about who the audience is, what the content is meant to do and how promotion actually happens. Answering the strategy question usually shrinks the tool question to something trivial.

> "I'm tired of being asked the question"

**Pitfall:** Answering the tool question directly. The person walks away with a subscription and the original problem intact.

**Apply at Pabau:** When someone at Pabau asks which tool to buy for a marketing problem, David should first ask what the current process is and what result it produces. If neither answer exists, the tool decision is premature.

**Apply anywhere:** When a colleague asks which tool to buy for a marketing problem, ask what the current process is and what result it produces. If neither answer exists, the tool decision is premature.

### 9. Treat the content team as a product team with a shipped product  `75.12`
*useful · general insights · source 75*

The author frames the operating model behind everything else in the article. The advice that businesses should build media companies rather than marketing departments means the content is the product, and its purpose is to deliver value to the reader while keeping them engaged. A good content marketer, he says, thinks about content the way a JTBD-focused product manager thinks about a product. The reframe pushes teams toward long-term value and toward the reader's needs rather than what the brand extracts in return, on the argument that content which fulfills reader needs builds awareness, affinity and word of mouth, and revenue over the long term. His conclusion is blunt: content experience is the future of SEO, and the faster teams think like product managers the better the results. It is context rather than a tactic, but it explains why he chooses tools and libraries over articles.

> "much like a JTBD-focused product manager"

**Evidence:** The author's closing position that content experience is the future of SEO, alongside his caveat that most teams lack the resources for advanced content products and can rely on blog posts instead.

**Pitfall:** Adopting the language without the resourcing. A content team asked to ship product experiences on an article budget produces neither, and the author concedes most teams lack the budget or skillset for advanced content products.

**Apply at Pabau:** Judge Pabau's content roadmap the way a product roadmap is judged: a small number of shipped experiences maintained over time, such as the code libraries and template pages, rather than a monthly article count.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge your content roadmap like a product roadmap, on a small number of maintained experiences rather than monthly output. Only commit to product-grade content if the build and maintenance budget exists.

### 10. Use $100 to $750 per article as the freelance writing benchmark  `152.10`
*useful · content insights · source 152*

Grow and Convert use $300 per article as the modeled cost for outsourced blog writing, and give the surrounding range explicitly: as much as $750 or more for really good pieces, as low as $100. They warn against anchoring on the $300 figure, because it depends on industry, piece length and research required. In the model, that per-article cost sits alongside custom graphics and any other genuinely per-post expense; if the marketing manager writes everything and there is no graphics spend, this bucket is zero. The range is useful mainly as a sanity check when negotiating with writers and when deciding whether a $750 specialist piece is defensible, which the sensitivity analysis suggests it usually is because writing cost barely moves CAC.

> "I've used a per article cost of $300"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite $100 at the low end, $300 as their model default, and $750 or more for really good pieces.

**Pitfall:** Benchmarking a specialist regulated-industry writer against the $300 midpoint. Research-heavy pieces sit at the top of the range or above it, and holding to the midpoint buys generic copy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's aesthetics and healthcare topics need writers who understand clinical workflow, which sits at the top of this range. David should budget nearer $750 a piece for those and treat the extra as cheap against the salary line.

**Apply anywhere:** Budget toward the top of the $100 to $750 range for regulated or research-heavy topics, and treat the premium as cheap against your salary line.

### 11. Use the launch spike as a starting point, then double down  `123.11`
*useful · general insights · source 123*

Hyam is explicit that the launch piece is a starting point and not the growth. After the Apple Watch post produced 200 linking domains and 20,000 visitors in a week, he says that one article served as the launching point for the blog, and then they doubled down on content marketing and grew from 0 to 35,000 monthly unique visitors in six months. The same pattern shows at Vistage, where day-one traffic of 3,000 from the influencer became 1,000 to 20,000 monthly visitors over the following year. The value of the launch is the link equity and the audience it hands you, which makes the ordinary content that follows rank and get read. A launch with no follow-through decays back to nothing.

> "then we doubled down on content marketing"

**Evidence:** ThinkApps went from 0 to 35,000 monthly unique visitors in six months after the launch post; Vistage went from 1,000 to 20,000 monthly visitors in the year after its relaunch.

**How to do it**

1. Treat the launch asset's referring domains as the authority budget that makes subsequent pages rank.
2. Have the next three months of ordinary content planned before launch day, so publishing does not stop after the spike.
3. Link internally from the launch asset to the pages you want to rank, since it holds the links.
4. Convert launch traffic to email so the audience can be reached again without another spike.
5. Measure monthly uniques rather than launch-week uniques when judging whether the strategy worked.
6. Repeat the flagship-asset play once or twice a year rather than relying on the first one.
7. Refresh the launch asset if it keeps earning links, since it is the page carrying the domain.

**Pitfall:** Reading the launch spike as the outcome. Traffic collapses within weeks if no ordinary content follows, and the referring domains from the launch never get used to rank anything commercial.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau builds a flagship data asset, David should plan the following quarter of blog and template pages at the same time, and internally link from the asset into the commercial pages that need authority.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat a launch spike as an authority and audience budget, not a result: plan the next quarter of ordinary content before launch, link internally from the launch asset into the pages that need to rank, and judge the strategy on monthly traffic months later.

### 12. Verticals with no paid-search fallback take more organic risk  `67.18`
*useful · general insights · source 67*

Dirk offers a structural explanation for why iGaming leans black hat that generalizes beyond gambling. In health you can run PPC as a fallback, so when a core update costs you rankings you shift budget into paid and survive. In gambling PPC is largely closed off, so organic is the only channel, and management and shareholders understand that. That absence of a fallback is what makes the industry willing to take risks other verticals would not. He rejects the related theory that Google goes softer on iGaming, pointing at Finixio's penalties as evidence, and argues iGaming is a YMYL industry alongside finance and health, so it gets the same scrutiny. The useful transfer is the risk calculus: the fewer channels you can fall back on, the less risk you should be taking with the one you have.

> "you can't use PPC as a backup"

**Evidence:** Dirk points at Finixio's iGaming properties being penalized very badly as evidence against the softer-treatment theory.

**Pitfall:** Assuming your vertical is treated leniently. Dirk says there is no official position that iGaming is exempt, and the operators who assumed so took the heaviest penalties in the 2025 update.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can run paid search, so its organic risk appetite should be lower, not higher, than an operator with no fallback. Nothing in Pabau's SEO should depend on a tactic that could cost the domain.

**Apply anywhere:** Set your organic risk appetite against how many other channels you can fall back on. If paid search is available to you, there is no case for tactics that could cost you the domain.

### 13. Weigh reputational/platform risk against opportunity cost before any hack  `43.11`
*useful · best practices · source 43*

Rand's formula for deciding whether to pursue a short-term, exploitable marketing or SEO hack versus a sustainable channel is a two-factor weighing: risk (reputational risk, and platform risk — could you be banned, and how likely is that) versus opportunity cost (the realistic estimated benefit, e.g., 'if we appeared in 100 more AI responses a day, is that worth an extra $100K or $1M a year,' weighed against what else that time/budget could buy, like more ad spend or product investment). He notes that for his own company, product investment (making SparkToro better and easier to use, a major visual redesign) has been the single biggest growth driver of the last six months, a data point that itself fed into this same risk/opportunity-cost weighing versus chasing more growth hacks.

> "the mathematics is essentially: what's the risk"

**How to do it**

1. Before adopting any short-term SEO/growth tactic, write down the reputational risk (how it looks if discovered/publicized) and the platform risk (could the account/site be banned, and how likely is that).
2. Estimate a realistic dollar-value benefit if the tactic works at the scale you expect.
3. List the alternative uses of that same time/budget (paid ads spend, product improvements, other channels) as your opportunity cost.
4. Compare the risk-adjusted benefit estimate against the opportunity cost of the next-best alternative use of that budget/time.
5. Only proceed with the tactic if the benefit clearly outweighs both the risk and the opportunity cost; otherwise redirect the resources to the alternative (inferred).
6. Periodically revisit this comparison as your product/growth levers change, since product investment became the biggest growth driver only after six months, not from the start (inferred).

**Pitfall:** Copying a 'growth hacker' tactic seen on social media without realizing the demonstrated win was likely run on a disposable churn-and-burn asset, not the operator's real money site or brand — applying the same risk tolerance to your actual brand's site is a category error Rand explicitly warns about.

### 14. When a channel is working, stop touching it  `68.22`
*useful · best practices · source 68*

Cody's closing maxim, delivered as a warning about a specific and common failure. He has worked at startups with a pipeline that genuinely worked, where a dollar in returned six dollars of lifetime value, and watched them decide that because they did not know why it worked they should tinker with it. They broke it, tried to revert, and the reverted version did not perform the same way. He says he is in exactly that position right now: positioning settled, go-to-market motion settled, product selling, and the correct action is to do nothing, which he calls a very unnatural state for anyone good at building businesses. He names sitting still in the quiet moments as the hardest unspoken skill in entrepreneurship, and pairs it with his mantra of patience, persistence and compound interest. Applied to SEO, this argues against unnecessary edits to pages that are ranking and converting.

> "when it's working, don't touch it"

**Evidence:** Startups Cody worked at broke a pipeline returning $6 of lifetime value per $1 spent by tinkering with it, then could not restore it.

**How to do it**

1. Identify the pages and channels that are currently performing and list them explicitly.
2. Freeze those assets: no template changes, no URL changes, no rewrites, no redesign.
3. Document what you believe is driving the performance, even if the explanation is incomplete.
4. Route improvement effort to underperforming assets instead, where the downside is bounded.
5. If you must test on a winner, change one variable and keep a rollback copy of the exact prior version.
6. Accept that a reverted change often does not restore the previous performance.

**Pitfall:** Reverting is not a safety net. Cody has seen teams break a working pipeline, roll back, and find it never performed the same again.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's top-performing articles and template pages should be on an explicit do-not-touch list during site-wide template or design changes, with bulk edits routed away from them.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep a written list of the pages and channels that are working and exclude them from redesigns, bulk edits and experiments. A rollback rarely restores what a change to a working asset destroys.

### 15. When a new channel works, go all in rather than testing again  `173.12`
*useful · best practices · source 173*

Grow and Convert draw an explicit lesson from KlientBoost's partnership phase: if you discover a new marketing strategy is valuable, go for it and do not just dip your toe in, because large results come from large amounts of effort. The evidence is the sequence. The taco party at CRO Day worked, so KlientBoost doubled down and started co-producing guides and webinars with non-competitive brands. That produced enough partner interest that they had to turn companies away, and it eventually became a whole resources page of webinars, ebooks and speaking engagements. They also hired someone full time purely to promote content. The pattern is one successful test followed by structural commitment, not a rolling series of small experiments that never compound.

> "Don't just dip your toe in. Large results come from large amounts of effort."

**Evidence:** KlientBoost went from one hosted conference party to a full program of co-produced ebooks, webinars and speaking, plus a full-time content promoter.

**How to do it**

1. Run the initial test at the smallest honest scale and define in advance what a positive result looks like.
2. When it clears that bar, do not run a second identical test; scale it in the same quarter.
3. Scale by adding volume and by adding a dedicated owner, not by adding it to someone's existing list.
4. Approach the whole category of potential partners or targets at once rather than sequentially.
5. Build the recurring asset the channel produces, such as a resources page, so each execution compounds.
6. Set a review date three to six months out and only then reassess whether to keep investing.
7. Cut a channel decisively if it fails the bar twice, rather than keeping it on low simmer.

**Pitfall:** Running every channel at 10% effort produces ambiguous results everywhere and compounding nowhere. The signal is a marketing plan with eight active channels and no owner for any of them.

**Apply at Pabau:** If one Pabau content format proves out, a co-produced guide with a device manufacturer for example, David should commission the next five in the same quarter rather than treating each as a fresh test, and give one person the promotion job.

**Apply anywhere:** Once a channel clears a predefined success bar, commit to it structurally in the same quarter: dedicated owner, category-wide outreach, and a permanent asset that accumulates. Repeated small tests waste the signal you already have.

### 16. Write bottom-funnel posts when last click beats first click  `151.9`
*useful · content insights · source 151*

Grow and Convert show an agency client where multiple blog posts had more last-click than first-click conversions, and no posts with the reverse, once the homepage row was ignored. Their reading is that most people sign up to talk to that agency in the same session as reading a post, and for the four highlighted posts those readers had first landed somewhere else on the site. The strategic conclusion they draw is specific: focus on writing bottom-of-funnel posts that qualify and attract hot prospects, because you now know those readers are willing to fill out the contact form immediately upon reading. This is the opposite prescription from the delayed pattern, and it comes from the same report read the other way.

> "ignore the first line, which is their homepage"

**Evidence:** For one Grow and Convert agency client, several posts showed more last-click than first-click conversions and none showed the reverse.

**How to do it**

1. Sort the model comparison table by last click descending and exclude the homepage row.
2. Count how many posts have last click higher than first click.
3. If that is the dominant pattern, treat the blog as a closing surface rather than a discovery surface.
4. List the search intents behind those posts and identify their bottom-of-funnel neighbours: alternatives, comparisons, pricing, best-of lists.
5. Brief the next quarter's posts against those bottom-of-funnel intents.
6. Put a qualifying contact CTA inside each of those posts rather than a generic newsletter box.
7. Recheck the table a quarter later to confirm the new posts converted on last click too.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Excluding the homepage row is essential; it usually carries the largest counts for reasons unrelated to content and will distort the comparison if left in.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's comparison and alternatives articles are the closing surface, so David should keep a demo CTA inside them and brief more bottom-funnel topics when they show last-click demos.

**Apply anywhere:** When last-click conversions beat first-click across your posts, treat the blog as a closing surface and brief more bottom-of-funnel topics with a qualifying CTA in-body.

### 17. Write down the audience and feature goals before the gap report  `118.8`
*useful · best practices · source 118*

Grow and Convert name three distinct goals teams have when digging into competitor keywords, and say to pick before you start: compete head-on for the same buying-intent keywords the competitor already ranks for; discover pain point, JTBD and long-tail terms your market uses that you had not thought of; or find terms competitors have not targeted at all. Each goal produces a different reading of the same report. They add a set of framing questions to answer first: is there an audience you want to target, such as enterprise teams, and equally is there an audience you want to avoid; are there product features you want to promote; was the product built for specific problems. Their worked example makes this concrete when they flag 'enterprise customer service software' as valuable only for brands actually chasing enterprise.

> "Establish specific goals before building out your content plan"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert flag 'enterprise customer service software' as a strong opportunity only for help desk brands actually targeting enterprise users.

**How to do it**

1. Before opening the tool, write one sentence naming which of the three goals this round serves: match, discover, or find untargeted.
2. List the audience segments you want more of, and separately list the segments you want to avoid.
3. List the two or three product features this quarter's marketing needs to push.
4. List the specific problems the product was built to solve, in the customer's words.
5. Use the avoid list actively when triaging, cutting keywords that would bring the wrong segment.
6. When a keyword carries a segment qualifier such as 'enterprise' or 'multi-location', keep it only if that segment is on the target list.
7. Note against each shortlisted keyword which goal and which feature it serves, so the plan can be reviewed against the goals later.

**Pitfall:** Running the report without a stated goal produces a plan that reads as reasonable but pulls traffic from segments you cannot serve profitably. The signal is a rising demo request count with a falling close rate.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's keyword rounds should start with a written note of the target practice type, single-site or multi-location, and the features being pushed that quarter, so qualifiers like 'multi-location' or 'medical spa' are kept or cut deliberately.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the round's goal, target segments, avoid list and priority features before opening the keyword tool, then triage the report against that note.
