### 1. Run a 60-90 minute recorded interview with product, sales and CS  `181.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 181*

Grow and Convert's production model refuses both freelancers who Google for research and scaled AI generation. Instead they record interviews of 60 to 90 minutes with the client's product managers, sales team and customer success experts. The stated purpose is to extract the unique differentiators and customer pain points that only insiders know, which is what makes the article hard for a competitor to replicate. Human writers then turn the recording into the article. The base already carries many interview-first entries; what is specific here is the length band and the three named roles as a set. Three roles matter because they hold different material: product managers know the workflow detail, sales knows the objection and the competitive comparison, customer success knows what actually breaks after purchase.

> "60-90 minute recorded interviews with your product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state this as their standing production process across 100+ SaaS clients since 2016.

**How to do it**

1. Book one recorded call of 60 to 90 minutes per topic, not a 20-minute check-in.
2. Invite three role types across your interview program: a product manager, a salesperson and a customer success rep.
3. Ask the product manager for the exact workflow and the feature detail an informed buyer would test.
4. Ask sales for the objections raised on calls and the specific competitor comparisons buyers make.
5. Ask customer success for the pain points that show up after purchase and the mistakes new customers make.
6. Record and transcribe the call, then write from the transcript rather than from notes.
7. Mark every claim in the draft that came only from the interview, since those are the parts a competitor cannot copy.
8. Send the draft back to the interviewee for a factual pass before publishing.

**Pitfall:** A single short interview with one role produces generic copy dressed as expertise. If the draft contains nothing a competitor could not have written from the SERP, the interview was too short or the wrong person.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles on clinic workflows should be built from a recorded call with a product manager plus someone from sales or support, not from desk research. That interview is where the detail that separates a Pabau page from a competitor's page comes from.

**Apply anywhere:** Book a recorded 60-90 minute interview per topic with a product manager, a salesperson and a customer success rep, and write from the transcript so the article carries detail outsiders cannot reproduce.

### 2. Run a five-minute sales debrief every morning, not a weekly meeting  `177.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 177*

Grow and Convert's author says the period when he was most effective as a marketer was when he spoke to the sales team every single morning. He asked three fixed things: how yesterday's calls went, what the quality of the leads coming through was like, and what objections were stopping prospects from buying. He is explicit that the standard forced weekly sales-and-marketing meeting is not enough. The daily cadence did three jobs at once. It gave him a live feedback loop on where marketing needed fixing, it signaled to sales that marketing was invested in their number, and it taught sales what marketing actually does day to day. For a content team this is the cheapest source of bottom-funnel topics and objection copy available, and it costs five minutes a day.

> "Every morning I would go and talk to the sales team"

**Evidence:** The author reports his most successful stretch as a marketer coincided with daily morning conversations with the sales team.

**How to do it**

1. Book a standing five-minute slot with the sales team each morning, before your own work starts.
2. Ask the same three questions every time: how did yesterday's calls go, how was lead quality, and what objections came up.
3. Log every objection verbatim in one running document, with the date and the rep who heard it.
4. Tag each objection as a content gap, a lead-quality problem, or a product gap.
5. Turn recurring objections into article sections or FAQ entries within the same month.
6. Report back what you changed as a result, so sales sees the loop close.
7. Keep the weekly formal meeting for decisions only, not for discovery.

**Tools:** Google Docs

**Pitfall:** A weekly meeting compresses a week of call detail into a summary, and the specific phrasing prospects used is the first thing lost. The signal you have hit this is objection notes that read like categories rather than sentences.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run a short recurring check-in with Pabau's sales team and log the objections aesthetic and healthcare practices raise on demo calls. Those objections become sections in comparison and pricing articles rather than being invented from the SERP.

**Apply anywhere:** Speak to your sales team every morning for five minutes rather than relying on a weekly meeting. Ask how calls went, how lead quality looked and what objections came up, and log the objections verbatim as your content backlog.

### 3. Run a half-day customer research session with sales and support present  `122.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 122*

Grow and Convert open every client engagement with an intensive user research session, typically half a day at the client's office. The session deliberately includes far more than the marketing team: C-level staff, someone from sales development, the account executive team, customer support or account management, and any other employee with direct customer contact or deep customer knowledge. Their reason is blunt. Marketers usually do not talk to customers, so anything marketing reports is filtered or a best guess. They want it straight from the people who speak to customers daily. The session is the input to everything downstream, including the best-customer list and the pain point list, so it happens before any keyword or topic work.

> "the first thing we do is an intensive user research session"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report doing this at the start of every client engagement, across clients with blogs ranging from under 1,000 to over 200,000 monthly visitors.

**How to do it**

1. Book a half-day block on site, not a 30-minute call, and treat it as the kickoff for the whole content program.
2. Invite at minimum: a C-level exec, an SDR, an account executive, and someone from support or account management.
3. Add anyone else with direct customer contact, including implementation and onboarding staff.
4. Tell marketing to listen and take notes rather than answer for the customer.
5. Pull the CRM up live in the room so claims can be checked against account data.
6. Record the session and transcribe it so pain-point wording can be lifted verbatim later.
7. Close the session with a written list of candidate best customers and their pain points.
8. Schedule follow-up research where the room disagreed or could not answer.

**Pitfall:** Running the session with marketing only. You get the team's assumptions about the customer back, not the customer, and the resulting content plan inherits the same blind spot it was meant to fix.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this session at Pabau with a clinic-facing CSM, an AE and support present before the next content quarter is planned, and record it. Aesthetic practice owners phrase their problems very differently from how the marketing team writes about them.

**Apply anywhere:** Kick off any content program with a half-day research session that includes sales, support and an executive, not just marketing. Record it and lift the customer's own wording for later use.

### 4. Run a three-filter writer hire: application, paid test, real pieces  `164.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 164*

Grow and Convert's current hiring process for their agency has three sequential filters, built after evaluating 200-plus writers and 1000-plus applications. Step one is an application carrying two parts: portfolio samples and an on-the-fly mini sample. Step two is a paid test project that replicates real client work, including one round of feedback. Step three is several real pieces, because even survivors of the first two filters do not all work out. They are explicit that the common approach, look at a portfolio then assign a live article, fails: portfolio pieces alone are not enough because you cannot tell how much a client edited them. They say hiring writers is easy and hiring good writers is hard, and the filtering is what separates the two.

> "you need multiple filters to find"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert filtered 1000-plus writer applications and tested and hired dozens of writers for their agency over six years.

**How to do it**

1. Build an application form that asks for three to five portfolio links plus one on-the-fly mini sample question.
2. Filter on the portfolio first; expect to cut roughly 80% of applicants here.
3. Advance only applicants strong on both portfolio and mini sample to the paid test project.
4. Run a paid test project that reuses a piece you already published, giving the writer the same inputs you had.
5. Give one round of detailed written or recorded-video feedback and judge the revision, not just the first draft.
6. Assign several real pieces to survivors and accept that some still fail at this stage.
7. Track which filter each failure slipped through and tighten that filter's question next round.

**Pitfall:** Skipping straight from portfolio to a live client article. Grow and Convert say writers with years of experience and awesome portfolios did terribly on the test project, most likely because clients had heavily edited the portfolio work.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau commissions outside writers for blog or template articles, David should run all three filters rather than hiring from a portfolio. The mini sample should ask the writer to describe one Pabau feature against a named competitor.

**Apply anywhere:** Use three filters when hiring writers: an application with portfolio plus an on-the-fly mini sample, a paid test project modeled on real work, then several real pieces. A portfolio alone tells you nothing about what the writer produced unedited.

### 5. Run a two-exercise funnel to hire freelance writers  `97.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 97*

Grow and Convert describe a condensed hiring funnel whose purpose is to weed out weak writers before you pay anyone. Post the job on AngelList, ProBlogger or a similar source. Send every applicant a writer onboarding guide containing an overview of the company, the vision for the blog, and a writing exercise. Exercise one is headline pitching based only on the onboarding guide. Applicants drop off here, which is the point: you are left with motivated people. Exercise two only runs if the pitched headlines fit your blog's topics, and it is a full test post on one of their own pitches. The logic they give is that a writer who pitched a headline should be able to write a high-quality post on it. Accept the writers who pass, pay them for the test work, and set a price for a long-term relationship.

> "creating a funnel for writers to weed out"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this as the condensed process they use to find the best freelancers.

**How to do it**

1. Post the listing on AngelList, ProBlogger or an equivalent board rather than a general freelance marketplace.
2. Write a writer onboarding guide with three sections: company overview, the vision for the blog, and the writing exercise.
3. Send the guide to every applicant and require exercise one, a set of pitched headlines built only from the guide.
4. Reject any pitch set that does not fall in line with the topics your blog actually covers.
5. Ask survivors for exercise two, a full test post on one of the headlines they pitched themselves.
6. Judge the test post on specificity and real examples, not grammar.
7. Pay the writers who pass for the test post rather than treating it as free spec work.
8. Agree a per-piece price on the basis of a long-term relationship, not a one-off rate.

**Tools:** AngelList, ProBlogger

**Pitfall:** Skipping the headline stage and going straight to a paid test post means you pay for output from applicants who have not shown they understand the audience. The drop-off between the two exercises is the filter, not an inconvenience.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau commissions outside writers, David should require pitched headlines against a Pabau onboarding guide first, then a paid test article, rather than hiring from portfolio samples in other industries.

**Apply anywhere:** Hire freelance writers through a two-stage funnel: headline pitches from an onboarding guide first, then a paid test post on a headline they pitched themselves.

### 6. Run a written value-prop alignment exercise across every team  `91.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 91*

Grow and Convert say companies routinely arrive with the sales team, marketing team, product team and founders each giving a different answer for the main value proposition. They treat writing the disruption story as the forcing function that resolves it. The test is concrete: ask each team, or each individual if the company is small enough, to write down the main problem the company solves and how it solves it. They ran it on their own team and the answers converged on one theme, pain point equals content that does not produce results, solution equals we produce content that converts, which is exactly what their homepage says. They present divergence as normal rather than a failure, but insist you cannot write a disruption story on top of it. Client examples of a finished narrative: 'Most companies have software engineers own QA. Our platform lets anyone own QA.'

> "ask each team (or each person if your company is small enough) to write down the main problem"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own team produced consistent answers matching their homepage line 'Most Content Doesn't Drive Real Business. We Fix That.'; client outputs include Rainforest QA's 'anyone can own QA' and Vocal Video's 'quality video testimonials in minutes'.

**How to do it**

1. Send one question to sales, marketing, product and the founders: write down the main problem we solve and how we solve it.
2. Require written answers submitted independently, with no discussion first, so you see real divergence.
3. Collect the answers and highlight the words and pains that recur across them.
4. Name the gaps out loud: which teams describe a different pain, and which describe features instead of a pain.
5. Draft one sentence in the form 'Most companies do X. We let them do Y instead.'
6. Check that sentence against your homepage headline and fix whichever is weaker.
7. Circulate the agreed sentence back to all four groups as the canonical value prop.
8. Reuse it verbatim in the disruption story, in sales calls and in every other blog post that references your value prop.

**Pitfall:** Running the exercise in a live meeting produces false consensus, because the first person to speak anchors everyone else. Collect written answers separately or you learn nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before writing any Pabau positioning content, ask Pabau's sales, support, product and founders to each write one sentence on the main problem Pabau solves for aesthetic practices. If the answers diverge, that gap is showing up in the blog and on the templates pages too, so resolve it first.

**Apply anywhere:** Before writing positioning content, ask each team and the founders to independently write down the main problem you solve and how you solve it. Compare the answers, resolve the divergence into one sentence, and use that sentence everywhere.

### 7. Run content marketing as a six-stage system ending in ROI and hiring  `183.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 183*

The material Grow and Convert taught in the phone course and the workshops was a six-stage process, and the last two stages were added because clients kept asking for them. Stages one to three came from Benji Hyam's own operating experience: understand customers, build targeted content, promote it to drive targeted traffic. Devesh Khanal formalized that and added converting traffic to leads or customers, then measuring the ROI and CAC of the whole system. The sixth stage, scaling by hiring writers, plus the ROI measurement stage, were the two that exit interviews said companies could not do. Their diagnosis of the market gap is that companies were doing content marketing in a haphazard way and it was grossly ineffective, because they ran stages two and three with nothing on either end.

> "understanding customers, (2) building targeted content, and (3) promoting it"

**Evidence:** The process was taught to 15-20 companies through a $750 phone course and $1500-$3000 workshops, and the ROI and hiring stages were added because clients repeatedly named them as gaps.

**How to do it**

1. Start with customer research, not a keyword list, and write down the actual problems customers describe.
2. Build content targeted at those problems rather than at broad traffic terms.
3. Promote each piece deliberately to the audience it was written for, instead of publishing and waiting.
4. Add a conversion step: define what a reader does next on the page and instrument it.
5. Measure ROI and CAC for the channel as a whole, not traffic per post.
6. Only once the first five stages run reliably, scale by hiring writers into the documented process.
7. Audit an existing programme by asking which of the six stages is missing; most run only stages two and three.
8. Turn each recurring client complaint about the system into a documented stage, as they did with ROI measurement and hiring.

**Pitfall:** Running only the middle of the system. Producing and promoting content with no customer research in front and no conversion or CAC measurement behind is exactly what they describe as haphazard and grossly ineffective.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content programme should be audited against these six stages. The weak ends are usually stage one and stage five: article topics chosen from keyword tools rather than from practice-manager interviews, and success measured in sessions rather than demo requests per article and cost per demo.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your content programme against six stages: customer research, targeted content, deliberate promotion, conversion, ROI and CAC measurement, then scaling with writers. Most programmes run only the middle two, which is why they produce traffic and no revenue.

### 8. Run four fixed stages per article: SERP, interview, write, on-page  `102.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 102*

Grow and Convert use the same four-stage workflow for every new piece. SERP analysis: read the results page for the target keyword to work out which topics have to be covered for the article to rank. Content interview: interview a subject-matter expert inside the company to learn the topic and to get product and domain expertise into the piece, which they name as the thing that makes content truly high quality. Content writing: structure and write the article so it carries both the topical content and a compelling argument for the product, including a call to action for the next step. On-page SEO: place the target keyword and related terms in headings, subheadings and body copy. The interview stage is the one most teams cut, and it is the one they credit for quality.

> "use a 4-step process to create each piece of new content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say the expert interview is what makes content truly high quality, and run it on every new piece at their agency.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the current top-ranking pages for the target keyword and list the subtopics that appear across most of them.
2. Turn that list into the outline, adding the sections the ranking pages all missed.
3. Book a 30 to 45 minute recorded interview with the internal expert closest to the topic.
4. Ask the expert for the specifics competitors cannot copy: workflows, numbers, failure cases, customer examples.
5. Draft from the outline plus the interview transcript, weaving in how the product resolves the problem.
6. End the piece with an explicit call to action for the next step you want the reader to take.
7. Do the on-page pass last: target keyword and variants in the title, headings, subheadings and body.
8. Do not skip the interview because the writer has researched the topic; competitor summaries are what everyone else already publishes.

**Pitfall:** Cutting the expert interview to save time. The result reads as a rewrite of the SERP, ranks poorly against pages with first-hand detail, and gives the reader no reason to trust the product argument.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should require a recorded interview with a clinical or product specialist for every new article, and the transcript should be attached to the brief so the writer can quote specifics rather than paraphrasing competitors.

**Apply anywhere:** Standardize every article on four stages: SERP analysis for required coverage, a recorded interview with an internal expert, drafting from outline plus transcript, and a final on-page keyword pass. The interview is the stage that produces the quality difference.

### 9. Run paid ads to a new article while it waits to rank  `102.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 102*

Grow and Convert use paid promotion as a bridge, not as a channel in its own right. At their agency they run paid traffic to a client's newly published article while they wait for it to rank organically, which can take weeks to months depending on keyword competitiveness. The purpose is to pull conversions out of the piece during the dead period between publishing and ranking, from a targeted audience, so the investment starts returning before the organic curve arrives. They are explicit about the tradeoff: that traffic is evergreen only as long as you keep paying for each click, which is why it is a bridge rather than the plan. This pairs naturally with their bottom-funnel topic selection, since a high-intent article is worth paying for clicks on.

> "drive traffic to our clients' articles while we wait for"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run this at their agency to drive conversions from targeted audiences in the short term, before organic traffic arrives.

**How to do it**

1. Publish the article and record the date, so you can separate paid and organic performance later.
2. Pick only the high-intent articles for paid promotion; broad awareness pieces will not repay the click cost.
3. Set up a small paid campaign targeting an audience that matches the article's pain point, on the platform your buyers use.
4. Track conversions from the article separately by source so you can see paid and organic contribution side by side.
5. Check rankings monthly and taper the paid budget as the piece starts pulling organic traffic for its target keyword.
6. Stop the campaign entirely once organic conversions exceed what the paid spend was producing.

**Pitfall:** Running paid traffic to top-funnel pieces, where the click cost never returns. The signal is spend with impressions and no tracked conversions from the article.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau could put a small paid budget behind newly published bottom-funnel articles, such as software comparison and pricing pages, and taper it as rankings arrive rather than waiting months for the first conversion.

**Apply anywhere:** Put a small paid budget behind newly published high-intent articles to earn conversions during the weeks or months before they rank, then taper the spend as organic traffic arrives.

### 10. Run the five-step SEO content process from topic to on-page polish  `96.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 96*

Grow and Convert document the exact five-step sequence their agency has used across dozens of clients and hundreds of keywords. Topic comes first: content ideas and target keywords chosen for business value. Then SERP analysis, to learn what has to be covered for the piece to rank. Then structure: post type, angle and outline chosen from that analysis. Then writing, filling the structure with information that sells the product. On-page SEO comes last, not first. The ordering is the point. They argue most marketers start at step five, inserting keywords into a title tag, and treat that as doing SEO. The pre-work of steps two and three is what actually decides whether the piece ranks, and step four is what decides whether it converts.

> "It's broken down into 5 steps"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say they have used this approach to rank hundreds of articles in the top three spots for their intended keywords, over four years of agency work.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the topic from customer pain points, then find keywords that match it, rather than starting from a volume list.
2. Run a full SERP analysis of page one for that keyword before any outline exists.
3. Decide the post type, the differentiating angle and the H2/H3 structure from that analysis.
4. Write the draft with the product woven in, using company expertise rather than desk research.
5. Run the finished draft through an on-page tool to lift relevant keyword coverage.
6. Write the meta description last, once the draft is final.
7. Only then publish and move to promotion.

**Tools:** Clearscope, Surfer SEO

**Pitfall:** Skipping straight to on-page optimization. Grow and Convert say that yields results only for obscure, non-commercial keywords and will not reliably reach page one for keywords competitors also target.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO and /generate flows should keep SERP analysis and outline approval ahead of any drafting, and treat keyword-density tooling as a final polish only. If a brief reaches the writer without a documented page-one analysis, send it back.

**Apply anywhere:** Fix the order of your content process: topic, SERP analysis, structure, writing, then on-page. Keyword tooling is a last polish, not the strategy.

### 11. Run the five-step in-house expert process for every single article  `126.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 126*

Grow and Convert base almost every client article on an interview with someone inside the client's company, and they publish the exact five steps. Step one, at kickoff for each piece, coordinate with the marketing point of contact to find the best person inside the company to interview on that specific topic. Step two, analyze the SERP first and write down the holes in your own understanding as interview questions. Step three, run the interview, record it on Zoom and transcribe it with Otter.ai. Step four, run a pre-writing questionnaire and outline step so the writer and the editor agree on the arguments and the evidence before drafting. Step five, normal draft, edit, publish. They say this gets both experience-based expert detail and product positioning into the post, which lifts rankings and conversion rate together.

> "we coordinate with our marketing point of contact"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite client pieces ranking #1 for 'alternatives to standup meetings', 'delivery software for small business' and 'post-concussion nausea' produced this way.

**How to do it**

1. At kickoff, ask the client's marketing contact who inside the company handles this exact topic daily, not who is most senior.
2. Analyze the SERP for the target keyword and write down search intent, what competitors say, and how the product ties in.
3. List every gap in your own understanding that the SERP exposed as a numbered question doc for the expert.
4. Book a 45-60 minute Zoom with the named expert and record it.
5. Transcribe the recording in Otter.ai so the writer works from the expert's own words.
6. Have the writer fill a pre-writing questionnaire naming each claim and the interview quote that backs it.
7. Get the strategist or editor to approve the questionnaire and outline before any drafting starts.
8. Only then write the draft, edit, and publish.
9. Repeat the interview step per piece, not once per engagement.

**Tools:** Zoom, Otter.ai

**Pitfall:** Skipping step four. Grow and Convert warn that without the questionnaire and outline gate you end up with first drafts, or even final drafts, that are way off the mark and have to be rebuilt.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should make an internal interview mandatory on every Pabau article, not optional. Before a /generate or /SEO brief is approved, name the Pabau person to interview: a customer success manager for workflow articles, an implementation lead for migration articles, a clinician-facing account manager for treatment pages. Record on Zoom, transcribe, and require the writer to cite the transcript for each claim.

**Apply anywhere:** Base every article on a recorded interview with someone inside your own company. Pick the person who handles that topic daily, analyze the SERP first to find your knowledge gaps, turn those gaps into questions, transcribe the call, then gate the draft behind an approved outline built from the transcript.

### 12. Run the seven high-intent post frameworks as a BOFU checklist  `136.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 136*

After the Leadfeeder discovery, Grow and Convert wrote down seven content frameworks for high purchase intent posts and produced against them for eight months. They are: comparison posts against known alternatives; category tools posts that help a buyer discover the tools in a category; how-to posts teaching the manual, harder way to do what the product does; alternatives posts for someone whose current tool falls short; product use case posts for the specific solution someone searches; stories about why the product or company was built, which show the pain point and how it was solved; and case studies of a customer solving a common pain point with the product. This is a wider list than the three-framework version of pain point SEO quoted elsewhere in the base, and the last three items show that story-shaped content can carry purchase intent when the story is about your own product.

> "seven content creation frameworks that we came up with"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert produced against these seven frameworks for eight months at Leadfeeder, published almost exclusively pain point SEO posts from July 2018 onward, and saw conversions rise linearly month over month for over a year from December 2018.

**How to do it**

1. Write the seven frameworks as columns in a sheet: comparison, category tools, manual how-to, alternatives, product use case, origin story, customer case study.
2. For each column, list every specific instance your product supports: one named competitor per comparison, one named category per list, one named use case per page.
3. Check each candidate against a live SERP to confirm a buyer-stage page ranks, and drop anything where the results are all definitional.
4. Fill the comparison and alternatives columns first, since those carry the highest intent.
5. Assign one article per cell rather than combining several competitors into one page.
6. Write origin-story and case-study pieces so the pain point and the product decision are explicit, not as generic brand narrative.
7. Track signups per article by framework and retire any framework that produces none after four published pieces.
8. Move to mid-funnel how-to topics only when all seven columns are empty.

**Pitfall:** Teams read the framework list as content types to rotate for variety. It is a coverage checklist: the value comes from exhausting every named competitor, category and use case within a framework before moving on.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should map the seven frameworks onto its own backlog: one comparison page per named competitor, one category list per practice type, manual how-tos on running a clinic on spreadsheets, alternatives pages, use-case pages per feature, the Pabau origin story, and customer case studies. Any cell without a page is a gap in the funnel, not a nice-to-have.

**Apply anywhere:** Build a coverage sheet with seven columns for high-intent posts: comparison, category tools, manual how-to, alternatives, product use case, origin story and customer case study. Fill every cell with a named competitor, category or use case before writing anything mid-funnel.

### 13. Run three go/no-go questions before adopting AI for a content type  `174.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 174*

Devesh Khanal frames the AI decision as three questions about the specific content you produce, not about AI in general. Can AI produce the content that we produce better than a human on our team can? Can it educate customers on a product or industry better than a human can? Does it have unique and compelling arguments that are different from what everyone else writes on the subject? His answer to all three, for Grow and Convert's work, is no, and that is why the agency stayed unbullish on AI while competitors pushed the AI narrative. The value of the framing is that it is per content type. For surface-level introductory coverage the honest answer to all three may be yes, and then AI is a reasonable replacement. Run the test article by article rather than deciding once for the whole blog.

> "Can AI produce the content that we produce better than a human"

**Evidence:** Khanal reports the answer is no on all three questions for Grow and Convert's work, and cites this as the reason the agency did not push AI content like other content agencies and vendors.

**How to do it**

1. List your content types separately: product-led posts, comparison pages, how-tos, opinion pieces, glossary and definition pages.
2. For each type ask question one: would AI output beat what your best in-house person produces on this exact type?
3. Ask question two: can AI educate a customer about your product or industry better than someone on your team can?
4. Ask question three: does AI produce arguments on this topic that differ from what everyone else already publishes?
5. Mark a type 'AI-eligible' only when all three answers are yes, and record the reasoning in the content plan.
6. Route the AI-eligible types through AI with a light human check, and staff the rest with humans and expert input.
7. Re-run the three questions each quarter, since the answer for a given type can change as models improve.

**Pitfall:** Deciding AI policy at the company level rather than the content-type level. That produces either blanket bans that waste time on definitions pages, or blanket adoption that guts the pieces meant to sell your expertise.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should split pabau.com's output by type. Code-reference pages such as diagnostic and procedure codes are largely factual lookups where AI assistance passes all three questions. Blog articles on how a practice runs, and template pages that argue for a workflow, fail question three and stay human-led with expert input.

**Apply anywhere:** Split your output by content type and test each one against three questions: can AI beat your best person on it, can it educate customers better, and does it produce arguments different from everyone else's? Only types that pass all three go to AI. Reference and lookup pages usually pass; opinion and expertise-led pages do not.

### 14. Run three sequential filters before a writer touches a real page  `167.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 167*

Grow and Convert say the standard hiring process, read two portfolio pieces then assign a live client article, fails because portfolio pieces are not enough. Their replacement is three stacked filters. Filter one is the application: a portfolio plus a short unedited writing sample. Filter two is a paid test project that replicates real client work on a piece they have already published, so the submission can be benchmarked against their own version and against every other applicant. Filter three is several real pieces, where they say the hit rate is still not 100 percent. They frame this bluntly: no internal process or system fixes a bad writer, so the filter has to happen before onboarding, not after. They have tested hundreds of writers over five years of running the agency and filtered more than 1000 applicants.

> "the hit rate even at this stage is not 100%"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert filtered 1000-plus applicants and tested hundreds of writers across five years of agency work before settling on this three-filter process.

**How to do it**

1. Publish a writer application that collects a portfolio and one short on-the-fly writing question, rather than sourcing from a marketplace profile.
2. Filter on the portfolio first and expect to cut roughly 80 percent of applicants here.
3. Cut again on the unedited mini sample before anyone is paid.
4. Assign a paid test project that reuses a real published piece and the exact inputs you had when writing it.
5. Use the same test piece for every applicant so submissions can be benchmarked against each other.
6. Give one round of written or recorded video feedback and grade the revision, not only the first draft.
7. Move survivors onto several real pieces and accept that some still fail at this stage.
8. Track your own pass rate per filter so you know how many applicants to source for each hire.

**Tools:** Upwork, Fiverr

**Pitfall:** Collapsing the filters into one, portfolio then live article, is the failure mode they name. You find out the writer cannot do the work only after an editor has spent hours rewriting a piece that was due.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should treat any outside writer for pabau.com as unhired until they have passed a paid test brief built from an already-published Pabau article, with the internal version held back for comparison.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not hire a writer on portfolio pieces alone. Stack an application filter, a paid test project built from a piece you have already published, and a probation period of real articles before you rely on anyone.

### 15. Run topic, SERP, structure, writing and on-page in that fixed order  `115.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 115*

Grow and Convert set out their writing process as five ordered stages. Topic comes first: content ideas and target keywords that have business value, not just volume. SERP analysis comes second and answers two separate questions, which they keep distinct: which topics must be covered for the article to rank, and which content type is winning the top spots, meaning list posts, product landing pages, how-to articles or templates. Structure comes third and is derived from that analysis, both the angle and the section order. Writing comes fourth and fills the structure with information that also sells the product or service. On-page SEO and the meta description come last. The ordering is the point: teams that write first and analyze the SERP afterwards end up retrofitting subtopics into a structure the SERP never asked for.

> "Which types of content are getting top spots"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's documented five-stage process: Topic, SERP Analysis, Structure, Writing, On-Page SEO, with SERP analysis explicitly split into required subtopics and winning content type.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the topic from a keyword with business value, and write down which product capability it maps to.
2. Open the top ten results for that exact keyword before writing anything.
3. List every subtopic that appears across three or more of the top results; those are mandatory sections.
4. Separately classify the page type of each top result: list post, product landing page, how-to, template or tool.
5. Adopt the dominant page type; do not publish a how-to into a SERP full of listicles.
6. Build the outline from the mandatory subtopics plus one angle the top results all miss.
7. Write each section to fill that structure, naming the product where it genuinely answers the section's question.
8. Do on-page last: title, H1, URL and meta description built around the single target keyword.
9. Re-run the SERP analysis before any refresh, since the dominant page type shifts over time.

**Pitfall:** Collapsing the two SERP questions into one. Teams extract subtopics, miss that the top spots are all templates or product pages, and publish a well-researched article into a SERP that does not rank articles.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should record both SERP answers before an outline exists. If the top spots for a term are template downloads, the piece belongs on /templates/ with a download box rather than as a /blog/ article.

**Apply anywhere:** Follow a fixed order: topic with business value, SERP analysis, structure, writing, on-page. Split the SERP analysis into two answers, the mandatory subtopics and the winning content type, and match the format before writing a word. On-page and meta come last, built around one target keyword.

### 16. Score the mini sample on specificity, not on energy or stats  `164.3`
*core · best practices · source 164*

Grow and Convert publish both a passing and a failing mini sample so the standard is legible. The passing answer opened with a concrete benefit figure tied to the product, explained plainly what the tool does, named the mechanism, and closed with a direct call to action. The failing answer opened with a linked third-party study and a percentage, used rhetorical questions, capitalised words for emphasis, made unearned trust claims, and ended with a hype question. Their portfolio review asks the same questions: has the writer covered advanced, complex topics, can they sell a product or feature well, do they use filler and fluff sentences, and do they start pieces with needless quotes and stats. The last of these is the fastest tell, because opening with a borrowed statistic is the default move of a writer who has nothing product-specific to say.

> "Do they start pieces with needless quotes and stats?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish the exact pass and fail submissions from their Vocal Video mini sample question.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the three or four questions you will ask of every sample before you read any.
2. Ask whether the writer has handled advanced, complex topics rather than beginner explainers.
3. Ask whether they can sell a product or feature specifically, naming the mechanism.
4. Count filler sentences that carry no information and reject on volume of them.
5. Reject any sample that opens with a borrowed statistic or quote instead of the product point.
6. Reject rhetorical questions, capitalised emphasis and unearned trust claims as hype substitutes for specifics.
7. Save one passing and one failing sample as the calibration pair for anyone else scoring applications.

**Pitfall:** Grading prose quality instead of selling ability. A clean, energetic writer who cannot articulate a differentiator produces articles that read well and convert nobody.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's screening pair should be a paragraph on a real Pabau feature. Reject anything that opens with an industry statistic, since that pattern also shows up in Pabau's own weaker drafts.

**Apply anywhere:** Score writing samples against fixed questions: complex topics handled, product sold specifically, filler count, and whether the piece opens with a borrowed stat. Keep one pass and one fail sample as your calibration pair.

### 17. Score the test project against a specific-versus-fluff benchmark sentence  `167.6`
*core · best practices · source 167*

Grow and Convert give the exact standard they hold test submissions to, using a hypothetical best accounting software brief. The passing shape is: most accounting software has X issues which fail to solve Y pain points, our software solves those pain points in Z way. The failing shape is: running a business is hard, after you have done HR and sold to customers you sit down after a long day and now you have got to do the books. They call the first specific and the second general fluff content. This is the operational definition behind the mirage content problem they describe earlier, where a writer googles the topic and regurgitates what they read into their own article. Having the two model sentences written down turns a subjective quality call into something two reviewers can agree on.

> "The first response is specific."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contrast the specific and fluff versions of an opening for a best accounting software piece as their working grading standard.

**How to do it**

1. Write your own version of the passing sentence in the X, Y, Z shape: most tools have X problem, which fails to solve Y pain point, our product solves it in Z way.
2. Write a matching fluff example from your own niche, the empathy-preamble opening that says nothing.
3. Put both in the test brief scoring sheet, not in the brief itself.
4. Score each submission by asking which of the two shapes its opening paragraphs resemble.
5. Reject submissions where the product claim could be swapped for any competitor without changing a word.
6. Reject submissions that establish the reader's day rather than the product's differentiator.
7. Reuse the same two model sentences for every reviewer so scores stay comparable.
8. Reuse them again in writer onboarding, so the standard is taught rather than discovered.

**Pitfall:** Mirage content: the writer googles the topic and regurgitates what they read. It reads fluently and passes an unstructured review, so without a written benchmark sentence it gets published.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's writer scoring sheet should carry one model sentence naming a Pabau differentiator against a named competitor, and one banned-style example of generic practice-life preamble.

**Apply anywhere:** Write one model sentence showing the specific product framing you want and one showing the fluff version. Score every test submission against those two rather than on general quality.

*core · best practices · source 163*

Grow and Convert's fourth vetting question follows from the buying-intent priority. ### 24. Sequence use-case content: case studies, then features, then pain points  `83.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 83*

Grow and Convert give an explicit build order for the content that wins invisible prompts. Create content for as many specific use cases and scenarios as possible. Start with the places and ways you shine. Start with actual case studies. Then move to features, then to the pain points you solve, then to the specific situations where your product or service is the right fit. Their point is that you cannot predict the prompts a priori, so coverage of situations replaces prediction of phrasings. They say their financial sector client was never targeted, they had simply written for ten years about why most content marketing fails to generate leads and how they fix it. The order matters because case studies carry the most situational detail per page.

> "Start with actual case studies. Then move to features"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say they never created content for their financial sector client's prompt; ten years of writing about how content marketing fails to generate leads was what got them recommended.

**How to do it**

1. List every customer segment you have served and the outcome you produced for each.
2. Publish one case study per segment first, naming company size, industry, prior tools and the specific result.
3. Next publish one page per feature, written around the job it does rather than the specification.
4. Then publish one page per pain point you solve, in the words customers use for it.
5. Then publish pages for specific fit situations, such as switching from a named competitor or opening a second location.
6. Name the segment, size and constraint explicitly in each page's opening two sentences, since that is the text an LLM matches against.
7. Track which segments have zero pages and treat those as the backlog.
8. Review quarterly against newly collected customer chats and fill whatever situation is missing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's ordering should be case studies from real practices first, then feature pages framed as jobs, then pain-point pages, then fit-situation pages such as moving off paper records or consolidating a multi-site clinic. **Apply anywhere:** Build case studies first, then feature pages framed around the job each feature does, then pain-point pages, then fit-situation pages. Case studies carry the most situational detail per page, which is what AI tools match against.

### 25. Shape whole articles around one hour-long recorded expert interview  `178.10`
*core · content insights · source 178*

Grow and Convert draw a hard line between quote-harvesting and their interview model. They are explicit that they are not talking about grabbing a few quotes from experts to drop into an article. They run hour-long recorded interviews and shape the entire article around one internal expert's viewpoint and knowledge, choosing someone who can speak to the topic and tie in the product. The writer is then not asked to pretend to be an expert, which they call a massive shift from traditional content services. What the format buys is copy on detail no outside writer could reach: how the product is differentiated, what it replaces, and why specific features were designed the way they were. That last item is the tell. An article that explains a design decision could only have come from an interview.

> "the writer is not asked to pretend to be an expert themselves"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this as a massive shift from traditional content services and the basis of their thought-leadership output.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the one internal person who can speak to the topic and connect it to the product.
2. Book a full hour and record it; treat shorter calls as quote-gathering, not sourcing.
3. Ask specifically what the product replaces, and why each relevant feature was designed that way.
4. Build the outline from the expert's viewpoint after the call, not from a SERP-derived template.
5. Write the article around that viewpoint rather than inserting quotes into a pre-planned structure.
6. Include at least one design-decision explanation per article as the proof the interview happened.
7. Send the draft back to the interviewee for accuracy, not for style.

**Pitfall:** Running the interview after the outline is fixed. The expert's answers then get trimmed into a structure built from other people's articles, and the piece reverts to a research paper with quotes.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau feature and workflow articles should be outlined after the product interview, not before, and each should carry one explanation of why a Pabau feature works the way it does.

**Apply anywhere:** Record a full hour with one internal expert, build the outline from what they said, and make sure each article explains at least one product design decision only an insider could know.
