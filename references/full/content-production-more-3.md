### 2. Expect the portfolio filter to remove about 80% of applicants  `164.16`
*useful · best practices · source 164*

Grow and Convert put a number on where their hiring funnel narrows: the portfolio stage is where the vast majority, maybe 80%, of writer applicants are filtered out. That figure matters for planning, because it sets how many applications you need to reach a shortlist and how much of the funnel is unpaid screening versus paid testing. They pair it with a second reality check at the far end: even after the portfolio, mini sample, paid test and feedback round, the hit rate on real pieces is not 100%, and a decent number of writers still do not work out. They call this a numbers game, and their claim is only that the filters put you in a lot better shape than the standard portfolio-then-live-article approach.

> "maybe 80%) of writer applicants are filtered out"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report roughly 80% of applicants are filtered out at the portfolio stage, from 1000-plus applications reviewed.

**How to do it**

1. Plan applicant volume backwards from the 80% portfolio cut rather than from a target hire count.
2. Keep the portfolio review fast and criteria-based so cutting 80% costs little time.
3. Budget paid test projects only for the roughly 20% that clear both application filters.
4. Assign several real pieces, not one, before deciding a writer has worked out.
5. Expect losses even at the real-piece stage and keep a second candidate warm.
6. Re-run the funnel periodically rather than treating hiring as a one-off event.

**Pitfall:** Running the funnel with too few applicants. With an 80% cut at stage one, a dozen applications leaves you two or three paid tests and no margin for the failures that still happen on real pieces.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau opens a writer round, David should aim for at least 40 applications so the funnel still yields two or three testable candidates after the portfolio cut.

**Apply anywhere:** Expect about 80% of writer applicants to fail the portfolio review, and expect some losses even after a paid test. Size your applicant pool from that ratio rather than from your target hire count.

### 3. Fill a fixed outline questionnaire before expanding any bottom-funnel draft  `140.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 140*

Lewis begins each article by filling out the Grow and Convert SEO Questionnaire, which produces the outline. She describes what it forces: thinking through the points she wants to make, cutting through jargon, and getting to content that is actually helpful and could affect whether the reader requests a free audit. That last criterion is the useful one, because it makes the outline answer to the conversion, not to the keyword. Only after the outline is complete does she expand it, and she expands it in a specific way, by backing up claims with facts, giving real life examples, and hitting the pain points that matter to that audience. The order matters: outline against the conversion, then expand with evidence, then optimize with the scoring tool.

> "cut through jargon, and get down to the content"

**Evidence:** Lewis used the G&C SEO Questionnaire on every article in the ADA Compliance Pros campaign that took blog leads from zero to 5-15 a month in four months.

**How to do it**

1. Fill a fixed questionnaire per article before drafting: target keyword, audience, pain points, angle and the conversion the piece should drive.
2. Write the outline as the points you will make, not as headings copied from the SERP.
3. Strike any jargon term the interview showed the buyer does not use.
4. Test each outline section against whether it moves a reader toward the conversion offer; cut the ones that do not.
5. Expand each section by attaching a fact, a real example or a named pain point.
6. Only then write the full draft, and only then run the content-scoring pass.
7. Keep the completed questionnaires so later refreshes start from the original angle.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** An outline built from competitor headings inherits their structure and their omissions, and produces a piece with no view. The signal is an outline where no section would change a reader's buying decision.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should add the conversion test to Pabau's outline step: for each planned H2, say how it moves a practice owner toward booking a demo. Sections that fail get cut before the writer starts, not in review.

**Apply anywhere:** Fill a fixed questionnaire before every draft covering keyword, audience, pain points, angle and the conversion the piece should drive. Build the outline from the points you want to make, strip jargon your buyers do not use, and cut any section that would not move a reader toward the offer. Expand with facts and real examples, then draft, then optimize.

### 4. Flip content sentiment negative when positive won't rank  `18.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 18*

The tactic: when a positive/affiliate-toned page about a brand or product won't rank, try rewriting it with a negative or cautionary sentiment instead (why to avoid the brand, its downsides) and use that page to cross-sell competing affiliate offers as the recommended alternative. The source frames this as one example of testing 'intent or sentiment' as a lever distinct from mechanical on-page factors (word count, H2 count, TF-IDF density), which it explicitly dismisses as outdated thinking. The broader diagnostic given is binary: if the page isn't indexing at all, the problem is a missed search intent (rewrite and republish); if it's indexed but not ranking for the right terms, changing the sentiment/angle is the suggested next lever to pull.

> "writing about why it's bad, and get the clicks"

**How to do it**

1. Identify a page/topic where a positive, affiliate-toned article isn't ranking for its target keyword despite reasonable effort.
2. Check indexing status first via Google Search Console URL Inspection — if it's not indexed at all, the issue is a search-intent mismatch, so rewrite to match intent and republish.
3. If it is indexed but not ranking for the intended terms, rewrite the page with a negative or cautionary sentiment instead (e.g. 'downsides of [Brand]'), staying factual and non-defamatory.
4. Within the negative-angle piece, cross-sell alternative products or affiliate offers as the 'better' option, replacing the original monetization path.
5. Republish and monitor Google Search Console for indexing and ranking movement on the target keyword.
6. Compare performance against the original positive-framed version and keep whichever angle ranks and converts better. (inferred verification/decision step)
7. Treat this as one of several intent/sentiment variants to test on underperforming pages, rather than a one-time fix.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Defaulting to mechanical on-page fixes (word count, H2 count, TF-IDF density) instead of testing a different search intent or content sentiment; keep any negative-angle content factual and non-defamatory.

### 5. Generate one or two illustrations per post via Napkin, export WebP  `07.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 07*

For adding visual variety to a finished draft without design skills, the free tool Napkin can auto-generate an illustration directly from pasted article text: paste a paragraph, click generate, and it proposes a styled visual that can be restyled or edited before export. Because Napkin exports as PNG by default, and heavy PNGs slow page load (which matters for both UX and Lighthouse scoring), every export should be converted to WebP with a free converter before uploading to WordPress. The creator recommends doing this once or twice per post, not on every section, to avoid the page looking like a slideshow of AI illustrations rather than a written article.

> "It creates an illustration to help make your point"

**How to do it**

1. Finish the article draft and identify one or two sections that would genuinely benefit from a visual explainer, not every section.
2. Create a free account at Napkin.
3. Select 'Create by pasting text.'
4. Copy the relevant paragraph(s) from your draft and paste them into Napkin.
5. Select the pasted text and click 'Generate visuals.'
6. Review the auto-generated illustration options, choose a style matching your site's branding, and edit individual elements as needed.
7. Download the result (note: Napkin exports as PNG by default).
8. Run the PNG through a free PNG-to-WebP converter before uploading — do not upload the raw PNG.
9. In WordPress, insert an image block at the relevant point in the post and upload the converted WebP file.
10. Limit this to one or two illustrations per article. (inferred: re-check the page's Lighthouse performance score after adding images to confirm load time wasn't degraded)

**Tools:** Napkin, WebP converter, WordPress

**Pitfall:** Uploading Napkin's default PNG export directly to WordPress — a heavy PNG will slow the website down; always convert to WebP first since page speed is scored directly in Lighthouse.

### 6. Generate topics straight from the pain point list, nothing else  `122.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 122*

Grow and Convert note that once the research is done, ideating content is not complicated. They spell out why by contrasting each research step with what most companies do. You have narrowed the full customer list to a subset based on actual data, where most companies write for anyone in their general field. You have talked to multiple stakeholders to check your reasoning, where most employees work in silos. You have uncovered pain points by talking to real customers, where most marketers assume. With those three inputs in hand, the ideation step is simply creating pieces that address the actual pain points uncovered for those ideal customers. There is no separate brainstorming exercise.

> "ideating on content is not that complicated"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe ideation as easy once the three research steps are complete, and attribute most companies' failure to skipping all three.

**How to do it**

1. Put the pain point list from the customer interviews in a sheet, one row per pain point.
2. Write one candidate article title per pain point, phrased in the customer's own words.
3. Delete any candidate that did not come from a pain point row, including ideas from a keyword tool alone.
4. Attach the verbatim customer quote to each candidate as the brief's opening evidence.
5. Rank candidates by how many best-fit customers named that pain point.
6. Keep each piece narrow: one pain point per article, not a broad roundup.
7. Only after this, check search volume and adjust the title wording to match how people search.

**Pitfall:** Reverting to a keyword tool for ideation after doing the research. Volume-first lists reintroduce the broad, beginner-level topics the research was meant to eliminate.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial calendar should be built from a pain point sheet sourced from clinic interviews and tickets, with keyword volume applied afterwards to phrase the title, not to pick the topic.

**Apply anywhere:** Build the editorial calendar from the researched pain point list, one article per pain point, and apply keyword data only to refine the title after the topic is chosen.

### 7. Get a screen-share demo on every interview and mine it for images  `134.8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 134*

Grow and Convert treat the demo as non-optional, and say Zoom makes it trivial because the expert just shares their screen and clicks around. Their argument is that a visual helps you retain the information and usually makes the thing seem far simpler than it sounded in words. There is a second payoff: the recording is an image source. You review the recording, grab screenshots of the product screens that demonstrate each feature, and although those grabs are usually too low-resolution to publish, you send them back to the expert and ask them to recreate the shot on their own screen. They note this is often much easier than describing in words the image you want. The rule extends past software. If the client works in a factory, take a Zoom or FaceTime tour of it. If they sell a service, have them walk through every stage of a client relationship and show the end results.

> "the demo is often a great way to source images"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert used interview-sourced product screens in the Rainforest QA articles.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the expert to share their screen at the start of the interview, not at the end.
2. Have them perform the workflow the article covers, clicking through it rather than describing it.
3. Record the session and note timestamps whenever a screen matches a planned article section.
4. Review the recording afterwards and screenshot every one of those screens.
5. Send the low-resolution grabs back to the expert and ask them to recreate each one at full resolution.
6. For non-software clients, request a video tour of the facility or a walkthrough of each stage of a client engagement plus the end results.

**Tools:** Zoom, FaceTime

**Pitfall:** Describing the screenshot you want in words. Grow and Convert say the round trip is far slower than sending back your own rough grab, and you usually get the wrong screen twice before the right one.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau writers should record a screen-share of the exact Pabau workflow an article covers, screenshot the relevant screens from the recording, and ask the product team to recreate them at full resolution for the article. Each one still needs a caption saying how the feature helps the reader.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask for a screen-share demo on every expert call, record it, pull rough screenshots of the screens you need, and send them back so the expert recreates them at publishable resolution.

### 8. Give new posts three months before judging whether they rank  `116.13`
*useful · best practices · source 116*

Grow and Convert exclude the most recent three months of published posts from any audit of whether content is ranking and converting. Their stated reason is simply that blog articles take time to rank, so a post published six weeks ago carries no information about whether the strategy is working. In their client audit this took the denominator from 71 published posts to 60 assessable ones, and the resulting figure, 31 of 60 converting at least monthly, was the number they used to judge the blog's health. The rule cuts both ways. It stops you writing off pages that have not had a chance, and it stops a burst of recent publishing from making the ratio look worse than it is.

> "Blog articles take time to rank, so we excluded 3 months"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cut a 71-post inventory to 60 assessable posts by excluding three months, and reported 31 of 60 converting at least monthly.

**How to do it**

1. Add a published date column to your content inventory.
2. Exclude every post published in the last three months from ranking and conversion tallies.
3. Report the ratio of converting posts against the reduced denominator only.
4. Keep the excluded posts on a watchlist with a review date at the three-month mark.
5. Re-run the audit monthly so posts roll into the assessable set as they age.
6. Never assign a refresh or a rewrite to a post inside the three-month window.
7. State the exclusion when reporting the ratio so the number is comparable month to month.

**Tools:** Google Sheets, GA4

**Pitfall:** Reporting a converting-post ratio that includes recent publishing. The more you publish, the worse your blog looks, which pushes teams to rewrite pages that were fine.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's monthly content report should exclude articles published in the last three months from any ranking or conversion percentage, and hold them on a watchlist instead. Otherwise a heavy publishing month reads as a decline.

**Apply anywhere:** Exclude articles published in the last three months from any ranking or conversion percentage in your monthly report, and hold them on a watchlist instead.

### 9. Give writers feedback every time or lose the quality curve  `167.18`
*useful · best practices · source 167*

Grow and Convert name absent feedback as the fifth common mistake. Many of their writers report that at previous clients they handed over a draft and got back thanks, or I do not like it, and nothing else. Their argument is mechanical: if you never give feedback and never share your editing process, the writer has no way to know whether they did a good job and no way to improve, so they will not. That is why feedback is built into the process at two points, one graded round during the paid test project and continuous coaching afterwards from the content strategist. The cost of skipping it is paid twice, in an editor rewriting the same problems on every piece and in a writer who never becomes cheaper to work with.

> "share your editing process with them, how will they know"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report that many of their writers had received no feedback beyond thanks or I do not like it from previous clients.

**How to do it**

1. Send every writer the tracked-changes version of their edited draft, not only the published URL.
2. Add one comment explaining the reasoning behind the largest edit in each piece.
3. Hold a short recurring review, for example after every third article, covering repeated issues rather than single sentences.
4. Keep a running list of that writer's recurring problems and check the next draft against it.
5. Say what worked as well as what did not, so the writer can repeat it.
6. Move a writer off the roster when the same corrections keep appearing after explicit feedback.
7. Treat feedback time as part of the article's cost and schedule it, rather than fitting it in when there is time.

**Tools:** Google Docs

**Pitfall:** Silently rewriting drafts because it is faster than explaining. The editing burden never falls, and the writer reasonably assumes their work was accepted as delivered.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial pass should return the diff and a short note to whoever wrote the article, including when that writer is an internal team member, so corrections compound instead of repeating.

**Apply anywhere:** Return your edits and the reasoning to the writer on every piece. Without that, the writer cannot improve and your editing cost stays flat forever.

### 10. Grade the mini sample on specificity, not energy  `167.4`
*useful · content insights · source 167*

Grow and Convert publish both a passing and a failing mini sample, which makes the grading standard concrete. The passing paragraph leads with a specific claim, names the mechanism, then closes with a plain call to action: video testimonials convert 25 percent more buyers, traditional production takes time and money, VocalVideo lets you collect responses through a shared link with templates and auto-generates a branded video you can embed. The failing paragraph does the opposite. It opens with a borrowed Vidyard statistic, uses all-caps emphasis, rhetorical questions, invented enthusiasm like your sweet wonders, and closes with are you still letting doubt stop you. Grow and Convert say that second submission immediately told them the writer was not a fit. The difference is not grammar or energy. It is whether the writing explains the product or performs at the reader.

> "write a short paragraph highlighting the software"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert print the two contrasting VocalVideo submissions, one from a writer who joined the team and one they rejected on sight.

**Pitfall:** Mistaking punchy, high-energy copy for good direct-response writing. Rhetorical questions, all-caps and borrowed stats read as persuasive to an untrained reviewer and are the exact signature of a writer who has not understood the product.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David reviews sample copy for Pabau, the pass test is whether the paragraph names a specific Pabau mechanism and outcome, not whether it sounds enthusiastic about aesthetic practices.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge writing samples on whether they explain a specific mechanism and outcome. Borrowed stats, all-caps and rhetorical questions signal a writer who is performing rather than explaining.

### 11. Hand AI only the content where expertise is not being sold  `174.6`
*useful · best practices · source 174*

Khanal concedes a clear zone where AI is a good replacement: largely surface-level coverage of introductory topics. He defines the zone by what the writing is for, not by how easy it is. It is writing where you do not care about selling your personal or your company's expertise to someone else, and where you do not care about having your company or CEO's opinion woven into the piece. He adds that the writers and agencies producing this kind of mirage content will be replaced, and that this is fair, because their approach and strategy was flawed before AI existed. The useful move is to sort your calendar by that test, because it stops the argument about AI in the abstract and turns it into a per-page routing decision.

> "largely surface-level coverage of introductory topics"

**Evidence:** Khanal states he absolutely agrees AI could be a good replacement for surface-level coverage of introductory topics, and expects the agencies producing that kind of work to be displaced.

**How to do it**

1. Tag every planned page with the funnel stage and the job it does: educate a stranger, or persuade a buyer.
2. Ask of each page whether your company's expertise or your founder's opinion needs to appear in it.
3. Route pages where the answer is no into the AI-assisted lane with a factual accuracy check.
4. Route pages where the answer is yes to a human writer with an expert interview booked before drafting.
5. Cap the AI lane's share of the calendar so the blog does not drift entirely into introductory coverage.
6. Review the AI lane quarterly for pages that started ranking, and upgrade those with expert detail.

**Pitfall:** The AI lane quietly expands, because introductory topics are easy to plan and cheap to produce. A blog that is mostly definitions and overviews has no page that sells expertise, and nothing for an LLM to cite you differently for.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's diagnostic-code and procedure-code reference pages sit squarely in the AI-assisted lane, since they sell no opinion. Blog articles about running an aesthetic practice and template pages arguing for a workflow do not. David should hold the reference lane to accuracy checks and keep expert input mandatory on the rest.

**Apply anywhere:** Sort your calendar by whether the page has to sell your expertise or carry your opinion. Pages that do not, such as definitions and reference lookups, can run through an AI-assisted lane with an accuracy check. Everything else needs a human writer and an expert interview. Cap the AI lane so the site does not become all introductory coverage.

### 12. Hire a full-time content promoter once publishing is consistent  `173.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 173*

KlientBoost doubled down on content production and hired someone whose full-time job was promoting content, which Grow and Convert describe as building systems around the engine that fuels growth. The split matters: production and promotion are separate jobs, and the promotion role only appears once there is a steady stream of published work to promote. It sits alongside the partnership program, so the promoter has co-produced ebooks, webinars and speaking engagements to place, not just blog posts to tweet. This is the structural version of Dane's wider rule that when something works you commit rather than dip a toe. At the time of the interview the company had 25 employees and over 16 account managers, so the promotion hire was made while headcount was still tight.

> "hired someone to promote content full time"

**Evidence:** KlientBoost hired a full-time content promoter at roughly 25 employees, alongside a program of co-produced ebooks, webinars and speaking.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm you are publishing on a reliable cadence before hiring for promotion.
2. Write the role as promotion and distribution only, with no writing quota attached.
3. Give the role ownership of partner outreach, guest posting and speaking submissions, not just social.
4. Set its targets on referring domains, partner placements and referral sessions rather than posts published.
5. Feed it the co-produced assets from partnerships so it has something bigger than blog posts to place.
6. Review at six months on links earned and partner distributions secured.
7. Keep production and promotion as separate seats even when budget is tight, rather than one hybrid role.

**Pitfall:** Merging promotion into the writer's job means promotion is always what gets dropped when a deadline slips. The signal is published articles with no outreach attached to any of them.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau's publishing cadence is steady, the next marketing hire should own distribution: partner co-productions with aesthetics vendors, guest posts, and getting Pabau cited on the comparison and review sites that drive AI citations. David should not fold this into a writer's brief.

**Apply anywhere:** Once you publish reliably, make promotion a dedicated full-time seat with its own targets on links, partner placements and referral traffic. Promotion folded into a writing role is always the part that gets dropped.

### 13. If forced to one homepage keyword, hit five spots  `41.5`
*useful · concrete actions · source 41*

If you insist on targeting exactly one keyword with the homepage, Edward's method is to pick a single umbrella, bottom-of-funnel keyword, his example is a local service term like 'roofing repair Austin,' and place it in the H1, the page title, the beginning of the first sentence, the meta description, and the alt text of the homepage's top image. Because a homepage has no URL slug of its own to carry keyword relevance the way an internal page would, he has you compensate by placing the keyword in these extra locations, which would otherwise be considered over-optimization on a normal page. Even done this way, he still warns you're running the same underlying risk described elsewhere: you're optimizing the page that gets the most non-SEO-channel visits for keyword relevance rather than for conversion, so he still recommends leading the H1 and hero section with your brand's core benefits rather than a keyword whenever possible.

> "the beginning of the first sentence, the meta description, and"

**How to do it**

1. If you must target a single keyword on the homepage, select one umbrella, bottom-of-funnel keyword phrase that best represents your core offer, rather than several separate keyword targets.
2. Place that exact keyword phrase in the homepage's H1.
3. Place the same keyword phrase in the page title tag.
4. Open the first sentence of the homepage's body copy with that keyword phrase.
5. Include the keyword phrase in the meta description.
6. Include the keyword phrase in the alt text of the top or hero image on the homepage.
7. Since the homepage has no URL slug to carry additional relevance signal, treat these five placements as the full extent of on-page optimization and do not add further keyword repetition beyond this.
8. Still prioritize writing the H1 and hero section around your brand's core benefit or value proposition where possible, accepting some tension between the keyword placement and pure conversion copy.

**Pitfall:** Even when done correctly with just one keyword in the five recommended locations, you're still optimizing the page that gets the most visits from non-SEO channels for keyword relevance instead of conversions, so you still risk losing leads on your highest-traffic page - the overall recommendation remains to avoid targeting any keyword on the homepage if you can instead target it with a dedicated page.

### 14. Interview around a dozen internal experts across a long SaaS engagement  `131.9`
*useful · best practices · source 131*

Grow and Convert close the Smartlook case study by noting that nearly a dozen subject matter experts inside the company lent their time to explain the product and the problems it solves for customers. That is the input that made the deep comparison content possible: the competitor nuances, the feature use cases and the advanced product analytics detail were not researched from other blogs. For an engagement covering 50 target keywords across category, comparison and jobs-to-be-done terms, roughly one expert per four or five pieces is the working ratio. The signal that this is missing from a program is comparison content that repeats the competitor's own marketing copy instead of naming where the competitor falls short.

> "nearly a dozen subject matter experts generously lent their time"

**Evidence:** Nearly a dozen Smartlook subject matter experts contributed across the 16-month engagement covering 50 target keywords.

**How to do it**

1. Name the internal experts who own each product area before the content calendar starts, not per article.
2. Book a recurring interview slot with each one rather than chasing ad hoc calls.
3. For each comparison piece, interview the person who handles competitive deals and ask where the competitor genuinely wins.
4. For each feature piece, interview the person who supports that feature and ask for the two customer problems it most often solves.
5. Record and transcribe every session and keep a searchable library so later pieces reuse it.
6. Send the draft back to the interviewed expert for a factual pass before publishing.
7. Track which experts have been used so the load stays spread and coverage does not skew to one product area.

**Pitfall:** Running an entire SaaS content program on desk research produces comparison articles that restate competitor marketing. Nobody in the sales team recognizes the piece as how the product actually differs.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should line up named internal experts per module, onboarding, support, and the clinical advisory side, and book recurring interview slots rather than sourcing product detail from documentation alone. Comparison articles especially need input from whoever handles competitive deals.

**Apply anywhere:** Line up named internal experts per product area at the start of a content program and book recurring interviews, rather than researching product and competitor detail from documentation and other blogs.

### 15. Judge long-term writer fit on four behaviors, not on one draft  `167.16`
*useful · best practices · source 167*

Grow and Convert give four questions to ask while a writer moves through the hiring process and the first real pieces. Can they respond well to feedback, can they make sensible revisions, do they hit deadlines, and can they write on multiple types of brands and topics if that matters to your business. Writers showing those traits, they say, will likely work out long term. The list is deliberately behavioral rather than about prose. It matches the rest of their process, where the revision after feedback is graded separately from the first draft, and where filter three exists because some writers who pass every writing test still fail on real work. Deadlines and revision behavior only surface once someone is doing live pieces, which is why filter three is a real stage rather than an onboarding formality.

> "Write on multiple types of brands/topics"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list the four traits as their predictor of which writers work out long term, after five years of testing hundreds of writers.

**How to do it**

1. Turn the four traits into an explicit scorecard: feedback response, revision quality, deadline adherence, and range across brands or topics.
2. Score them during the test project and again across the first several live pieces.
3. Only include the range criterion if you genuinely need one writer across several topics or clients.
4. Record deadline misses from the very first assignment, since the pattern shows early.
5. Distinguish a writer who patches your specific comments from one who applies the underlying principle.
6. Set a decision point, for example after three live pieces, rather than letting a marginal writer drift on.
7. Keep the scorecard for future hires so you can compare candidates across years.

**Pitfall:** Deciding on writing quality alone. A writer who produces good drafts but misses deadlines or argues with every edit costs more editor time than a slightly weaker writer who revises cleanly.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should score Pabau's outside writers on turnaround and on whether fact-check corrections are absorbed into the next article, not only on the quality of the first draft.

**Apply anywhere:** Score writers on four behaviors across their first live pieces: feedback response, revision quality, deadlines, and range. Those predict long-term fit better than any single draft.

### 16. Judge whether a topic needs an expert before assigning a freelancer  `160.13`
*useful · best practices · source 160*

Grow and Convert warn against hiring freelance writers with the expectation that you give them a topic and the post comes back the way you want it. Their reasoning is about detection, not quality: when a writer with no subject matter expertise writes for a customer who has been in the industry for years, the customer can tell, and instead of building trust the article turns the customer away. Their fix is to reframe the writer as a journalist who produces marketing content, sending them to interview people inside the company or outside experts on a specific topic and turning those interviews into articles. The addition to the general advice is the trust argument: an expertise gap actively costs you the reader, it does not merely produce a bland page.

> "the customer can tell and instead of building trust, it turns the customer away"

**How to do it**

1. Score each planned topic for how much industry-specific knowledge a reader will expect the author to hold.
2. Assign only low-expertise topics to a writer working solo from research.
3. For every high-expertise topic, book a 30 to 45 minute interview with an internal expert or an outside practitioner first.
4. Give the writer the recording and require they build the article around what was said, including the specifics only a practitioner would know.
5. Review the draft for any claim the interview did not support, and cut it rather than smoothing it.
6. Never hand a high-expertise topic to a writer with only a keyword and a word count.

**Pitfall:** Briefing an unbriefed freelancer on a specialist topic. Industry readers spot the missing detail immediately, and the article loses trust rather than building it, which is worse than not publishing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's readers are practitioners and practice managers, so clinical and operational topics should be built on interviews with clinic staff or Pabau's own onboarding team. David should treat any draft on a clinical workflow with no practitioner input as unpublishable.

**Apply anywhere:** Score topics by how much expertise readers expect, and never send a specialist topic to a writer working from research alone. Have writers interview practitioners first, because industry readers spot the gap and lose trust in the brand.

### 17. Keep a short competitor list so you are the only page that compares  `71.17`
*useful · content insights · source 71*

Devesh nearly cut a short section mentioning other content brief generators, because none of the ranking pages talked about other tools and he did not want a list post. He kept it on a specific reasoning: they could mention the competitors briefly, and Google might treat the page as the only thing that lists multiple different content brief generators. The logic is that when every incumbent page is a single-tool landing page, a page that surveys the field occupies a slot nobody else fills, without becoming a listicle. He keeps the mention deliberately short so the article stays a teardown with a product argument, not a roundup. It is a gap-filling move driven by what the SERP does not contain, rather than by what it does.

> "the only thing that lists multiple different content briefs"

**Evidence:** Devesh kept a brief competitor mention in the 'AI content brief generator' piece specifically because no ranking page mentioned other tools, reasoning Google might value the only page listing several.

**How to do it**

1. After reading the top 10, list the content types nobody in the SERP has published.
2. Check specifically whether any ranking page compares multiple products or whether they are all single-product pages.
3. If none compares, add a short comparison section to your piece rather than a full listicle.
4. Keep the mention brief, a sentence or two per competitor, so the page keeps its primary angle.
5. Name each competitor explicitly with a checkable detail such as a plan limit, so the comparison is real.
6. Track whether the page picks up impressions on comparison-style queries as the test of whether the gap was worth filling.

**Tools:** Google

**Pitfall:** Extending the short comparison into a full listicle changes the page type and puts you back in competition with the same format everyone else has. The signal is a comparison section longer than the product section.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau blog topics where every ranking page is a single-vendor page, a short honest comparison paragraph naming two or three alternatives can fill a gap without turning the article into a listicle.

**Apply anywhere:** When every ranking page covers only its own product, add a short comparison of two or three named alternatives. It fills a format gap without turning your page into a listicle.

### 18. Keep a topic log so new posts don't duplicate old ones  `49.10`
*useful · best practices · source 49*

A classic cannibalization pattern on service-based sites (the example given is a plumber) is a strong but thin main category page (e.g., "Plumbing") competing against a weaker but more detailed subpage covering the same service — near-duplicate content split across two pages of different strength. The two fixes are to differentiate them (genuinely different titles and content angles) or merge them (301-redirect the one you don't keep), but the recommended prevention is procedural: maintain documentation of what topics have already been covered and check it before publishing any new piece, so you don't unknowingly create a near-duplicate of existing content.

> "best way to avoid it in the first place is proper documentation"

**How to do it**

1. Before publishing any new page, maintain and consult a running content/topic log (spreadsheet or CMS tag list) recording every keyword or topic already covered sitewide.
2. When a new piece's proposed topic closely resembles an existing log entry, decide upfront whether it needs a genuinely different angle and title or should instead be folded into the existing page.
3. If proceeding as a separate page is justified, make the title and content substantively different from the existing page's, not just a longer or shorter version of the same topic.
4. If the topics are too similar to justify two pages, merge the new content into the existing page (or vice versa) and 301-redirect the one not being kept.
5. For large sites with many pages already rotating or competing, treat full retroactive cleanup as lower priority than preventing new instances via the documentation check.

**Pitfall:** Publishing a detailed subpage covering the same service already summarized on a strong main category page creates near-duplicate content that splits rankings between a stronger thin page and a weaker detailed one, even without either page being a literal copy.

### 19. Keep news posts short and spend the effort on being accurate first  `168.6`
*useful · best practices · source 168*

Nii Ahene is explicit that CPC Strategy's industry news posts were not long. The team focused on quality over quantity, and the single most important task was putting out accurate information fast. This is a direct counterweight to word-count-driven content programs. For a news post the value is the fact, the date and the number, and every extra paragraph delays publication without adding anything a reader wants. It also protects the asset: the blog earned its position as a trusted news source, and one wrong rate-card number would cost more trust than ten long posts would build. The trade-off only works in a niche where you have first-hand knowledge, because speed without a primary source is how errors get published.

> "focused on quality over quantity"

**Evidence:** Nii Ahene says CPC Strategy's news posts were not long and that putting out accurate information fast was the most important task.

**How to do it**

1. Set a hard cap on news posts, around 200 to 400 words, and treat anything longer as a different content type.
2. Lead with the change itself in the first sentence: what changed, from what to what, effective when.
3. Require a primary source link, meaning the vendor's own page, filing or statement, before the post can publish.
4. Have a second person verify the specific figures, since a wrong number in a news post is the one error that costs the audience.
5. Timestamp the post and update it in place when the vendor clarifies, rather than publishing a second post.
6. Skip publishing entirely if you cannot verify within the same day, rather than publishing an unsourced version.
7. Reserve long-form effort for the data reports and evergreen guides the news posts link into.

**Pitfall:** Padding a news post to hit a word count delays it past the window where being first matters, and it dilutes the fact readers came for.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau publishes regulation or platform-change posts, they should be short and dated, not stretched into 1,500-word guides. The depth belongs in the evergreen template and code-reference pages those posts link to.

**Apply anywhere:** Cap news posts at a few hundred words, require a primary source and a second pair of eyes on every figure, and put the long-form effort into the evergreen pages the news links to.

### 21. Lengthen a nurture sequence in steps and settle where results hold  `155.19`
*useful · concrete actions · source 155*

Erika did not design the eight-email course up front. Grow and Convert record that she sent users through a five to eight email autoresponder, kept experimenting with longer and longer series, and has currently settled at eight. The starting material was a series she had already been running for over a year, which she rebuilt with roughly 15 hours of additional work. The value of the incremental approach is that each added email is tested against the ask that follows it, so the sequence length is decided by what the sequence produces rather than by a template. It also means the ask positions move as the sequence grows, which is why the hard sell sits at email 6 of 8 rather than at a fixed day count.

> "she kept experimenting with longer and longer series"

**Evidence:** Erika moved from five emails to eight through repeated experiments, having started from a year-old series.

**How to do it**

1. Launch the sequence at five emails so it starts producing data quickly.
2. Keep the hard sell in the second-to-last third of the sequence rather than at a fixed day number.
3. Add one lesson email at a time and let it run for a full cohort before judging.
4. Check opens, clicks and product sales per cohort after each addition.
5. Stop lengthening when an added email fails to raise sales or holds open rates flat.
6. Reposition the soft mentions after each change so they stay at the first, middle and hard-sell slots.
7. Retire the weakest-performing lesson instead of only adding new ones.
8. Record the settled length and the ask positions so the next sequence starts from a proven shape.

**Tools:** Mailchimp

**Pitfall:** Fixing the sequence length before launch means the ask positions are guesses, and adding emails later shifts them without anyone re-checking where the hard sell now lands.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should launch any new nurture sequence at five emails and extend it one at a time, judging each addition on demo requests rather than opens.

**Apply anywhere:** Launch a nurture sequence at five emails, extend it one email at a time, and stop when an added email no longer lifts sales.

### 22. Lift a Clearscope grade from B to A without keyword stuffing  `96.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 96*

Grow and Convert's final step is a content-grader pass, and they are specific about how they use it. Once the draft is complete, they run it through Clearscope to check they are using enough of the relevant keywords, meaning the keywords that appear repeatedly in the existing top content. At that stage they typically sit in the B range, and they look for ways to include further keywords to push the score into the A range, ideally A+. They say this requires finesse and often creative ways to include keywords that do not fit naturally. The hard limit is that where a keyword genuinely does not make sense, and no creative placement works, they leave it out. They warn that stuffing keywords unnaturally sacrifices content quality, turns off readers, and is no longer SEO-friendly because Google's algorithm can pick up on it.

> "we'll typically have a content grade in the B-range"

**Evidence:** The TapClicks piece reached an A+ Clearscope grade for 'paid search dashboard' and ranks at position 2.

**How to do it**

1. Finish the full draft before opening the grading tool at all.
2. Run the draft through Clearscope or a similar tool against the target keyword.
3. Expect a B-range grade on the first pass and treat that as the starting point.
4. Work through the missing terms and add the ones you can place in a sentence that reads naturally.
5. Prefer placements in subheadings and body prose over forced repetition of the exact phrase.
6. Stop and leave a term out whenever no natural placement exists, even if the grade stays short of A+.
7. Never lower readability to raise the grade.

**Tools:** Clearscope, Surfer SEO

**Pitfall:** Chasing A+ mechanically. Forced terms read as stuffing, turn readers off, and Grow and Convert say Google's algorithm now detects it, so the grade improves while the page gets worse.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can use a grader as a final coverage check on /blog/ and /templates/ drafts, but the house sentence rules and voice guide win on any conflict. A missing term is acceptable; a forced one is not.

**Apply anywhere:** Grade the finished draft, expect a B, and lift it by placing terms that fit naturally. Drop any term that has no natural home.
