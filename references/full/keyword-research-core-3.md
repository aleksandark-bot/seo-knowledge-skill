### 1. Expect rivals to abandon the misconception keyword out of pride  `109.3`
*core · content insights · source 109*

The mechanism that makes deviant keywords profitable is competitor self-selection. In the renewable heating case, the author found that other vendors were just as annoyed by the geothermal misconception and had avoided the terms for the same reason his client wanted to. The result was that the wrong phrasing was materially less competitive in the SERPs than the correct product descriptions, despite carrying comparable commercial intent. The stronger the technical culture in an industry, the wider this gap gets, because the whole peer group polices the same vocabulary. He frames the lesson as not being precious about your product. If searchers use certain language to describe their needs, be present for those searches, then use the page itself to explain what they actually need.

> "many of their competitors were similarly frustrated by the popular misconception"

**Evidence:** Geothermal heat pump keywords were much less competitive in UK SERPs than the accurate air-source and ground-source terms, because vendors avoided them.

**Pitfall:** Wrong terms sometimes get auto-corrected by Google to the accurate query, which collapses the opportunity. Check the live SERP before committing, because Google defaults to the correct search on some terms and not others.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau finds a term aesthetic practitioners use that the industry considers wrong, check whether competitor blogs avoid it. If they do, that is a lower-difficulty route into a high-intent SERP.

**Apply anywhere:** When customers use an incorrect term for your product, your competitors are likely avoiding it out of the same professional pride you feel. That makes the wrong phrasing less contested than the accurate one at similar intent. Verify the live SERP has not been auto-corrected, then publish.

### 2. Expect side-offering keywords to convert at a quarter of your core terms  `142.3`
*core · content insights · source 142*

Grow and Convert found that category keywords around a secondary feature or use case average 1.94%, against 4.85% for main category keywords describing exactly what you sell. Their worked example is a digital asset management client. A post targeting storage of video assets converts at 1.45%, while posts targeting general DAM keywords for the same client convert at 3%, 6% and even 15%. Their reasoning is that a searcher who only needs video storage can buy something simpler than a full DAM, so the query does not match the product. They also list other causes: the side offering may be weak, may not be purchasable separately, or the rest of the marketing site may not sell it as hard as the core product. The rule they draw is that intent alignment with what the product does, not copy quality, sets the conversion rate.

> "it converts at 1.45%"

**Evidence:** 1.94% average for side category keywords against 4.85% for main; one DAM client's video-asset post at 1.45% against general DAM posts at 3%, 6% and 15%.

**Pitfall:** Product marketing pushes content for every newly launched feature. Those posts convert at roughly a quarter of core-category posts, so a roadmap dominated by feature launches looks busy and produces few demos.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau launches a module, David should not assume the supporting article will convert like the core practice-management posts. Budget it at roughly a quarter of the rate and keep the core category terms funded first.

**Apply anywhere:** When you launch a secondary feature, do not assume its supporting article converts like your core category posts. Budget it at roughly a quarter of the rate and keep core category terms funded first.

### 3. Filter JTBD keywords by whether the job needs software to finish  `142.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 142*

Jobs-to-be-done keywords averaged 2.44% in Grow and Convert's study, but the spread is the point. Terms where completing the job requires buying a tool converted extremely well: 'collect video testimonials' at 9.71%, 'how to run standups in Slack' at 12.5%, 'record website visitors' at 10.48%. Terms describing advice rather than a task converted at a fraction of that. For the same video marketing client, keywords about collecting or gathering testimonials converted at 5 to 9%, while a keyword about what to say in a testimonial video converted at 0.6%, because nobody buys software to learn what to say. So the usable filter is not funnel stage or volume, it is whether the searcher needs a product to complete the job they described. That single test separates a 10% JTBD keyword from a 0.6% one.

> "they just need advice"

**Evidence:** 9.71% for 'collect video testimonials', 12.5% for 'how to run standups in Slack', 10.48% for 'record website visitors', against 0.6% for a keyword about what to say in a testimonial video.

**How to do it**

1. List the jobs your product completes, in the words a customer would type, mostly as how-to queries.
2. For each candidate, ask whether finishing that job realistically requires buying software.
3. Discard any query whose answer is advice, opinion or wording help rather than a task.
4. Keep queries where the task is exactly what your product does, and mark those as priority.
5. Check what page type ranks for each keeper to confirm a commercial post can rank.
6. Write each post to solve the job first, then name your product as the way to do it.
7. Track conversion per post and expect the require-a-tool group to sit between 5% and 12%.
8. Move any post converting under 1% out of the priority list and stop commissioning that shape of query.

**Pitfall:** Treating all how-to queries in your space as one bucket. Advice-shaped JTBD queries convert around 0.6% and will drag the whole program's average down while looking like legitimate bottom-funnel work.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, prioritize JTBD queries like sending appointment reminders or taking consent forms digitally, where the job needs software. Deprioritize advice queries such as what to say during a consultation.

**Apply anywhere:** Prioritize jobs-to-be-done queries where finishing the job needs software, and deprioritize advice-shaped queries in the same topic area, even when the volume looks better.

### 4. For software, bottom-of-funnel keywords are the whole stack you sit in  `61.5`
*core · best practices · source 61*

Cody's definition of what bottom-of-funnel means for a software company, with the strategic frame around it. The patterns are X versus Y, X alternative, how to X, best X for Y - and they work because the searcher is in a buying mindset, but also because you can include your own brand inside those articles. His example: write 'Rippling vs Deel', and inside it note that you have a tool integrating with Rippling that automates payroll taxes. Get that post to page one or two and when someone searches the comparison, you've created a stronger likelihood of appearing in the AI results for it too. The broader instruction is about coverage of your stack: if you're a landing-page builder, you want content across every keyword related to landing pages, funnel building, Facebook ads - whatever appears in the workflow your product fits into. You are a tool within a stack, so you want content on every channel and platform relating to every part of that stack.

> "Bottom-of-funnel keywords are super effective"

**How to do it**

1. Map the workflow your product sits inside and list every other tool and step in it.
2. Generate the four bottom-of-funnel patterns against those: X vs Y, X alternative, how to X, best X for Y.
3. Include the comparisons between other tools in your stack, not just comparisons involving you.
4. In each of those articles, place your own product contextually as the thing that connects or automates part of it.
5. Add a CTA on each page aimed at the searcher's actual intent, which is choosing between the two tools named.
6. Extend the same coverage to the other channels and platforms where that workflow is discussed.

**Pitfall:** Writing comparisons between two competitors you don't feature in only works if your product genuinely relates to the workflow - otherwise it's traffic with no path to conversion.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should be writing the comparisons between the other tools in a clinic's stack - booking, payments, marketing, records - not only the ones featuring Pabau, because those pages let Pabau appear as the thing that joins them up.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the comparisons between the other tools in your customer's stack, not only the ones featuring you - those pages let you appear as the thing that joins them up.

### 5. Generate bottom-funnel keywords from five fixed SaaS phrase templates  `111.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 111*

Grow and Convert give the exact patterns they use to build a bottom-of-funnel list for SaaS: use case plus software, top or best plus use case plus software, use case plus tools, industry plus software, and industry plus use case plus software. For service businesses they swap 'software' or 'tools' for 'service'. They add a sixth pattern from the Circuit engagement: 'apps for industry or target customer', as in 'apps for delivery drivers'. These are the terms companies normally point a homepage or product page at. Grow and Convert argue you should target them with blog content instead, because a list-style post satisfies the intent better and often outranks home and product pages. Their reason is blunt: no brand will put a list of competitor products on its product page, but a blog post can.

> "Use Case + Software"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert used these templates for Geekbot ('daily standup software'), Leadfeeder ('best visitor identification software', 'lead generation tools', 'sales intelligence tools') and Circuit ('route dispatch software', 'apps for delivery drivers').

**How to do it**

1. Write out your product's use cases and the industries you sell into as two short lists.
2. Expand every combination through the five templates: use case + software, top/best + use case + software, use case + tools, industry + software, industry + use case + software.
3. Add the 'apps for [industry or job title]' pattern, such as 'apps for delivery drivers'.
4. For service businesses, substitute 'service' or 'services' for 'software' and 'tools' throughout.
5. Check each generated phrase in Ahrefs for volume, but keep sub-20-volume phrases on the list.
6. Open the SERP for each survivor and note whether listicles or product pages rank.
7. Where listicles rank, assign a blog listicle rather than optimizing the product page.
8. Include competitor products in that listicle, since that is the format advantage a product page cannot copy.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Pointing these terms at the homepage or a product page. The SERP usually wants a comparison list, and a product page cannot include competitors, so it loses the intent match.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's keyword sheet should be built by expanding these templates over its use cases and specialties, then each survivor becomes a /blog/ listicle that names competitors, not another paragraph on a product page.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your bottom-funnel list mechanically from phrase templates over your use cases and industries, then target the ones with listicle SERPs using blog roundups that include competitors.

### 6. Generate bottom-funnel terms from integrations, jobs and artifacts  `115.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 115*

Grow and Convert's bottom-funnel examples for their trades-software client are not random long-tail phrases. They follow three visible generation patterns. 'HVAC software compatible with QuickBooks' is category plus an integration. 'Electrical bidding software' is a single job the software does, named the way the trade names it. 'Plumbing invoice app' is the artifact the user wants to produce, plus the form factor. All three sit at 50 to 260 searches a month and all three indicate someone actively shopping. The pattern is reusable: take your vertical noun, then cross it against the tools your customers already run, the discrete jobs your product performs, and the documents or outputs it generates. That produces a long bottom-funnel list without touching a keyword tool's suggestion feed, which tends to return informational variants.

> "HVAC software compatible with QuickBooks"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's three leads-focused examples for the trades client: 'HVAC software compatible with QuickBooks' (50/mo), 'electrical bidding software' (260/mo), 'plumbing invoice app' (50/mo).

**How to do it**

1. Write down your vertical noun as your customers say it, not as your industry says it.
2. List every third-party tool your product integrates with, taken from your own integrations page.
3. Cross the vertical noun against each integration: 'X software compatible with Y', 'X software that integrates with Y'.
4. List the discrete jobs your product does, one verb each, from your feature pages.
5. Turn each job into 'job + software' and 'job + app' phrasings.
6. List the documents and outputs your product creates: invoices, quotes, consent forms, reports.
7. Turn each artifact into 'artifact + app', 'artifact + software', 'artifact + generator'.
8. Run the whole set through Ahrefs for volume, and keep everything at or above 50 searches a month plus the sub-20 terms that name your category exactly.
9. Check the SERP for each survivor and drop any where the results are all informational articles rather than product pages.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Using a keyword tool's related-terms feed as the generation source instead of your own product surface. The feed returns 'what is HVAC software' style informational variants, and the integration and artifact terms never appear because their volume is too low to surface.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run all three patterns for Pabau. Integrations gives 'clinic software that integrates with Xero' and 'practice management software with Stripe'. Jobs gives 'patient recall software' and 'treatment consent software'. Artifacts gives 'aesthetic consent form app' and 'clinic invoice software'. Each becomes its own page, not a section.

**Apply anywhere:** Build bottom-funnel keyword lists from your own product surface rather than a keyword tool's suggestions. Cross your category noun against your integration list, your discrete feature jobs, and the documents your product produces. These terms sit at 50 to 260 searches a month and indicate active shopping.

### 7. Generate long-tail terms by layering modifiers onto your category keywords  `104.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 104*

Grow and Convert's first discovery tactic starts from the category keyword, meaning any term that names the exact type of product you sell: 'time tracking software', 'time tracking platform', and so on. From each of those, you produce long-tail variants by adding either an adjective or a 'for X' qualifier. Their worked examples are 'time tracking software for personal use', 'for construction sites', 'for remote work', plus adjective forms like 'easy to use time tracking software', 'online time tracking software' and 'secure time tracking software'. They say this single tactic reliably produces far more keywords than teams expect. The value is that every one of these terms still names the product category, so the buying intent from the head term is preserved while the competition drops.

> "you would add adjectives or layers of specificity"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: 'You can usually find a lot more keywords than you think just by using this one tactic.'

**How to do it**

1. List every noun phrase that names your product category, including synonyms your buyers use: software, platform, system, tool, app.
2. For each category term, generate 'for X' variants across customer segment, company size, industry, use case and location.
3. Generate adjective variants for the attributes buyers actually shortlist on: easy to use, online, secure, affordable, HIPAA-compliant, mobile.
4. Cross the two lists so segment and attribute variants both exist for each category noun.
5. Drop any variant naming a segment you do not want as a customer, even where volume is good.
6. Check volume and keyword difficulty for the survivors in Ahrefs or Semrush, keeping terms with no reported volume in the list.
7. Assign one dedicated page per surviving variant and write it only about that segment or attribute.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Adding the qualifier to the title and then publishing the same generic category article underneath. The SERP already has broad pages ranking on that qualifier, so a broad page with a narrow title wins nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's category terms are practice management software, clinic software and EMR. David should systematically cross those with the segments Pabau wins in and the attributes buyers shortlist on, then build a page for each combination.

**Apply anywhere:** Take every term that names your product category, then multiply it across segment qualifiers and attribute adjectives. Each surviving combination gets its own page written specifically for that segment.

### 8. Get in early on niche terms whose volume is about to grow  `95.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 95*

Matt Goolding shows two cases where a mini-volume keyword was an early position in a growing niche. Circuit's post on starting a medical delivery service had sub-20 volume when they chose it; in the 9 months after publication it drove 31 free trial signups, and since it only reached page 1 in month 3 that is 31 signups in 6 months, about 5 a month. Over the following months many variations of 'medical delivery service' appeared and growth on the exact-match target sped up. For Geekbot, terms that were sub-20 when targeted now register more volume: 'best slack poll app' now shows 40 monthly searches and its article has generated more than 300 signups. Geekbot's niche took off when lockdowns accelerated remote work. Getting in before competitors notice the growth is the advantage.

> "you can get in early before competitors realize search volume is growing"

**Evidence:** Circuit's medical delivery service post: 31 free trial signups in 6 months of page-1 ranking. Geekbot's 'best slack poll app' article: over 300 signups, and the keyword now reads 40 monthly searches.

**How to do it**

1. List the emerging use cases, regulations or product categories your customers have started mentioning in the last 6 months.
2. Turn each into keyword phrasings and check them in Ahrefs; expect sub-20 or zero readings.
3. Cross-check the term in Google Trends to see whether interest is rising even where the tool shows nothing.
4. Publish a dedicated article per rising term rather than waiting for volume to appear.
5. Re-pull the volume figure for those terms every quarter and log the change.
6. When a term's reported volume climbs, expand the article and add internal links, since you already hold the ranking.
7. Track how many keyword variations of the term appear over time as a signal the niche is growing.
8. Report the early bets separately so the payoff is visible when volume arrives a year later.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Trends

**Pitfall:** Betting on a trend that does not arrive leaves you with a page nobody searches for. Keep these bets a minority of the calendar and pick trends your own customers are already raising.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish early on emerging aesthetic treatments and regulatory changes while the terms still read zero in Ahrefs. Holding the ranking before volume arrives is much cheaper than entering the SERP after competitors have.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish against emerging terms in your niche while tools still show sub-20 volume. When the volume arrives you already hold the ranking, and competitors have to displace you rather than the reverse.

### 9. Harvest category keywords from what competitors already bid on in PPC  `179.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 179*

Grow and Convert start every engagement by locating as many keywords as possible in three buying-intent categories, and they give a concrete discovery source for the first one. Product or service category keywords are the top terms most businesses in the space are already bidding on through paid search and social ads, and the obvious terms any business would want to rank for. Their examples are "Shopify development services" for a Shopify dev agency, "marketing analytics software" for a marketing analytics SaaS, and "executive assistant service" for a remote EA staffing company. The second category is competitor comparison terms: "competitor vs your brand", "competitor vs other competitor", and "competitor alternatives". The third is pain point phrases indicating the searcher has a problem the product solves. They target as many of these as possible before moving up the funnel.

> "The top terms that most businesses are bidding on through PPC ads"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say competitors spending real money on a term in search and social ads is itself the signal that the term carries buying intent.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the paid keyword report for your three closest competitors in Ahrefs or Semrush and export every term they bid on.
2. Keep the terms that name your product or service category and drop branded and irrelevant ones; this is your category bucket.
3. Build the comparison bucket by generating 'X vs Y' for every competitor pair including yours, plus 'X alternatives' for each competitor.
4. Build the pain point bucket from the problems your product solves, phrased the way a customer would type them, not as topic labels.
5. Do not filter any of the three buckets by search volume at this stage; intent decides inclusion.
6. Assign one dedicated page to each surviving keyword and publish through the three buckets before writing anything higher in the funnel.
7. Only move to lower-intent, higher-volume topics once the high-intent list is exhausted.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Skipping the PPC export and taking category terms from a keyword tool's suggestions instead. Grow and Convert warn that the tools most agencies use almost always surface high-volume, low buying-intent terms, which is how the traffic-first strategy takes hold.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the category bucket is what competitors bid on around practice management and clinic software, the comparison bucket is Pabau versus each named competitor plus alternatives pages, and the pain point bucket comes from problems aesthetic practices bring to sales calls. Publish through all three before adding general aesthetics advice posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your keyword list from three high-intent buckets before touching volume. Take category terms from what competitors actually bid on in paid search and social, build comparison terms from every competitor pairing plus alternatives queries, and write pain point phrases the way customers would type the problem. Exhaust all three with dedicated pages before moving up the funnel.

### 10. Ignore the volume estimate on competitor keywords, it is systematically low  `133.5`
*core · content insights · source 133*

Grow and Convert's Circuit page targeting 'routific competitors' and 'routific alternatives' is their proof case. Both terms showed 0 to 10 monthly searches in Ahrefs and 10 per month in Clearscope. The naive math says ranking first at a 30% click rate yields about six visitors a month across both terms, which reads as not worth writing. The page ranked second for both and took 80 pageviews in March, 71 of them organic, producing six first-click conversions. That is a 7.5% conversion rate from one blog post. Their explanation has two parts. Volume estimates for competitor keywords are almost always under-estimated, and any published page also picks up a spread of long-tail terms that individually show no volume but together carry most of the traffic. They add that competitor keywords have done well across many clients because they are low competition and extremely high intent.

> "they are almost always under-estimated"

**Evidence:** Circuit's /routific-competitors-alternatives page: target terms showed 0-10 volume in Ahrefs and 10 in Clearscope, but the page ranked #2 for both, took 80 pageviews in March with 71 organic, and produced 6 first-click conversions, a 7.5% conversion rate.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Applying a minimum-volume filter to a keyword list before checking intent. That filter deletes exactly the competitor and alternatives terms that convert best, on numbers the tools admit are estimates.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not cut competitor and alternatives pages from the plan on the basis of a low Ahrefs volume. Build a page for each named practice management competitor even where the tool reports single-digit volume, and judge them on demo requests rather than sessions.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not screen competitor and alternatives keywords on estimated volume. Those estimates run low, and the page will also collect long-tail terms the tool never shows. Judge these pages on conversions, not sessions.

### 11. Industry jargon rarely matches how customers actually search  `07.5`
*core · content insights · source 07*

The creator identifies a core keyword-research failure mode: people who know their industry well default to technical/internal terminology, which frequently diverges from how an actual customer phrases a search query. His worked example: an EV-charger installer might technically call the service an 'EVSE installation,' but the customer searches 'Tesla charger installation' — both are 'correct' descriptions of the same service, but only one matches real search demand. The lesson generalizes to any B2B/technical niche: the internally correct term and the commercially searched term can be entirely different strings, and only the second should drive page titles, H1s, and targeting. This is presented as one of the most common and costly keyword-research mistakes, ahead of any tool-usage mistake.

> "that's how your customer is searching for it"

**Evidence:** Worked example: 'an expert might call that technically an "EVSE installation." Sure, but your consumer might actually be searching for "Tesla charger installation."'

**Apply at Pabau:** Before finalizing target keywords for any Pabau page, David should check actual keyword-tool search volume for the internal/product term Pabau uses against plainer customer phrasing, and default to whichever phrase carries real demand even if it feels less precise internally.

**Apply anywhere:** Before finalizing target keywords for any page, you should check actual keyword-tool search volume for the internal/product term you use against plainer customer phrasing, and default to whichever phrase carries real demand even if it feels less precise internally.

### 12. Insert your product into competitor-versus-competitor comparison keywords  `106.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 106*

Grow and Convert split comparison keywords into two shapes. The first is '[competitor] alternatives and competitors', where the searcher already uses a tool and wants to switch, or is evaluating one tool and wants options. The second is '[competitor 1] vs [competitor 2]'. Their rule for the second shape: if you can find a versus keyword where your own software is one of the two named options, target those first, and they show Smartlook ranking first for 'smartlook vs hotjar'. But they say to go after versus keywords where your product is not named at all, simply by adding your software to the comparison. Smartlook ranks third for 'hotjar vs fullstory' by doing this. Both shapes catch buyers at the moment they are choosing.

> "Simply add your software to the comparison"

**Evidence:** Smartlook ranks #1 for 'smartlook vs hotjar' and #3 for 'hotjar vs fullstory', a keyword where its own product is not one of the named options.

**How to do it**

1. List every named competitor in your category, including ones you rarely lose to.
2. Generate '[competitor] alternatives' and '[competitor] competitors' for each name.
3. Generate '[competitor] vs [your brand]' pairs and check which already have search demand.
4. Publish the pages where your brand is one of the two named options first, since intent is highest.
5. Then generate '[competitor 1] vs [competitor 2]' pairs among rivals you are absent from.
6. SERP-check each pair to confirm the ranking pages are third-party comparisons rather than vendor pages only.
7. Write the comparison covering the two named tools honestly, then add your product as a third option with the criteria where it wins.
8. Track rankings for these pages separately, since position three on a versus keyword still converts.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Writing a comparison that only exists to promote you. If the two named tools are not covered fairly the page reads as an ad, and third-party comparisons already ranking will beat it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build a comparison page for every named competitor pair in aesthetics practice management, including pairs Pabau is not part of, and add Pabau as the third option with the criteria where it wins.

**Apply anywhere:** Build comparison pages for both shapes: '[competitor] alternatives' and '[competitor 1] vs [competitor 2]'. Prioritize versus keywords where you are already one of the two names, then write yourself into the pairs you are absent from.

### 13. Interview buyers by asking them to draw a timeline of how they got here  `75.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 75*

The article opens with Bob Moesta's Detroit condo research, and the interview technique is the transferable part. Moesta interviewed people who had already bought and asked them to "draw a timeline of how they got here." Demographics and psychographics showed no pattern, and no feature set tipped decisions. The timeline surfaced something nobody had asked about: buyers kept talking about their dining room table. What blocked a move was not features but the fear of giving up memories. Moesta's conclusion was that the company was in the business of moving lives, not new-home construction, and it changed the product to make room for dining tables and added moving services. The lesson for content teams is that the blocking job is found by reconstructing the buyer's timeline, not by profiling the buyer.

> "draw a timeline of how"

**Evidence:** Moesta's Detroit apartment project: no demographic or feature pattern among buyers, but repeated unprompted mentions of the dining table; the company added table space and moving services and sales rose.

**How to do it**

1. Recruit six to twelve customers who bought recently, not prospects, since only buyers can reconstruct a completed decision.
2. Ask each one to draw or narrate a timeline from the first moment they felt something was wrong to the day they signed.
3. Mark on the timeline what triggered each step and what stalled them between steps.
4. Do not ask which features they valued, and do not collect demographics as an explanatory variable.
5. Listen for the object or task people mention repeatedly and unprompted, the equivalent of the dining room table.
6. Write the blocking job as a sentence about what the customer was afraid to give up or unable to resolve.
7. Restate your business in those terms, for example moving lives rather than building homes.
8. Turn each stall point on the timeline into a content brief that removes that specific anxiety.

**Pitfall:** Interviewing prospects instead of buyers, or asking about features. Both produce the pattern Moesta found useless, since prospects said they did not need a dining room while buyers talked about the table constantly.

**Apply at Pabau:** Run timeline interviews with six to twelve practices that bought Pabau in the last quarter. The stall points, such as fear of losing years of patient records or of staff refusing a new system, become the briefs that Pabau's blog currently does not cover.

**Apply anywhere:** Run timeline interviews with recent buyers rather than prospects. Ask them to draw the path from first problem to purchase, mark every stall, and turn each stall into a content brief.

### 14. Interview current users to find which segment stays and spends most  `139.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 139*

Before picking a single keyword for Keeper Tax, Brandon interviewed the company's existing users rather than starting from a volume list. The interviews split customers into two groups: independent contractors just starting out, and established freelancers such as consultants, part-time marketers, developers and small Etsy sellers. The research showed the established contractors stayed longest and spent the most on the subscription. That finding, not search volume, decided which pain points became content. The same interviews surfaced the recurring worry that drove every later article: these workers did not have a good grasp of how to handle their taxes, and wanted confidence that every base was covered. Brandon treated the retention and spend answer as the tiebreak whenever two pain points competed for the same publishing slot.

> "their best customer who stays with them the longest and spends the most money"

**Evidence:** Keeper Tax: interviews identified veteran marketers, consultants and developers as the highest-value segment, which then shaped the whole keyword list.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the client for a list of current paying customers segmented by tenure and monthly spend.
2. Interview 8-15 of them, mixing new and long-tenured accounts, and record the calls.
3. Ask what they were doing before the product, what they tried, and what they were afraid of getting wrong.
4. Group the answers into segments and mark which segment has the longest tenure and highest subscription spend.
5. Write the winning segment's exact wording into a persona note that every keyword must serve.
6. List the recurring worries from that segment verbatim as candidate pain points.
7. Reject any keyword idea that a member of the winning segment would not plausibly type.
8. Re-run the interviews once a year, because the best-fit segment shifts as the product changes.

**Pitfall:** Teams interview whoever answers the phone and end up optimizing for the cheapest, fastest-churning segment. If your persona note has no tenure or spend figure attached, you have not done this step.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should have Pabau interview the practices that renew longest and pay most - likely multi-location aesthetic groups rather than solo injectors - and let their worries pick which blog and template pages get built next.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview your existing customers before building a keyword list, and let the segment with the longest tenure and highest spend decide which pain points you write about.

### 15. Interview sales and success to define the customer you refuse to target  `104.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 104*

Grow and Convert say the answers from your sales and customer success teams should be the foundation of keyword research, not an input to it. Those teams know first-hand which challenges your ideal customers face, which parts of the product resonate immediately, and what separates a good customer from a bad one. The last of those is the part most teams skip. Their example is an HR software company that sells to large organizations: it should target 'HR software for enterprise' and deliberately not target 'HR software for startups', even though the second term exists and has traffic. The output of the interviews is therefore two lists, not one. A list of pain points and product strengths to build pages around, and an exclusion list of segments whose traffic you do not want.

> "it's very helpful to talk to your sales team"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's HR example: target 'HR software for enterprise' and not 'HR software for startups' when small companies are not good clients.

**How to do it**

1. Book 45 minutes each with a senior sales rep and a customer success manager.
2. Ask which three problems come up most often in discovery calls, and record the customer's own phrasing verbatim.
3. Ask which product capability makes a prospect lean in during a demo, and which one closes deals.
4. Ask them to describe the worst-fit customer they have onboarded and why that account went badly.
5. Turn the pain points and phrasings into a jobs-to-be-done keyword list.
6. Turn the strengths into qualified category terms that name your differentiator.
7. Write down the bad-fit segments as an explicit exclusion list attached to the keyword sheet.
8. Delete any keyword naming an excluded segment before checking its volume, so the traffic never tempts anyone.
9. Re-run the interviews once the first ten pages have conversion data attached.

**Pitfall:** Collecting only the pain points and skipping the bad-fit question. Without an exclusion list, a high-volume segment term looks attractive on the sheet, ranks, and fills the funnel with leads sales cannot close.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should interview Pabau's sales and onboarding teams and write down the practice types that churn or never convert. Those segments then get excluded from keyword sheets, so Pabau does not build pages that pull in the wrong practices.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview sales and customer success for pain-point phrasing, for the features that close deals, and for the customer profile you regret selling to. The third answer becomes an exclusion list your keyword sheet enforces.

### 16. Interview sales and support for the wrong term customers keep using  `109.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 109*

Grow and Convert's clearest deviant-keyword discovery method is not a tool. Working with a UK renewable heating company, the author asked the sales and customer service teams what frustrated them, and learned that callers kept asking about 'geothermal' heat pumps. In the UK a geothermal system means an industrial borehole drilled deep toward the earth's core, not a residential product. The term was wrong, but it was the language buyers used. He built content for 'residential geothermal heat pump', 'geothermal heat pump installation cost', 'water to water geothermal heat pump' and 'best geothermal heat pump'. The team's expertise was actively hiding the opportunity, because the people who knew the technology best were the ones most offended by the phrasing. Frontline staff hear the misconception dozens of times a week and never think to report it as a keyword.

> "speaking to the sales and customer service teams uncovered something"

**Evidence:** The renewable heating client's geothermal content contributed to 8x growth in conversions and 3,400% growth in high-intent organic traffic over two years.

**How to do it**

1. Book 30 minutes with the sales team and 30 with support, separately, before any keyword tool is opened.
2. Ask one question: what do prospects call our product that makes you wince, and how often?
3. Write down the exact wording they quote, including abbreviations and misused technical terms.
4. Ask a second question: what do people assume we do that we do not, and what do they assume we do not do that we do?
5. Put every wrong phrase into a keyword tool and pull volume plus its variants.
6. Check the SERP for each: if the top results are generic explainers rather than vendors, the term is uncontested.
7. Confirm the searcher would still convert by asking sales whether callers using that phrase ever buy.
8. Write the page in the searcher's wording, then correct the terminology inside the article body.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Subject-matter experts veto the term on accuracy grounds and the opportunity dies in review. Expect to have to argue for it, and bring the SERP screenshot showing competitors have avoided it too.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this interview with Pabau's sales and onboarding teams and record every phrase aesthetic practice owners use that the team considers wrong. Terms like 'clinic booking system', 'salon software' or 'medspa CRM' may be technically off but are how buyers search. Those become blog and template targets.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview your sales and support staff for the phrases customers use that your experts consider incorrect. Those misused terms often carry real buying intent and, because rivals find them embarrassing, weaker competition. Build pages in the customer's wording and correct the terminology inside the article.

### 17. Invert keyword research: interview four internal teams before opening Ahrefs  `113.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 113*

Grow and Convert say the easy route is to use Ahrefs or Semrush intent classifications, volume and difficulty, but that a better route for companies starting out is to invert the process entirely. Start from customer pain points, then find the words people use when searching for solutions, then validate volume in the tools. They name four specific internal sources: the sales team, for the exact problems prospects raise in discovery calls; support tickets, for what customers struggled with before they found you; the product team, asked what problem each feature was built to solve and what alternatives customers used before; and customers directly, surveyed with the question of what they would Google to find your product or solve the problem it solves. Volume validation comes last, and low numbers are not a reason to drop a topic.

> "invert this process entirely"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report their highest-converting posts have targeted keywords the tools showed at zero search volume.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the sales team for the exact problems prospects name in discovery calls, in the prospect's own words, not the internal feature name.
2. Read the last three months of support tickets and log what customers say they struggled with before they found you.
3. Interview the product team feature by feature: what problem was this built to solve, and what were customers using instead beforehand.
4. Survey customers with one question: what would you type into Google to find a product like ours, or to solve the problem it solves.
5. Write each finding as a search phrase a person would actually type, not as an internal category label.
6. Plug the resulting phrases into Ahrefs or Semrush only now, to validate volume and check difficulty.
7. Keep phrases the tools score at zero volume if they came from a real customer, because tools underestimate long-tail high-intent terms.
8. Check the top 10 results for each surviving phrase to confirm the page types ranking indicate buying intent.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Starting in the keyword tool anchors you to whatever has volume, which is nearly always the beginner-level informational phrasing. The signal you have made this mistake is a keyword list that no salesperson recognizes.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau this means interviewing the sales and onboarding teams about what aesthetic and healthcare practices say in demo calls, and reading support tickets, before any keyword list is built. Feed those phrases into the brief, then validate volume last.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview sales, read support tickets, question the product team feature by feature, and survey customers on what they would Google. Turn the answers into search phrases, then validate volume in a keyword tool last.

### 18. Judge keyword demand from four signals, not just volume  `11.4`
*core · best practices · source 11*

Gotch calls search-volume-first keyword selection 'one of the biggest newbie mistakes' and reframes the core question as proven demand, assessed across four signal types: traditional search volume from Google Ads Keyword Planner, Google Search Console impressions (noisy because bot traffic inflates them, but still a valid demand proxy for topics you already rank for), user signals from Reddit and Quora, and first-party data from direct customer contact such as sales calls, coaching calls, and Q&A sessions, where real questions people ask in person count as proof of demand even if no tool shows it. Volume is explicitly demoted to 'just one variable among many' rather than the deciding factor.

> "Go to Reddit, go to Quora, go anywhere users are engaging"

**How to do it**

1. For any candidate keyword, check Google Ads Keyword Planner for raw search volume as one data point, not the deciding one.
2. Pull the same topic or URL's impressions from Google Search Console's Performance report, filtered by query, as a second demand signal, discounting slightly for likely bot impressions.
3. Search the topic on Reddit and Quora for real user discussion volume as a third signal.
4. Log any first-party evidence of demand, such as questions asked on sales calls, coaching sessions, or support tickets, as a fourth, tool-independent demand signal (inferred: keep a running note or CRM tag for recurring customer questions).
5. Only exclude a topic from the working list if it scores weakly across all four signals, not just because Keyword Planner shows zero or low volume.

**Tools:** Google Ads Keyword Planner, Google Search Console, Reddit, Quora

**Pitfall:** Relying on Keyword Planner volume alone and dismissing zero-volume topics — Gotch stresses this is a huge mistake especially for local and long-tail queries where real demand exists but doesn't yet register in the tools.

### 19. Keep the deviant test as conversion likelihood, never traffic  `109.14`
*core · best practices · source 109*

Grow and Convert draw a hard line around the deviant-keyword idea to stop it becoming a licence for traffic chasing. They are explicit that this is not about writing content to rank for random keywords in order to generate traffic, which is tempting when a competitor's visitor numbers look impressive. Website traffic by itself is a vanity metric. They would rather have modest traffic with a high conversion percentage, which is also why they target mini-volume keywords for clients. A deviant keyword is a non-intuitive or seemingly wrong route to the same end, conversions from SEO and content. The practical filter is that every deviant candidate must survive the question of whether the searcher will convert, even though the keyword does not describe your product.

> "website traffic by itself is a vanity metric"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated preference for modest traffic with high conversion rates, and their practice of targeting mini-volume keywords for clients.

**How to do it**

1. Write the conversion hypothesis for every deviant candidate before it enters the content calendar: who converts and why.
2. Reject any candidate whose only argument is search volume or a competitor ranking for it.
3. Prefer a low-volume keyword with a clear conversion path over a high-volume one without.
4. Confirm the searcher has a pain point your product addresses, even if their query names a different solution.
5. Set a conversion threshold per published deviant page and a review date three months out.
6. Unpublish or repurpose deviant pages that bring traffic and no conversions.
7. Report conversion rate per page alongside traffic in every content review.
8. Keep mini-volume keywords on the list when their intent is strong; volume is not the entry criterion.

**Pitfall:** Once a team learns the deviant concept, it becomes a justification for any keyword they wanted to write anyway. The signal is a growing traffic chart with a flat conversion line.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every deviant keyword proposed for pabau.com needs a written sentence saying which practice owner converts from it and why. Without that sentence it does not get commissioned, no matter the volume.

**Apply anywhere:** Require a written conversion hypothesis for every unusual keyword before it is commissioned. If the only argument is volume or a competitor ranking, drop it. Low-volume terms with clear intent beat high-volume terms without.

### 20. Kill high-traffic keywords whose searchers cannot buy your product  `150.3`
*core · content insights · source 150*

Grow and Convert describe a client with a survey and customer feedback app aimed at marketers and product people. The client ranked near the top for 'likert scale', an academic term for a surveying method, and pulled a lot of traffic from it. Signups from that keyword were zero or nearly zero, because people looking up what a likert scale is are not shopping for a survey tool. Management had big blog traffic growth goals and the keyword was helping them hit those goals, which is exactly the trap. The keyword satisfied the reported metric and produced no business value. Their conclusion is that traffic volume tells you nothing about whether a keyword belongs in the plan, and that the check has to be whether the searcher could plausibly buy.

> "which is an academic term around a particular surveying method"

**Evidence:** A Grow and Convert client ranked near the top for 'likert scale' and got zero or nearly zero signups a month from it.

**How to do it**

1. List your top 20 blog pages by organic sessions.
2. Pull conversions per page from the analytics model comparison report over the same window.
3. Flag any page in the traffic top 20 with zero or near-zero conversions over six months.
4. For each flagged page, write one sentence on who is typing the keyword and what they wanted.
5. Where the searcher is a student, researcher or someone using the term academically, mark the keyword as non-commercial.
6. Stop building further content around non-commercial terms even when volume is high.
7. Keep the existing page if it earns links, but remove it from the KPI report so it stops inflating the traffic number.
8. Redirect the freed production capacity to bottom-of-funnel terms where the searcher is buying.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** A traffic growth goal makes an academic keyword look like a win, so nobody questions it. The signal is a top-five traffic page with a six-month conversion count of zero.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has the same exposure on clinical and academic terms in aesthetics, where definition-style queries pull volume from students and curious readers rather than practice owners. David should audit the traffic top 20 for pages with zero demo conversions and stop expanding those clusters.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your highest-traffic blog pages for conversions. Where a page ranks for an academic or definitional term and produces no leads over six months, stop expanding that cluster regardless of its search volume.

### 21. Layer four specificity modifiers onto every category keyword you own  `142.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 142*

Grow and Convert treat 'category plus a layer of specificity' as the hidden-gem group most brands miss: a category term with a word or two added that narrows it to a feature, use case, vertical or customer type. Examples given are 'best accounting tools for small business' and 'men's running shoes for trail running'. They group the 13 posts studied into four modifier types: business size (enterprise accounting software), cost (free or budget-friendly accounting software), industry (accounting software for HVAC business) and feature (accounting software with inventory management). The average is 2.96%, below main category terms, because most bets miss. But a hit can be extraordinary: targeting 'marketing asset management software' for the DAM client, matching features built for marketers, converted at 15.2%. For the same client, enterprise-framed terms converted around 6.5% while other industry-specific ones sat around 2%.

> "saw a whopping 15.2% conversion rate from that post"

**Evidence:** 13 posts across clients averaged 2.96%; 'marketing asset management software' hit 15.2%; enterprise terms around 6.5%, other industry terms around 2%.

**How to do it**

1. Write out your two or three main category keywords in plain product language.
2. Generate business-size variants: enterprise, small business, mid-market, solo, team.
3. Generate cost variants: free, cheap, budget, affordable, under $X.
4. Generate industry variants for every vertical you actually serve, one keyword each.
5. Generate feature variants naming each capability you are genuinely stronger at than rivals.
6. Score every variant on how well it maps to a real product strength, and drop the ones that do not.
7. Publish in batches and expect most to land near 2%, with a small number above 6%.
8. Double down on the winners with internal links and refreshes rather than spreading budget evenly.

**Pitfall:** Publishing the whole modifier matrix and judging the program on the average. Most variants miss; the return comes from the few that align with a real product strength, so a flat budget across all of them wastes money.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, build the matrix out of practice types Pabau is genuinely strongest for, medspa, aesthetic clinic, dermatology, plus size modifiers like multi-location and single-practitioner. Expect most to sit near 2% and a couple to carry the group.

**Apply anywhere:** Build the modifier matrix out of the segments your product is genuinely strongest for, then add size and cost variants. Expect most to sit near 2% and a couple to carry the whole group.

### 22. List every persona who could search a term before targeting it  `106.16`
*core · concrete actions · source 106*

Grow and Convert's test for whether a high-volume industry term has buying-intent is to write out who else could be typing it. For 'human resource management', 15,000 searches a month according to Ahrefs, they list a college student writing a research paper, a founder who just hired their first employee, a job seeker surveying fields, and a high school student considering a career in HR. Then the decisive observation: the actual buyers, HR managers and directors who have worked in HR for years, would not search a basic concept in their own field, because they already know more than any introductory article offers. The persona list makes the mismatch visible before you commission the article rather than after the conversion rate comes in.

> "Any one of the following personas could be"

**Evidence:** 'Human resources management' shows 15,000 monthly searches in Ahrefs, and its top results are definitional HRM explainers rather than software pages.

**How to do it**

1. Take the high-volume candidate keyword and write it at the top of a page.
2. List at least four distinct people who could plausibly type it, including students, job seekers and career researchers.
3. Write down what each of them wants from the results page.
4. Ask whether your actual buyer, with years in the field, would search a basic concept in their own domain.
5. Cross-check the persona list against the top ten titles to see which persona Google is serving.
6. Discard the keyword if your buyer is not among the personas the SERP serves.
7. If the topic is right but the persona is wrong, rewrite the query with a product or use-case qualifier and re-test.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Assuming topical relevance equals buyer relevance. A term can be squarely about your industry and still be searched almost entirely by students and job seekers.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should run the persona test on broad aesthetics terms before commissioning. Queries like 'what is aesthetic medicine' are searched by students and prospective patients, not by clinic owners buying software.

**Apply anywhere:** Before targeting a big industry term, list four people who could be searching it. If students, job seekers and career researchers dominate the list and your buyer already knows the answer, the keyword has no buying-intent.

### 23. Log every question sales hears on calls and validate it in Google Suggest  `114.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 114*

At ThinkApps the author answered every inbound phone call on top of doing marketing, specifically so he could hear prospect pain points first hand. One prospect said they were trying to build an app but did not know whether to develop on iOS or Android. He wrote the question down, checked Google Suggested search, found strong search demand for iOS vs Android development, and published a post on it. That post drew heavy social sharing and became one of their highest converting pieces. His workaround for marketers who cannot take calls is a brainstorming session where sales writes down the questions they receive. The extra instruction that makes the list usable is tagging each question by call stage: was it asked on the first call, the second call, or further down the funnel. That tag maps the question to a funnel position, which decides whether the resulting article should be educational or comparison-heavy.

> "I'm trying to build an app, but I'm not sure if I should develop it"

**Evidence:** ThinkApps: a single prospect question about iOS versus Android development became one of their highest converting posts after validation in Google Suggest.

**How to do it**

1. Get on inbound calls yourself if you can, or run a dedicated brainstorming session with the sales team.
2. Have each rep write out the literal questions prospects ask, in the prospect's own words.
3. Tag each question with the call it came from: first call, second call, or later stage.
4. Sort the list by funnel stage so early-call questions become educational pieces and late-call questions become comparison and objection pieces.
5. Type each question into Google and read the Suggested search completions to confirm other people search it.
6. Keep questions with clear suggest demand and drop ones with none unless a customer asked it more than once.
7. Write one article per surviving question that answers it directly and names your product where it applies.
8. Track conversions per article and feed the winners back to sales as call material.

**Tools:** Google Suggest

**Pitfall:** An untagged list of sales questions produces articles aimed at the wrong funnel stage: a late-stage objection written as a beginner explainer converts nobody. The signal is a post that ranks for a comparison query but reads like a definition.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's sales and onboarding teams should keep a running list of questions from demo calls, tagged by call number. Late-stage questions about migrating patient records or switching from a competitor become comparison pages, not blog explainers.

**Apply anywhere:** Collect the exact questions your sales team hears, tag each by which call it came from, and validate demand in Google Suggest. Write early-call questions as explainers and late-call questions as comparisons and objection handlers.

### 24. Map five competitor types before building the keyword list  `93.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 93*

Goolding says competitor definition is a topic-generation exercise, not a positioning exercise, and gives five types. Direct competitors doing the same thing for the same people at similar pricing. Niche competitors doing the same thing for a smaller slice, geographic or vertical, which is relative: for accounting software the niche rival is healthcare accounting software, and for healthcare accounting software it is dental-specific software. Broader competitors, the same relationship in reverse, where a bigger tool offers a plugin or template covering your niche. Alternative solutions, including spreadsheets and humans, such as an outsourced billing team instead of billing software, or an in-house team built to do the job. And search competitors who write about the same subjects without selling the same product. Each type produces different keyword angles, so working through all five is what stops the list being generic.

> "So here's my full list of competitor types"

**Evidence:** Goolding cites StrataPT, where the alternative to physical therapy billing software is an outsourced human team creating and chasing claims.

**How to do it**

1. Open a sheet with five rows: direct, niche, broader, alternative solutions, search competitors.
2. List every direct rival with similar features and pricing under row one.
3. List vertical and geographic specialists that serve a slice of your market under row two.
4. List the larger platforms that cover your niche with a plugin or template under row three.
5. Under alternative solutions, list spreadsheets, manual processes, outsourced agencies and in-house teams.
6. Under search competitors, list the sites that rank for your topics without selling your product.
7. For each entry, write the keyword angles it opens: alternatives, vs, how to do X in that tool, hiring that team.
8. Score each angle by how directly it touches a pain point you fix, and brief the top rows first.

**Pitfall:** Teams stop at direct competitors, so their alternatives pages cover four rivals and miss the incumbent that actually wins most deals, which is usually a spreadsheet or a manual process.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's competitor map should include paper diaries, Excel client lists, standalone booking apps and outsourced reception services, alongside the named practice-management rivals. Each earns its own comparison or how-to page.

**Apply anywhere:** Build the competitor map across five types before writing keywords, so manual processes, outsourced teams and larger platforms with niche plugins all generate topic angles.

### 25. Match the page type you build to what the SERP already ranks  `120.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 120*

Before assigning a keyword to a page, Grow and Convert read the SERP for page type rather than content quality. For a product or service category keyword they check whether the top results are mostly home pages, product or service pages, or blog posts. The page you optimize should match the type that dominates, because that pattern shows which page type the algorithm favors for the query. They allow exceptions but treat it as a firm guideline. The rule then feeds a simple decision. If you already own a page that closely matches the search intent, optimize that page for the term. If you do not, create a new dedicated page of the type the SERP rewards, either a blog post or a landing page. This stops teams from pointing a product page at a query the SERP answers with articles.

> "check whether the top results are primarily home pages"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say matching the dominant page type indicates the algorithm favors that page type for the keyword, and they follow it as a guideline with rare exceptions.

**How to do it**

1. Search each high-priority keyword in an incognito window and record the page type of all ten results.
2. Count how many are home pages, how many are product or service pages, and how many are blog posts or listicles.
3. Pick the majority type as the format you will build or optimize.
4. Check your own site for an existing page of that type whose intent already matches the query.
5. If one exists, optimize it for the term rather than publishing a second page.
6. If none exists, create a dedicated page of the winning type, not whichever type is easiest to publish.
7. Re-check the SERP mix before any later refresh, since a shift in page type means intent has moved.

**Tools:** Google

**Pitfall:** Forcing a commercial landing page at a query the SERP answers with articles, or the reverse. The page can be excellent and still stall outside the top ten because it is the wrong format for the query.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should check page type per target term before choosing between a /blog/ article, a /templates/ page and a product page. Several buying-intent terms for practice software return listicles, which means an article, not the product page.

**Apply anywhere:** Read the top ten for page type before assigning a keyword. Build or optimize the type that dominates the SERP: home page, product page, or blog post. Reuse an existing matching page if you have one, otherwise create a dedicated new one.
