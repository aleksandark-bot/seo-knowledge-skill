### 1. Score each content type on six dimensions before funding it  `66.1`
*core · best practices · source 66*

Aleyda Solis's worksheet replaces 'is this good content?' with a scored decision. Every recurring content pattern on the site is rated across six dimensions: click resilience (how likely the user still needs the site after seeing an AI answer), citation potential (how likely the page offers unique, verifiable, primary or well-documented information an AI platform, publisher or other site may reference), brand mention potential (whether the content creates a relevant reason for the brand to be associated with an entity, topic, problem or need), business value (how directly it supports acquisition, conversion, retention, support or another defined outcome), proprietary advantage (how hard it would be to reproduce without the organization's data, access, expertise, systems, customer relationships or operating context) and expected effort (the editorial, subject-matter, technical and maintenance resources required, where high effort means higher cost). The first five are the return, the sixth is the cost, and the decision is the ratio between them, not any single score.

> "The scores provide a directional starting point that you can adapt based on your audience, priority prompts, performance and business model."

**How to do it**

1. List the content patterns the site actually publishes, one row per pattern rather than per page: about pages, pricing pages, feature docs, comparison pages, glossary entries, template downloads, case studies, and so on.
2. For each row, score click resilience High/Medium/Low by asking one question: after an AI answer to this page's target prompt, does the user still need the site? Score higher when the visit requires interaction, verification of live details, evidence inspection, or action completion.
3. Score citation potential by asking whether the page contains a primary fact — something measured, owned, dated or first-hand — that another site could cite. A page assembled from public sources scores Low here no matter how well written.
4. Score brand mention potential by asking whether the content gives an AI system a reason to name the brand when discussing an entity, topic, problem or need, rather than just answering the question generically.
5. Score business value against a named outcome (acquisition, conversion, retention, support), not against traffic. Traffic with no credible next step is not business value.
6. Score proprietary advantage by asking what a competitor or an AI system would need to reproduce the page: if only a writer and public sources, score Low; if your data, customer relationships, systems or operating context, score High.
7. Score expected effort last, covering editorial, subject-matter, technical and maintenance cost — including the recurring cost of keeping the page current, which is the part most audits omit.
8. Rank the rows by the five value dimensions against effort, and fund the top of that ranking rather than the top of the search-volume list.
9. Re-score on a fixed cadence, because the framework is explicitly directional and the interfaces it responds to keep moving.

**Tools:** Google Sheets, Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Averaging the six scores into a single number. The dimensions are not interchangeable — a page can be worth building on citation and brand mention potential alone with Medium click resilience, and averaging hides exactly that case. Read the row, not the mean.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau commissions another article, score the content pattern it belongs to on the six dimensions, and treat the pattern's score — not the keyword's search volume — as the funding decision. Pabau's template pages, pricing pages and feature documentation score High on proprietary advantage and business value; a generic industry explainer scores Low on both.

**Apply anywhere:** Before commissioning another article, score the content pattern it belongs to on the six dimensions, and treat the pattern's score — not the keyword's search volume — as the funding decision. Pages built on your own product data, pricing and documentation score High on proprietary advantage and business value; a generic industry explainer scores Low on both.

### 2. Sell a service first, find the recurring problem, then build the product  `68.23`
*core · concrete actions · source 68*

Asked what he would do starting over after ten years, Cody rejects starting with a product and rejects starting with SEO, which he says indie hackers do first and which is typically not the best investment. His sequence: sell consulting or a service into one industry, get a handful of customers, and watch for the same problem recurring across all of them. Then build a single-feature product against that problem and sell it back to the customers you already have, before selling it to the rest of the industry. The agency cash flows the product build. His argument is that most first-time founders build things nobody wants because they never learned the problems firsthand. He is equally blunt that a podcast is not the tool for a first customer; you start the show once you reach sustainability. Choosing the industry can be as simple as opening Google Maps or Thumbtack categories and picking one, such as auto body shops.

> "that agency cash flows the product"

**Evidence:** Cody's worked example runs from Google Ads for auto body shops through noticing missed-appointment rescheduling as the repeated problem, to an AI rescheduling agent sold back to those same shops.

**How to do it**

1. Open Google Maps or Thumbtack categories and pick one narrow business type you can serve.
2. Define a single service you can deliver to that category, such as Google Ads for auto body shops.
3. Sell that service through cold email, cold DMs or paid ads until you have several paying customers.
4. Log the problems each customer raises and look for the one that repeats across all of them.
5. Build a single-feature product against that repeated problem, not a platform.
6. Sell it back to your existing service customers first, then to the rest of the industry.
7. Use the service revenue to fund the product build rather than raising for it.
8. Only start a podcast or long-payback content channel once the business is sustainable.

**Tools:** Google Maps, Thumbtack

**Pitfall:** Building the product first means you do not know the real problems, so you ship something nobody buys. Cody says he sees this constantly with first-time founders.

**Apply at Pabau:** The pattern explains Pabau's own content leverage: the recurring operational problems practices report are the product roadmap and the article list at the same time, and support tickets are the cheapest source of both.

**Apply anywhere:** Start with a service in one narrow industry, find the problem every customer has, then build a single-feature product for it and sell it back to them. The service cash flows the build and teaches you the problem.

### 3. Sell a tech-enabled service first to fund the software product  `170.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 170*

Patrick Campbell built Price Intelligently as what he calls a 'people powered product': algorithms analyzed customer survey data, but a trained person had to run the engagement. He says in hindsight that starting as a tech-enabled service was a better route into a software company than writing software first. It produced cash while bootstrapped, and it forced the team into the customer's actual problem. Campbell is not an engineer, so he sold and delivered instead of building. The service booked around $130k in contracts in six months and reached roughly half a million dollars a year within two years. That margin later paid for the engineer who built ProfitWell.

> "We sort of fell into the tech-enabled service"

**Evidence:** Price Intelligently booked around $130k in contracts in six months and about $500k a year by year two; it now does over $6 million a year.

**How to do it**

1. Name the problem you can already solve manually with your own expertise, not the software you eventually want to sell.
2. Package it as a fixed-scope engagement with a named deliverable rather than open-ended consulting.
3. Build the repeatable analytical core first (Campbell's was survey collection plus a pricing algorithm) and keep the human delivery around it.
4. Price the engagement to leave real margin, not just cover salaries, because that margin is the product budget.
5. Run the delivery yourself for the first six months so the learning lands on the founders.
6. Log every recurring problem clients bring, tagged by frequency, as the input to the product roadmap.
7. Set a revenue threshold at which you hire your first engineer, and do not hire before it.
8. Route all service margin into the product build once the threshold is hit.

**Pitfall:** The service can become the whole company. Campbell says the fact it was not a standalone product 'weighed on us a lot', and staff hired on a product vision will leave if the agency work never ends.

**Apply at Pabau:** David can treat Pabau's hands-on onboarding and migration work the same way. Log the recurring questions practices ask during setup, tag them by frequency, and turn the top ones into template pages and code-reference pages rather than guessing topics from keyword tools.

**Apply anywhere:** If you want to sell software but cannot yet build it, sell the service version first. Deliver it yourself, log every recurring client problem by frequency, and use both the margin and the problem log to fund and direct the product.

### 4. Send bottom-funnel readers to a trial or demo, not an email capture  `110.5`
*core · best practices · source 110*

Grow and Convert draw a direct line from topic choice to call-to-action choice. If you rank for broad terms, very few readers are ready to buy, so you are forced to collect emails, nurture them and hope they come back ready later. That is how most SaaS companies run content. When the topic itself indicates buying intent, you can go straight for the sale, pointing the reader at the product call-to-action you actually want, a free trial or a demo signup. Their own example is refusing to chase 'content marketing' despite it being the highest-volume term in their space, because the only term in that set indicating a real need for either of their products is 'content marketing agency'. The topic decides whether a product CTA is realistic, so the CTA decision belongs at the keyword stage.

> "you can go directly for the sale instead of pushing people to an email signup"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert have never targeted 'content marketing' despite it being the highest-volume term in their space, because only 'content marketing agency' indicates a need for either of their products.

**How to do it**

1. Before commissioning a piece, decide which single call-to-action it will carry: product signup or email capture.
2. If the honest answer is email capture, ask why the topic is in the queue ahead of a product-intent topic.
3. For product-intent topics, put the trial or demo CTA in the body copy, not just at the bottom.
4. Match the CTA wording to the pain point in the article rather than using a generic site-wide button.
5. Track conversions to the product action separately from newsletter signups in analytics.
6. Review quarterly: if the majority of published pieces carry email CTAs, the topic mix is top-heavy.
7. Move genuinely top-funnel ideas out of the blog queue rather than adding nurture sequences to rescue them.

**Pitfall:** Adding a demo CTA to a top-funnel article does not make it convert. The article's topic is what filters readers, and a hard product CTA on a research-intent piece just lowers the page's engagement.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every pabau.com bottom-funnel article should end at /book-demo/ rather than a newsletter signup, and the demo CTA should reference the specific practice problem the article covers. If an article can only justify an email capture, question whether it should be commissioned yet.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide each article's single call-to-action at the keyword stage. Product-intent topics get a trial or demo CTA worded around the article's pain point; if a topic can only justify an email capture, move it behind the product-intent topics in the queue.

### 5. Sequence B2C keywords: product terms, then pain points, then use cases  `112.7`
*core · best practices · source 112*

Grow and Convert set an explicit order for B2C content, and it is the inverse of the standard awareness-consideration-conversion funnel. They target, in order: bottom-of-funnel product keywords that the general product pages will not rank for, pain-point keywords indicating someone has a problem the product solves, and use-case keywords indicating someone wants to do something the product helps with. They call this Pain Point SEO and say they use it for both B2B and B2C clients, built over four years of running the agency. The logic they give is that the keyword itself reveals readiness to buy, which reading an informational how-to does not. They accept that this content has much lower search volume and traffic potential than top-of-funnel terms, and argue it produces more conversions anyway.

> "you are showing up in front of prospects that are ready to buy"

**Evidence:** Four years of Grow and Convert agency work across B2B and B2C clients, including the Cognitive FX blog grown to 70,000 pageviews in 14 months.

**How to do it**

1. Split your keyword list into three buckets: product terms, pain points, use cases, and discard anything that fits none.
2. Publish the product-term bucket first, one dedicated page per term, before writing anything else.
3. Move to pain points only once the product terms are live and indexed.
4. Move to use cases only after that, and treat lifestyle topics as a separate later decision.
5. Judge each bucket on conversions produced, not sessions, and report it that way.
6. Accept lower traffic on these pages and do not let a volume comparison reorder the queue.
7. Expand upward into top-of-funnel content only once the bottom three buckets are exhausted.

**Pitfall:** The pressure to reorder comes from traffic dashboards, because the bottom-of-funnel pages will always look small next to a lifestyle post. Reporting sessions rather than conversions is what breaks the sequence.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial calendar should be ordered the same way: competitor and category pages first, practice-owner pain points second, how-to use cases third. David should resist filling the calendar with aesthetics-industry trend pieces until the first two buckets are actually covered.

**Apply anywhere:** Order content as product keywords, then pain points, then use cases, and only consider awareness topics after those are exhausted. Report each bucket on conversions rather than sessions, or the traffic numbers will push the queue back upside down.

### 6. Set the three-to-six month traction and six-to-twelve month ROI expectation  `179.10`
*core · best practices · source 179*

Grow and Convert give explicit timeline numbers from their own client base. Most clients start seeing meaningful traction, meaning content ranking on page one, traffic increasing and leads arriving, somewhere between three and six months into the engagement. Significant ROI, where leads generated clearly exceed the investment, typically lands between six and twelve months. They use these numbers as a filter on agencies as much as a plan: if an agency promises faster results, be skeptical, because they are either targeting very low-competition keywords that will not move the needle or they are overpromising. They also frame the early months honestly as an investment period where content is publishing and starting to rank while conversions are still building. What matters in that period is that the trend line is moving toward break-even, not that it has crossed it.

> "somewhere between three and six months into the engagement"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report meaningful traction at three to six months and clear positive ROI at six to twelve months across their client base.

**How to do it**

1. Write the expected timeline into the engagement plan before month one: page-one rankings and first leads at three to six months, ROI at six to twelve.
2. Ask any agency promising faster results which specific keywords produce that speed, and check their difficulty and commercial intent.
3. Reject a fast-result plan built on very low-competition terms that carry no buying intent.
4. Define the break-even number of leads per month before starting, so the early months have a target to trend toward.
5. Report monthly on direction of travel against break-even rather than on whether it has been crossed.
6. Do not judge or cancel the program on a metric snapshot before month six.
7. At month twelve, judge on whether leads generated exceed total spend, not on traffic growth.

**Pitfall:** Signing a short contract and reading month three as the verdict. Grow and Convert describe months one to six as an investment period, so a three-month pilot is structurally guaranteed to look like a failure.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any SEO program Pabau runs, in-house or outsourced, should be reviewed against a written break-even lead number at month six and judged at month twelve. Publishing plans for pabau.com should not be reworked on month-three data.

**Apply anywhere:** Plan for page-one rankings and first leads between three and six months, and clear positive ROI between six and twelve. Treat a promise of faster results as a warning: it usually means very low-competition keywords that will not move the business. Set the monthly break-even lead number up front and report on direction of travel rather than on whether it has been crossed yet.

### 7. Spend 90 percent of SEO effort on two things and treat both as mandatory  `179.16`
*core · best practices · source 179*

Grow and Convert state their allocation explicitly: 90% of their time goes to two things, prioritizing keywords with high buying intent, and producing unique dedicated content for each of those keywords. They insist the two are equally important and that failing either one collapses the result. Pick the right keywords but target them with sprinkled or shared pages and you probably will not rank. Build dedicated pages but aim them at keywords with no buying intent and any rankings you win generate very little business value. Technical SEO and link building sit in the remaining share and are done in service of these two. This is a useful allocation to apply to an in-house program as well, because it gives a concrete answer to where a month of SEO hours should go.

> "what we spend 90% of our time focusing and working on"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert spend 90% of their working time on buying-intent keyword prioritization and unique page production, with technical SEO and link building explicitly subordinate.

**How to do it**

1. Log where your SEO hours actually went last month across four buckets: keyword selection, page production, technical, and links.
2. If keyword selection plus page production is under 90% of the total, move hours until it is.
3. Check the first failure mode: every priority keyword has its own dedicated page rather than a mention inside a broader article.
4. Check the second failure mode: every dedicated page targets a keyword with buying intent, not a high-volume advice term.
5. Kill any page that fails both checks rather than optimizing it further.
6. Cap technical and link work at the remaining share, and tie each item to a named target page.
7. Re-run the hour audit quarterly, because technical and link work expand into whatever space they are given.

**Pitfall:** Getting one of the two right and expecting half the result. Right keywords with sprinkled pages produce no rankings, and dedicated pages on zero-intent keywords produce rankings with no revenue. Neither failure is a partial success.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's monthly SEO time should be audited against this split. If the majority of hours on pabau.com go to technical maintenance or link outreach rather than choosing high-intent keywords and building the pages for them, the allocation is inverted.

**Apply anywhere:** Put 90% of your SEO hours into two things: choosing keywords with real buying intent, and building a unique dedicated page for each. Both are mandatory. Right keywords on sprinkled pages will not rank, and dedicated pages on zero-intent keywords rank without earning anything. Audit where the hours actually went last month, and keep technical and link work in the remainder, each tied to a named target page.

### 8. Standalone affiliate and niche sites are finished; product sites aren't  `57.11`
*core · content insights · source 57*

Asked where niche sites go, Jackie is unequivocal: the traditional niche site or affiliate site is completely decimated and will be within the next couple of years - 'there's no comeback from this.' The only structure he sees working is having your own core product and running the affiliate blog on that product's site - and it cannot be your core product content, because Google can detect that now. His ranking of what's working in search outside Reddit: e-commerce companies are doing very well, SaaS companies are doing all right because they're already savvy. Cody's supporting test is more precise: they built a general affiliate blog and, separately, a site with a signup flow so the code looked like a web application, with the same type of content and the same links - and the one that looked like a real company with a product ranked more effectively. Cody also notes his own hedge worked by accident: he had built software products that fit his blog audiences, which mitigated the demonetisation risk when updates hit.

> "your traditional niche site or affiliate site is completely decimated"

**How to do it**

1. If you run content-only properties, assume the model is ending and plan for it rather than optimising it.
2. Attach a real product to the audience you have built - software, a tool, a paid service - so you control monetisation.
3. Keep the content layer adjacent to the product rather than being the product.
4. Make the site read as an application, not a blog: signup flow, product surface, real company footprint.
5. Diversify monetisation before an update forces the issue - Cody's software products are what saved him.
6. For a portfolio, accept that some properties will go to zero and size positions accordingly.

**Pitfall:** Jackie notes he spent hundreds of thousands of dollars buying affiliate sites and they are all at zero now - the model's decline wasn't gradual enough to trade out of.

**Apply at Pabau:** This is the structural reason Pabau's content works: it sits on a real product with real users, which is the asset class both practitioners say survives. Content that drifts away from the product is the part that carries risk.

**Apply anywhere:** This is the structural reason content on a real product site works: it sits on a real product with real users, which is the asset class both practitioners say survives. Content that drifts away from the product is the part that carries risk.

### 9. Start from where your customer already spends time, not from a channel list  `61.6`
*core · best practices · source 61*

Cody's method for deciding where to build mass, which is the practical version of digital gravity. The question is always where your target customer spends time online, then how you make as much mass as possible in that space. His worked example: for an AI video editor, people go to YouTube to learn about AI video editors - so you need a YouTube strategy, and the question becomes how to automate your process to make as much content as possible in that category. LinkedIn is probably not where people discover video editing products, but Instagram Reels might be. Once you know the place, the strategy follows and then you define tactics for producing content at scale there. Jordan's practical sequencing adds the test-and-concentrate step: find the three places they are, test three channels, see which one or two are most effective, then invest everything into those - and only then automate the process you've proven.

> "where is my co target customer spending time"

**How to do it**

1. Identify where your specific buyer goes to learn about products in your category - not where your category's marketers hang out.
2. Pick three candidate channels from that, no more.
3. Test all three with real effort rather than token posts.
4. Concentrate everything on the one or two that work, and stop the others.
5. Only once you have a proven manual process, automate it to increase volume in that channel.
6. Re-run the question as the product changes, since the discovery surface moves.

**Pitfall:** Choosing the channel you personally find easiest, or the one your competitors use, rather than the one your buyer actually uses for discovery, is the error this sequence is designed to prevent.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau the honest answer to 'where do clinic owners go to learn about practice software' is probably industry Facebook groups, YouTube and peer recommendation - which argues for concentrating there rather than spreading across every platform.

**Apply anywhere:** Answer honestly where your buyer goes to learn about products in your category - it's often industry groups, video and peer recommendation - and concentrate there rather than spreading across every platform.

### 10. Start the hardest category terms on day one while banking long-tail wins  `120.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 120*

Grow and Convert prioritize product and service category keywords first because they convert best, but they are open that these are usually the most competitive terms on the sheet. Their resolution is not to defer them. Because those terms take the longest and need the most backlinks, work on them should begin immediately, and the long-tail variations run in parallel to produce visible results in the meantime. Their example contrasts 'hvac software' with 'hvac software compatible with quickbooks'. The second is easier to rank, still carries full buying intent, and buys the program credibility while the head term matures. This is a sequencing answer to a common failure: programs that only chase quick wins never start the terms that take a year, and programs that only chase head terms show nothing for two quarters.

> "meaning it can take more time and require more backlinks to rank"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite 'hvac software compatible with quickbooks' against 'hvac software' as the long-tail versus head split they run in parallel.

**How to do it**

1. Sort the category keyword list by competition, not by volume.
2. Pick the two or three head terms you eventually want and start their pages now, accepting a long ranking timeline.
3. For each head term, write out the long-tail variants that add an integration, a platform, a size or a use case, in the shape 'hvac software compatible with quickbooks'.
4. Publish the long-tail pages first, since they rank sooner and carry the same buying intent.
5. Point internal links from the long-tail pages up to the head-term page as they start to rank.
6. Direct the link-building budget at the head-term pages only, since the long-tail ones rarely need it.
7. Review at 90 days: if the head term has not moved past page three, add links rather than rewriting.
8. Keep publishing long-tail variants continuously so the program shows results while the head term matures.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Deferring the head terms until the easy wins run out. They need the most time and the most links, so a late start pushes the payoff out by a year and the delay is invisible until it is too late to fix.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should start the 'practice management software' and 'clinic software' pages now and accept a long timeline, while publishing the integration and use-case variants such as software compatible with specific payment or EMR systems to earn rankings in the meantime.

**Apply anywhere:** Start your most competitive category pages immediately and accept that they will take a year, then publish integration, platform and use-case long-tail variants alongside them for near-term rankings. Send link budget only to the head-term pages.

### 11. Stop capping buying-intent targets at the five to ten your product pages own  `145.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 145*

Grow and Convert describe the default site structure they see: homepage optimized for one or two category keywords, a small set of product, feature or solution pages optimized for similar terms, and a blog aimed at high-volume low-intent topics for awareness. Their count is that this leaves a company targeting maybe five to ten buying-intent keywords in total, while the real pool runs into dozens. The fix is structural, not editorial: treat the blog as the place where the remaining high-intent keywords get their pages, rather than as a funnel-feeder. They say this is what their agency does and what most companies do not.

> "only targeting a small handful of buying intent keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report this five-to-ten pattern as the common case across the businesses, in-house teams and agencies they have audited.

**How to do it**

1. List every page on the site currently optimized for a buying-intent keyword, including the homepage and all product or solution pages.
2. Count them; if the number is under about ten, assume the gap is large rather than assuming coverage is complete.
3. Build the full buying-intent pool from three sources: category and noun variants, competitor and alternatives terms, and jobs-to-be-done terms.
4. Subtract the keywords already owned by a page to get the uncovered list.
5. Assign each uncovered keyword to a new blog post rather than trying to stretch an existing product page.
6. Order the build queue by buying intent first and search volume second.
7. Track conversions per page, not sessions, so the new pages are judged on the metric they were built for.
8. Repeat the inventory quarterly as the product and verticals expand.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** The blog stays reserved for awareness topics, so high-intent keywords have nowhere to live and the company concludes the category is saturated when it has simply never targeted most of it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog should carry the buying-intent terms that the product pages cannot: alternatives pages, vertical-specific software terms and job-based queries. David should audit how many Pabau URLs currently target a buying-intent keyword before commissioning more informational posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Count how many of your pages actually target a buying-intent keyword. If it is under ten, the pool is far larger than your site covers, and the blog is where the remaining terms should get dedicated pages.

### 12. Suspect positioning before blaming the acquisition channel  `169.10`
*core · general insights · source 169*

Hyam's opening argument is that weak positioning shows up as two marketing symptoms: conversion rate stays frustratingly low no matter what you do on the traffic side, and you cannot set prices as high as you want. Management teams almost always misread both as tactical failures, saying a channel does not work for us, or that content traffic is not converting so we need to go upmarket or optimize conversions. He calls that barking up the wrong tree. If brand positioning is off, no inbound channel fixes it, because the traffic will not convert or will not pay what you want. He also rejects a broad target market statement, 'we make a CRM for small businesses', as positioning; it is only an initial target statement. Real positioning answers who you target, what pain you solve, how you are unique, and whether you can prove it.

> "they're barking up the wrong tree"

**Evidence:** Hyam ran through four pivots at Grow and Convert and, at ThinkApps, found paid CAC too high and educational events attracting people with no budget before content worked.

**Pitfall:** Rebuilding the funnel or switching channels when the same low conversion follows you to every channel. Identical underperformance across paid, content and events is the signal that positioning, not tactics, is the constraint.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau campaign or content push underperforms, check whether the same conversion rate shows up across channels before changing tactics. If it does, the fix belongs in the positioning copy on the money pages, not in the channel.

**Apply anywhere:** When conversion stays low across every channel and you cannot raise prices, the problem is positioning, not the channel. A broad target-market sentence is not positioning; you need the target, the pain points, the uniqueness and the proof.

### 13. Target the categories your innovation replaces, not the category you invented  `135.15`
*core · content insights · source 135*

Grow and Convert set out the core problem with genuinely new products: nobody searches for them, because people do not know a tool like that exists. There was almost no keyword landscape for 'editing video like editing text'. Their answer is that people are still doing the job today by worse means, and those worse means have search demand. Video editors expect Adobe Premiere Pro; researchers expect transcription tools like Rev. The new product sits between the two and solves the same pains in a new way, so the categories it displaces are its keyword landscape. This reframes the whole strategy: stop trying to create demand for a category name and go take demand from the incumbents whose pains you remove, then use titles and intros to convert those searchers to the new method.

> "they just accomplish these tasks through other (worse) means now"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client grew organic traffic 120x with 120+ conversions by targeting the video editing and transcription categories rather than the new category name.

**Pitfall:** Spending the budget on category-creation content for a name nobody searches. You get impressions on branded phrasing only, and no pipeline until you go back to the incumbent categories.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should target the tools practices currently stitch together, booking systems, spreadsheets, paper consent forms and standalone marketing tools, rather than trying to build demand for an all-in-one category label nobody types.

**Apply anywhere:** For a product with no search demand of its own, map the inferior tools people currently use for the same job and take demand from those categories, using the title and intro to convert them to the new method.

### 14. Test SEO with five to ten articles instead of over-modeling ROI  `129.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 129*

Grow and Convert cut the estimating exercise short deliberately. They list what you genuinely cannot know in advance: where you will rank, how long it takes to get there, how much traffic the ranking actually produces, and the conversion rate you will get once ranked, which itself depends on how well you sell the product inside the article. Their conclusion is that the estimate should be treated as a go/no-go, and if you want real precision the way to get it is to run the channel, not to model it. Their prescription is a handful of articles, five or ten, which gives a much better feel for the actual numbers compared with the estimates. They also say to spend that test on the highest buying-intent keywords, since conversion rate varies significantly by article and keyword and you may as well take the low-hanging fruit and see the top end of what SEO can do.

> "simply test SEO as a channel with a handful of articles, say 5 or 10"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert recommend a 5 to 10 article test as a substitute for precise ROI estimation, and note conversion rate varies significantly by article and keyword.

**How to do it**

1. Stop refining the ROI model once it returns a clear go or no-go.
2. Pick the 5 to 10 highest buying-intent keywords you found in the screening step, not the highest-volume ones.
3. Commission one page per keyword built to match the search intent, and set up conversion tracking before publishing.
4. Wait the full ranking window rather than reading positions in the first weeks.
5. Record for each page the actual pageviews against the head term's reported volume, and the actual conversion rate.
6. Replace the assumed 1% conversion rate and the assumed traffic multiple in your model with these measured figures.
7. Rerun the four-line value math with real inputs and only then decide whether to scale.
8. Use the same measured conversion rates to rank which keyword type to build next.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Google Search Console, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Testing with top-of-funnel or high-volume keywords because they look easier to win. The test then measures the wrong thing: it reports a low conversion rate and kills the channel, when high-intent pages would have converted several times higher.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau builds out a whole new page family, ship five of them on the highest-intent queries, instrument the demo-booking conversion, and let the measured rate decide whether the remaining pages get commissioned.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not model SEO ROI to precision you cannot have. Once the estimate says go, publish five to ten pages on your highest buying-intent keywords with conversion tracking in place, then replace your assumed traffic multiple and conversion rate with the measured ones before deciding to scale.

### 15. Test any positioning claim against churn and referral before publishing it  `159.10`
*core · best practices · source 159*

Grow and Convert's fourth criterion is delivery, and they include it in positioning rather than product because it puts a hard limit on criteria one to three. If you claim to solve a pain point for a segment you cannot actually serve, you get customers but the bucket leaks. They name two specific consequences: recurring-revenue customers churn, and word of mouth stops working for you and eventually works against you. So before shipping a positioning claim, the question is whether the product or service can actually fulfil it. This is the one place where they endorse a product pivot, and only in a specific sequence: the initial hypothesis fails, research finds a burning pain point in a specific segment, and the current product genuinely cannot solve it. Only then does a pivot make sense.

> "your bucket will be leaky and your business will likely fail"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert tie failed delivery directly to churn in recurring businesses and to word of mouth turning against the company.

**How to do it**

1. Write out the positioning claim as a promise: we solve this pain point for this customer, uniquely in this way.
2. Take it to delivery, support and success and ask whether they can fulfil it for every customer the claim would attract.
3. Check retention data for the segment the claim targets; a segment that already churns above average is a claim you cannot keep.
4. Ask whether customers in that segment currently refer others, since referral is the cleaner proof of delivered promise.
5. Cut or soften any part of the claim that fails those checks, even if it is the most compelling line.
6. Only consider a product change when research has found a burning pain point in a specific segment that the current product cannot serve.
7. Re-run the check after any product change, since the claim and the product drift apart.
8. Prefer the positioning pivot every time, because it costs days rather than months.

**Pitfall:** A claim that wins on acquisition and loses on retention. The signal is rising signups with rising churn in the same segment, which looks like an onboarding problem and is not.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau markets a capability heavily, check retention and support load for the practices that capability attracts. Marketing a workflow the product handles only partly costs more in churn than it earns in demos.

**Apply anywhere:** Before publishing a positioning claim, check that delivery can keep it for every customer it will attract, and check churn and referral rates in that segment. A claim that wins acquisition and loses retention is a net loss.

### 16. The law of shitty clickthroughs is why you must build the flywheel early  `61.3`
*core · content insights · source 61*

The mechanism underneath the brand-versus-arbitrage argument. Cody names it directly: the law of shitty clickthroughs is the idea that when you find an arbitrage - cold email, paid ads - other people identify the same arbitrage and it goes away. Cost per acquisition therefore increases over time, naturally, for all transactional marketing. The only defence is to create a content marketing flywheel or engine that produces branded search and brand discovery, which reduces your customer acquisition cost as the arbitrage decays. His illustration of how the flywheel compounds is influencer marketing: you create top-of-funnel inbound, one creator makes a video, two others make videos because they saw it go viral, and a discovery flywheel starts. The strategic instruction is timing - you build this while the arbitrage is still cheap, because by the time CAC has 5x'd it's too late to start.

> "law of shitty clickthroughs"

**How to do it**

1. Assume every paid channel you find will get more expensive, and model CAC as rising rather than flat.
2. Start the content and brand engine while the paid arbitrage is still working, not when it stops.
3. Pick the flywheel that compounds on its own - creator content, community, or a share-driven product feature.
4. Watch for the second-order effect: one successful piece prompting others to copy it, which is the flywheel starting.
5. Track blended CAC across all channels as the measure of whether the flywheel is working.

**Pitfall:** Waiting until paid acquisition becomes unaffordable is the standard failure - the flywheel takes months to spin up, so the investment has to be made while it still feels unnecessary.

### 17. Transactional marketing buys revenue tomorrow; brand buys a falling CAC  `61.2`
*core · content insights · source 61*

Cody's framework for when to invest in what. Funnels work - they just produce linear growth: you put a dollar in and X comes out, and you can build a very successful business on it. Transactional marketing is the set of things that pay tomorrow: paid ads, cold email, cold DMs, and sometimes influencer marketing depending on category. That's what you start a company with, because you're looking for an arbitrage with immediate return. His advice to pre-seed startups is deliberately provocative: why are you investing in SEO when you don't know if you'll exist in two years and this won't pay off for 18 months? Non-linear, exponential growth requires the other half - investing in branded search and building gravity. He gives the concrete number that makes it real: a company where signups cost a dollar and, twelve months later, the same signup cost $5 - a 5x increase in CAC. The seesaw he describes: as arbitrage decays, brand has to rise to offset it.

> "I call transactional marketing"

**How to do it**

1. Start with the transactional channels that pay back immediately - paid ads, cold email, cold outbound.
2. Track your cost per acquisition over time on those channels and expect it to rise, not hold.
3. Don't invest in slow channels like SEO before you know the business will exist to collect on them.
4. Once the transactional engine works, start building the content and brand flywheel deliberately, before CAC has risen.
5. Judge brand investment by whether blended CAC is falling, not by the brand channel's own attribution.
6. Revisit the mix as the arbitrage decays - the balance is meant to shift, not hold.

**Pitfall:** The advice not to invest in SEO pre-seed is deliberately blunt and category-dependent - the underlying point is sequencing, not that SEO is a bad investment.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau is past the stage this warns about, so the relevant half is the CAC trend: if paid acquisition costs are climbing, that's the signal the content and brand flywheel needs more investment, not less.

**Apply anywhere:** For an established business the relevant half is the CAC trend: if paid acquisition costs are climbing, that's the signal the content and brand flywheel needs more investment, not less.

### 18. Treat a blog launch as a product launch with a pre-launch plan  `123.7`
*core · best practices · source 123*

Hyam's framing device is that nobody would build a SaaS product and launch it by sharing it with a few friends on Facebook, yet that is exactly how blogs get launched. A product launch has a game plan running up to launch day: pitching publications, posting to Product Hunt, posting in Facebook groups, getting influencers to share, doing outreach. He argues a blog deserves the same plan and that most companies go into a blog launch with no strategic plan for how they will generate the first waves of traffic from their intended audience. The plan itself does not have to be complicated. It reduces to one unique asset plus one distribution route, decided before anything is published.

> "You'd have an entire game plan leading up to launch day"

**Evidence:** Hyam applied the same two-step plan across three blogs, at ThinkApps, Grow and Convert and Vistage, with launch-week results of 20,000 visitors, 1,878 visitors and 3,000 day-one visitors respectively.

**How to do it**

1. Set a launch date and work backwards from it, the way you would for a product release.
2. Write the plan on one page: the unique asset, the audience, the distribution routes, and the target number for week one.
3. Pick the distribution routes explicitly from publication pitching, Product Hunt, community posting, influencer sharing and direct outreach.
4. Assign an owner and a date to each route before the asset is finished.
5. Build the email capture and the follow-up sequence before launch, since the launch traffic is one-time.
6. Hold publication until the distribution work is booked, not the reverse.
7. Review week-one numbers against the target and decide which routes to repeat.

**Tools:** Product Hunt

**Pitfall:** Publishing first and thinking about promotion afterwards. Hyam says most companies enter a blog launch with no plan for the first traffic waves, and the symptom is a live post with no pitch list behind it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any new Pabau content section, such as a new template hub or a code-reference cluster, should get a one-page launch plan with the flagship asset, the press or community list and a week-one traffic target, signed off before the first page publishes.

**Apply anywhere:** Plan a blog or content-hub launch the way you would plan a product launch: a one-page plan naming the flagship asset, the audience, each distribution route with an owner and a date, and a week-one traffic target, all settled before you publish.

### 19. Treat heavy technical and link emphasis as a hiring red flag  `163.3`
*core · best practices · source 163*

Grow and Convert report that clients' past SEO vendors spent most of their effort on on-page, technical SEO and link building, and much less on identifying high-value keywords and building pages to rank for them. They call that backwards. Their position is that technical work and links can only serve ranking for your highest-value keywords, and cannot generate revenue on their own. A technically healthy site with a stack of white-hat backlinks does very little without the keyword identification and the dedicated pages. So during vetting, ask how much weight the candidate places on technical SEO versus link building versus keyword strategy and content, and what portion of their time and effort goes to each. If they present the three as equally important, or lead with technical and links, they say think twice.

> "If they seem to emphasize technical SEO and link building heavily"

**Evidence:** Based on Grow and Convert's conversations with clients about their experiences with previous SEO services across dozens of accounts.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the candidate to split 100 points across technical SEO, link building, and keyword strategy plus content, for a typical engagement.
2. Ask separately what portion of their monthly hours each bucket consumes, since stated priorities and time often diverge.
3. Treat a roughly even split, or technical and links leading, as a red flag and probe why.
4. Ask what tangible results you can expect after 6 to 12 months, in the units you care about.
5. When they mention rankings, force specifics: how many keywords, which keywords, and which existing or new pages will rank for them.
6. Confirm any technical or link work they propose is tied to a named target keyword and page, not run as a standalone workstream.
7. Prefer the candidate who uses technical and links to support identified high-value keywords and dedicated pages.

**Pitfall:** Buying a large technical audit as the opening deliverable. It fills the first two months, produces a long ticket list, and moves no revenue keyword.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau scopes outside SEO help, David should require every technical or link recommendation to name the target keyword and the page it serves. A standalone audit with no keyword attached is not worth commissioning.

**Apply anywhere:** Require every technical or link recommendation from a vendor to name the target keyword and page it serves. A standalone audit with no keyword attached is not worth commissioning.

### 20. Treat keyword selection as the decision that makes the rest matter  `108.24`
*core · best practices · source 108*

Grow and Convert put keyword selection above everything else in the SaaS SEO stack and state the dependency plainly. If the keywords will not drive demos and trials when you rank for them, then the technical SEO does not matter, the link building does not matter, and the number of articles published does not matter. It does not matter how well a site is optimized if it is not ranking for terms that bring customers. This is why they treat the common SaaS complaint, plenty of blog traffic that barely converts, as a strategy problem rather than a tactical one. Their diagnostic follows from it: before auditing anything technical or commissioning outreach, check whether the target keyword list is made of terms a buyer would search. If it is not, fixing anything else changes nothing.

> "Thus, keyword selection is the most important thing to get right."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated hierarchy: technical SEO, link building and article volume are all conditional on keyword selection being right.

**How to do it**

1. Before any audit or outreach, export the full list of keywords your published pages target.
2. Mark each as software category, competitor comparison, jobs-to-be-done or top-of-funnel.
3. Calculate what share of the list carries buying intent.
4. If that share is under half, stop technical and link work and rebuild the keyword list first.
5. Rebuild it from the three buying-intent categories rather than from a volume-sorted tool export.
6. SERP-check each candidate for one to two minutes to confirm the intent.
7. Reassign writers to the new list before adding any further volume-driven posts.
8. Only resume technical and link investment once the pages being pushed target buyer terms.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Responding to weak content results with a technical audit or a link campaign. Both can be executed perfectly and change nothing, because the pages being improved target terms no buyer types.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau content program underperforms, audit the target keyword list before the site. If most articles target informational aesthetics topics, no amount of on-page or link work will produce demo bookings.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your keyword list before your site. Classify every targeted term by buying intent, and if most of the list is informational, rebuild the list before spending on technical fixes or links.

### 21. Treat wide-versus-narrow targeting as a lever you test both ways  `159.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 159*

Grow and Convert name the wide versus narrow problem as the hardest part of targeting, and say they end up working through it with 50-75% of new clients in the first month of an engagement, with clients openly admitting they do not know the answer. Their example runs from 'we are a product development agency' to 'mobile app development agency' to 'mobile app development agency focusing on android firmware'. Their own course went narrow first with B2B-only and failed, then went wide to any business and worked. They are explicit that neither direction is correct in general and that you may succeed going wide to narrow instead. The point is that this is an axis you can move without touching the product, unlike the months of wasted time a product change costs.

> "it's a matter of knowing that this is an axis you can play with"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report exploring the wide-versus-narrow question with 50-75% of new clients in the first month, and their own course succeeded only after moving from B2B-only to any business type.

**How to do it**

1. Write your current positioning statement as one sentence and mark where it sits on a wide-to-narrow scale.
2. Draft two alternatives: one a step narrower and one a step wider, changing only the audience or scope words.
3. For the narrow version, name the specific capability or segment you would claim to be best at.
4. For the wide version, check that the product genuinely serves the extra segments before claiming them.
5. Run each version as the hero on the main landing page for a fixed period with comparable traffic.
6. Compare conversion, not traffic, since a narrow page draws fewer but better-fit visitors.
7. Interview a few people who saw the losing version and ask what made them stop.
8. Keep the winner and leave the other in a documented note so the axis can be revisited later.

**Pitfall:** Assuming narrow is always better because that is the standard advice. Grow and Convert's own narrow bet failed and the wide version won.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should test whether landing pages read better as 'practice management software' or as segment-specific pages for aesthetics, dermatology and physiotherapy. Run it as a copy test on existing pages before commissioning a new page set.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat audience breadth as a testable lever. Draft a narrower and a wider version of your positioning, run each as the hero, and compare conversion rather than traffic.

### 22. Use bottom-of-funnel keywords as the route past higher-DA competitors  `153.15`
*core · content insights · source 153*

Grow and Convert draw a specific conclusion from their topical-relevance evidence. Domain authority plays a role but is not the only factor, and the way to compete against higher-DA sites is to focus on bottom-of-funnel keywords, the ones closely related to your niche and your products. The mechanism is that those terms sit inside your narrow topical lane, where a large generalist competitor is by definition the broader result, and they also carry the commercial intent that makes the ranking worth having. They say they frequently see client posts outranking competitors with higher DA, which they attribute to relevance and time rather than authority. This is a keyword-selection instruction, not a content-quality one.

> "Focusing on bottom of the funnel keywords — those closely related to your niche and products"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report client posts frequently outranking higher-DA competitors, with the Cognitive FX page ranking #1 above Mayo Clinic (DR 92) with zero backlinks to the page.

**How to do it**

1. Sort your keyword list by how close each term sits to the purchase decision for your product.
2. Start with the bottom-of-funnel block: alternatives, comparisons, pricing, use cases and problem-plus-product terms.
3. For each, check whether the ranking competitors cover a broader subject area than your site does.
4. Publish those first and leave broad informational terms until you have a body of bottom-of-funnel pages ranking.
5. Write each bottom-of-funnel page to convert as well as rank, positioning the product against the reader's named problem.
6. Track these pages on conversions as well as rankings, since that is the point of the funnel position.
7. Only move up the funnel once the bottom-of-funnel set has crossed into the top 10.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Building the library top-of-funnel first because the volumes look bigger. Those SERPs are where the high-DA generalists are strongest and where you have no relevance advantage.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's list should lead with bottom-of-funnel practice-software terms: alternatives, comparisons, pricing and specific practice-workflow problems. David should hold broad clinical and generic business content until those pages rank.

**Apply anywhere:** Build the bottom-of-funnel keywords first — alternatives, comparisons, pricing and problem-plus-product terms. Those sit inside your topical lane where large generalist competitors are the broader result, and they carry the intent that makes the ranking worth having.
