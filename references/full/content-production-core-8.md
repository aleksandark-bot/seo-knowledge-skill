### 1. Translate the product experience, not just the marketing pages  `62.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 62*

The Canva finding on internationalisation is more specific than 'translate your site'. They translate the whole product experience, not only the website and marketing materials - and the result cited is that they are the top design tool in Brazil, substantially because the platform is accessible in Portuguese. The narration's argument for why this compounds into SEO rather than just access: connecting with an audience in their own language builds trust and brand loyalty, and people who feel a company understands their language and culture are far more likely to engage with and share the content, which is the organic engagement that drives rankings.

> "their commitment to translated content"

**How to do it**

1. Identify the markets where you already have traction or where the category is under-served.
2. Translate the product interface, not only the marketing site - that's the difference they attribute the Brazil result to.
3. Translate the template and asset library, including the names and categories people search.
4. Localise rather than literally translate the keyword targets, since search behaviour differs by market.
5. Measure per-market organic growth separately so you can see which translations paid off.
6. Expand into the next market only once one is working.

**Pitfall:** Translating marketing pages while leaving the product in English is the half-measure this contrasts against - the visitor arrives via a translated page and immediately hits an English product.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, US versus UK English is the near-term version of this, but the deeper point holds: any market Pabau translates for needs the product and the template library translated too, not just the landing pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Regional English variants are the near-term version of this, but the deeper point holds: any market you translate for needs the product and the asset library translated too, not just the landing pages.

### 2. Treat AI as an amplifier of your existing process, good or bad  `88.3`
*core · best practices · source 88*

The author's central claim is that AI accelerates what you do well and what you do poorly in equal measure. An agency producing bad content with no real strategy will use AI to produce ten times more mediocre posts a month. A team with a real process, good people and creative ideas gets things it could not previously build. His framing is that AI does not replace thinking, it removes the bottlenecks that stopped the thinking from being executed. The practical consequence is an ordering rule: fix the process before adding the tool. Adopting AI on top of a weak content process does not fix the process, it multiplies its output. This matches the base's finding that unedited scaled AI output produces fast rankings then a crash.

> "AI accelerates what you do well and what you do poorly"

**Evidence:** The author's example: an agency with no real strategy can now publish 10x more mediocre blog posts per month.

**How to do it**

1. Before automating anything, write down the current process for the task, step by step, including who reviews the output.
2. Ask whether the last ten outputs of that process were good; if fewer than seven were, do not add AI to it yet.
3. Fix the weak step manually first, usually the brief, the research input or the review gate.
4. Then apply AI only to the specific bottleneck you identified, such as data analysis or chart production, not to the whole pipeline.
5. Keep a human decision point on story, angle and argument; automate only execution.
6. Measure output quality, not output volume, for the first month after adding the tool.
7. If volume rises and quality does not, cut the AI step back to a single stage.

**Pitfall:** The failure signal is a sudden jump in publishing volume with flat or falling conversions per article. Agencies and in-house teams under output quotas hit this fastest, because AI makes the quota easy to hit without making the content worth reading.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's factcheck-flow guides already act as the process layer. Keep the rule that no AI-assisted article ships without the human pass, and resist any push to raise the publishing rate before the review gate is proven at the current rate.

**Apply anywhere:** Document and fix your content process before adding AI to it. AI scales whatever process you already run, so a weak brief or a missing review step just produces more bad output faster.

### 3. Treat writers just don't get it as evidence of a broken process  `126.11`
*core · best practices · source 126*

Grow and Convert reframe the standard complaint about writer quality as a process diagnosis. They accept the complaint is partly fair, since one of the author's jobs is hiring writers and he sifts through mediocre article after mediocre article. But they argue the bigger issue is that the vast majority of companies producing blog content have a broken content creation process, and with a broken process even really good writers produce bad content, making it look like they just do not get it. They go further: asking writers to just get it is itself unreasonable and is the smoke signal for a broken process. They say it typically comes from founder or CEO types with decades of industry experience who expect to find writers who happen to hold the same nuanced understanding of the niche, ready to write deeply thought-provoking articles with minimal editing, for a few hundred dollars a piece.

> "the smoke signal for a broken process"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite Peep Laja's tweet about hundreds of applicants all producing mediocre work as the symptom, and the author's own hiring experience as agreement.

**How to do it**

1. When a draft disappoints, write down exactly which input the writer was missing rather than rating the writer.
2. Check whether the missing input was industry nuance, product positioning or experience-based detail.
3. Assign each missing input to an internal owner who supplies it before the next piece.
4. Stop reviewing writer applications until the inputs exist, since better applicants will fail the same way.
5. Compare your pay rate against the expectation: minimal editing and founder-level nuance is not a few hundred dollars of work.
6. Re-test the same writer after fixing the inputs before replacing them.

**Pitfall:** Churning through writers. Each replacement costs hiring time and produces the same result, because the defect is upstream of whoever holds the keyboard.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau draft lands flat, David should log which input was missing rather than the writer's score. If the answer is repeatedly 'no internal expert input', the fix is scheduling interviews, not a new writer.

**Apply anywhere:** When a draft is mediocre, name the input the writer did not have instead of rating the writer. Asking a writer to just get your niche is a symptom of a broken process, and replacing the writer reproduces the same output.

### 4. Turn a working accident into a named, documented method  `176.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 176*

Grow and Convert had produced a few SEO articles by accident, targeting terms they would now call bottom of the funnel. When those converted, they did not just publish more of the same. They coined the term Pain Point SEO to describe the method and pivoted the whole agency's positioning and process around it. Naming the method is what makes it repeatable across a team and defensible to clients, and it is also what makes drifting away from it visible. They have run the same system for over five years. The lesson is that commitment is an operational act, not a state of mind: the method gets a name, a documented process, and the positioning of the business behind it.

> "coined the term 'Pain Point SEO' to describe the method"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert pivoted their entire agency positioning around Pain Point SEO and have used it for over five years across B2B, B2C, SaaS, service and ecommerce clients.

**How to do it**

1. Identify the pieces that produced the result and write down what they had in common: keyword type, format, funnel stage.
2. Give the pattern a short name that describes the mechanism, not the output.
3. Write the process as a repeatable brief template covering keyword selection, structure and the conversion element.
4. Change your positioning and your sales or stakeholder language to lead with that name.
5. Require every new piece to pass the documented process or be explicitly logged as an exception.
6. Review the exception log quarterly; a rising count is drift, not innovation.

**Pitfall:** Naming a method before you have evidence for it produces a brand with no engine behind it. Only name the pattern after the conversion comparison is done.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should write the winning article pattern into the existing content guides as a named route with its own brief template, so every writer and agent produces it the same way rather than re-deriving it per article.

**Apply anywhere:** Once a content pattern proves itself, name it, write it up as a brief template, and put your positioning behind it so the team cannot quietly drift off it.

### 5. Turn one podcast episode into an ebook gated behind a Tally form  `68.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 68*

Cody's fastest email-list mechanic is to convert each long-form episode into a digital download. He takes the transcript, pulls out the insights, and packages them as a PDF positioned like this: I sat down with X, we talked about Y, and I learned these five things, link below for the full PDF. The PDF sits behind a Tally form, so the reader trades an email address for it. That is what turns the podcast into a newsletter machine rather than a stand-alone show. He is blunt about how simple this is: offering free stuff behind an email wall is the simplest thing you can do to grow an email list quickly. The same transcript also feeds email newsletters, blog posts, long-form LinkedIn posts and Twitter threads, so the ebook is one output of a single extraction pass, not extra work.

> "you put it behind a tally form and they get you their email"

**Evidence:** Cody describes this as the flywheel component: transcript becomes scheduled posts, posts carry the newsletter call-out, ebook captures the email.

**How to do it**

1. Run the episode through transcription software and save the full transcript.
2. Prompt an AI model to pull the distinct insights out of the transcript, not a summary.
3. Write those insights up as a short PDF titled around the guest and the specific lesson count.
4. Build a Tally form that captures email and delivers the PDF on submit.
5. Post the clip or the episode with a call to action naming the download, not the episode.
6. Add the submitted emails to the newsletter list automatically.
7. Reuse the same insight list for the newsletter issue, a blog post and a LinkedIn post.

**Tools:** Tally

**Pitfall:** A PDF that just reprints the transcript gets downloaded once and never opened. The insight-extraction pass is what makes it worth an email address.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau records practitioner interviews, each one should ship a gated PDF on the site alongside the article, matching the existing template-download box pattern already used on template pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn each long-form interview you record into a short insight PDF behind an email form. It costs one extra extraction pass and it converts your audience into a list you own.

### 6. Turn other people's podcasts into a newsletter growth machine  `57.19`
*core · ai workflows · source 57*

Cody found this being run against his own show. An operator takes long-form podcast episodes - he discovered it because they took his episode with a guest who explained sending 1.5 million cold emails a month - transcribes them, has AI turn the transcript into a blog post, publishes it to a relevant subreddit, and puts a call to action at the bottom to subscribe to a newsletter for more content like this. Going back through the operator's profile, every post was the same play, and the newsletter's entire list acquisition was that loop. His analysis of why it's efficient: the content already exists and has already been validated as interesting, so the writing lift is nothing and the risk of picking a dud topic is nothing. Jackie has run it himself and reports a ceiling - a couple of hundred subscribers max, about 100 at his best - but Cody's arithmetic is that a post a day at 300 subscribers a day is 10,000 a month and 100,000 in ten months, and business newsletters at 100,000 subscribers currently sell for $1 million to $1.5 million. Jackie's own version, which he points listeners to, is rewriting news he sees on X and publishing it to subreddits like /r/entrepreneur with a CTA at the bottom; he reports posts at 1,200 upvotes and 300,000 views.

> "turned it into a transcript"

**How to do it**

1. Build a monitoring list of long-form podcasts and YouTube channels in your category whose episodes reliably perform.
2. Pick episodes by demonstrated traction - view counts, comment volume - so you are repurposing validated interest rather than guessing.
3. Pull the transcript (captions via yt-dlp, or the podcast's own transcript) as the raw material.
4. Have the model turn the transcript into a structured article, keeping the guest's specific claims and numbers, since those are what make it worth reading.
5. Credit the source episode and link to it - this is what separates repurposing from theft and is also what stops the community removing it.
6. Publish to the subreddit where that topic's practitioners actually are, in the community's format.
7. Put the newsletter CTA at the bottom of the post rather than in the title.
8. Run it daily. Both the operator Cody found and Jackie's own version depend on cadence, not on any single post.
9. Track subscribers per post so you can see which podcasts and which subreddits actually convert, and drop the rest.
10. Model the exit if that's the goal - Cody's figure is $1m-$1.5m for a business newsletter at 100,000 subscribers.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Claude

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Here is the transcript of a podcast episode. Turn it into a structured article that keeps every specific claim, number and example the guest gave, in their framing. Credit the source episode at the top. Do not add claims that aren't in the transcript.
```

**Pitfall:** Jackie's real-world ceiling was around 100 subscribers per post, not 300, so Cody's arithmetic is the optimistic case. The bigger constraint is that republishing someone else's substance without credit is both a copyright exposure and the thing that gets the account banned - the credit link is what makes the play sustainable.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's version doesn't need a subreddit: the same loop applied to industry conference talks and webinars - transcribe, structure, credit, publish, CTA - turns events Pabau already attends into a repeatable content and list-building stream.

**Apply anywhere:** The same loop applied to industry conference talks and webinars - transcribe, structure, credit, publish, CTA - turns events you already attend into a repeatable content and list-building stream, without needing a subreddit at all.

### 7. Unpublish zero-click old pages, then republish them fresh post-migration  `45.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 45*

David's specific, named trick for smoothing out the traffic dip that typically follows a migration: identify older blog posts/pages that have had zero clicks in the last six months, unpublish and no-index them before the migration (not redirect them), then republish them on a new slug as if they were brand-new content once the site has gone live. Because these pages may have accumulated topical authority over time even without recent clicks (and may have originally had an unfavorable, overly long slug), republishing them fresh gives Google new content to discover and rank right when the migrated site's own rankings are in flux from re-indexing, creating a second traffic curve that offsets the temporary dip and produces a smoother overall trend line.

> "Find pages that have had no clicks in the last six months"

**How to do it**

1. Pull a report of all blog posts/pages with zero organic clicks in the trailing six months, filtered by page and sorted by clicks ascending in GSC's Performance report (inferred).
2. Review each candidate page's current URL slug — flag ones with overly long or poorly structured slugs as strong candidates, since these may not have been recrawled recently.
3. Before the migration, unpublish and no-index the selected pages (do not set up a redirect from them).
4. Proceed with the site migration as planned.
5. After the new site is live and stabilizing, take the content from each unpublished page and republish it on a brand-new slug, presented as new content rather than restored content.
6. Stagger the republishing of these old-but-new-again pages across the weeks following migration to create a steady stream of 'new' content signals rather than one large batch (inferred).
7. Track clicks/impressions on the newly republished slugs in GSC to confirm they are picking up fresh traffic and helping offset any migration-related dip (inferred verification step).

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Confusing this with a redirect strategy — David is explicit that this is NOT about 301-redirecting the old slug to the new one; the old page must be unpublished and no-indexed, then the content republished fresh on a different slug entirely.

### 8. Use an LLM as a linguist to coach non-native SEO writers  `27.13`
*core · ai workflows · source 27*

For a non-native English speaker doing SEO writing, the recommended workflow is not to let the LLM ghost-write the content, but to use it as a linguist and teacher: feed it a set of your own writing samples and have it construct a thorough style guide from them, then use the same tool to actively teach linguistics and grammar rather than silently auto-correcting. When the writer produces a draft with problems, the LLM is used to explain what's wrong and correct it, described as something LLMs are "actually very good at", while the underlying informational structure and subject-matter judgment still has to come from the human writer, who reviews the output sentence by sentence and paragraph by paragraph. In the real example given, the non-native-English operator built a style guide from the speaker's own bottom-of-funnel SEO landing pages so all new content matched that established style, while still personally verifying accuracy and sales framing throughout.

> "give it writing samples and have it construct a thorough style guide"

**How to do it**

1. Collect a set of your own best-performing writing samples, the real example used existing bottom-of-funnel SEO landing pages.
2. Feed those samples to an LLM and ask it to construct a detailed style guide capturing tone, sentence structure, and conventions from the samples.
3. Have the non-native-English writer draft content as normal, using their own subject-matter knowledge for the underlying information structure.
4. Submit each draft to the LLM specifically to flag and explain grammar or phrasing problems, rather than asking the LLM to rewrite the piece wholesale.
5. Have the writer review the LLM's explanations and corrections, learning the underlying grammar or linguistic rule each time rather than just accepting the fix (inferred, consistent with the source's framing of the LLM as a teaching tool).
6. Have the writer, or another human who knows the business, do a final sentence-by-sentence, paragraph-by-paragraph pass to confirm accuracy and that claims are sold or framed correctly.

**Pitfall:** Letting the LLM's grammar corrections replace the human's ownership of the underlying content structure would remove the exact check this workflow depends on — the source is explicit that the human writer still has to know if something's incorrect or not sold properly, with the LLM handling only the linguistic layer.

### 9. Use subject matter experts as the voice behind the whole piece  `99.8`
*core · best practices · source 99*

Khanal's diagnosis is that most companies commission blog posts from freelance writers who have roughly the knowledge he has about surfing: he has taken multiple lessons, stood up on a board a few times, and Googled and watched videos about it. He calls this mid-beginner expertise. The problem is that the posts are meant to impress customers who often know far more, and for B2B the customer is typically a professional with years or decades in the space. A writer cannot impress an industry veteran after Googling around for a couple of hours. Grow and Convert's solution inside their agency is to use subject matter experts to guide entire pieces, not just to supply a few quotes, but literally to be the voice behind all of the ideas in the piece. He adds an explicit caveat: even with an expert, the writing still needs enough detail to pass the reader's smell test, so the writer is not off the hook.

> "not just to provide a few quotes, but literally to be the voice"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use this model in their own content marketing agency; Khanal recommends it directly.

**How to do it**

1. Before commissioning, rate the writer's real expertise against the target reader's years in the role.
2. If the gap is large, assign a subject matter expert to own the ideas in the piece, not to supply pull quotes.
3. Interview the expert against the article's outline so every section has expert-sourced substance.
4. Have the writer draft from that interview, keeping the expert's specific examples and numbers.
5. Send the draft back to the expert with the unbackable-claim list as follow-up questions.
6. Run the detail pass on the final draft anyway, since expert sourcing does not guarantee detail survives the writing.
7. Reject any section where the expert material has been generalized into category-baseline advice.

**Pitfall:** Teams treat the expert quote as the deliverable and let the writer produce the rest from search results. The result reads expert in two paragraphs and generic everywhere else.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles on clinical workflow, compliance and treatment protocols should be built on interviews with practitioners or internal product people who own the workflow, and every section should carry their material, not just one quote box.

**Apply anywhere:** Build articles on clinical or technical workflows from an interview with a practitioner who owns that workflow, and make sure every section carries their material rather than one quote box.

### 10. Vet a content agency by naming the goal metric first  `101.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 101*

Grow and Convert open their five-step vetting process by forcing the buyer to decide what content is for before any candidate is interviewed. They say most companies want leads or sales (ecommerce purchases, sales call signups, SaaS trials and demos), but some later-stage companies with large existing lead numbers legitimately want traffic and email growth. Their point is that this is a choice, and that optimizing for traffic and email signups is a genuinely different strategy from optimizing for leads and revenue. Agencies whose strategy is built for traffic are easy to find; ones that produce attributable leads are not. So state the goal explicitly to every candidate up front and base every later question on how they would hit that specific number.

> "It's important to understand that this is a choice"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert have run their agency for over seven years and filtered 1000+ writer applicants.

**How to do it**

1. Pick one primary outcome for content: leads and sales, or traffic and email signups.
2. Write the goal as a number with a timeframe, not a direction, before you contact anyone.
3. Say the goal out loud in the first call and ask the candidate to respond to it directly.
4. Discard candidates who reframe your lead goal as a traffic or awareness goal.
5. Build every remaining vetting question around how they would move that one metric.
6. Record the goal in the contract so reporting is later judged against it.

**Pitfall:** Buyers who leave the goal vague get the agency's default strategy, which is almost always traffic-and-awareness content, then find nothing converts a year in.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should state for each pabau.com content stream what it is for: template pages and code-reference pages exist to capture buying-intent demand, so their success metric is demo requests, not sessions. Any freelancer or agency brief should carry that number.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide before hiring whether content exists to produce leads or to produce traffic, write the target as a number, and make every vetting question about that number. The two goals need different strategies, and vagueness gets you the vendor's default.

### 11. Wait for 50 daily visits before building a post's content upgrade  `148.3`
*core · best practices · source 148*

Shour deliberately does not add a content upgrade when a post publishes, even though he agrees it is not a bad idea. His content strategy is SEO-led, so he waits until a post ranks for its target keyword and pulls consistent organic traffic before spending time on a PDF and the automation rules behind it. His stated rule of thumb is at least 50 unique visits per day to the page, watched on a rank tracker and in Google Analytics, and he wants a couple of weeks of that consistent traffic before starting. The purpose is triage: an in-house content marketer has too much else on, and if a post turns out to be a dud, the upgrade work is wasted. Devesh Khanal calls it a genius time-saving strategy and says the team may borrow it.

> "at least 50 unique visits to the page/day"

**Evidence:** Shour's rule of thumb of 50 unique visits per day, held for about two weeks, before creating a post-specific content upgrade.

**How to do it**

1. Publish the post with no content upgrade and no opt-in automation attached.
2. Add the target keyword to your rank tracker and watch position weekly.
3. Check Google Analytics for the page's daily unique visits once it enters the first page.
4. Wait until the page holds at least 50 unique visits per day.
5. Confirm that level holds for roughly two consecutive weeks before committing time.
6. Only then produce the PDF or asset specific to that post.
7. Set up the opt-in forms and automation rules in the same session as the asset.
8. Leave posts that never reach the threshold with no upgrade and revisit them at the next refresh.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Building an upgrade for every new post spends the same hours on posts that never rank. The tell is a folder of PDFs attached to pages doing single-digit sessions a day.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should apply the same gate to template downloads and lead magnets. Before David commissions a new downloadable for an article, check Analytics for 50 daily sessions sustained over two weeks and skip the ones below it.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish first, then wait for the page to hold about 50 unique visits a day for two weeks before building its lead magnet. Posts that never reach that level never earn the production time.

### 12. Weight the first content hire toward grow skills over convert skills  `165.1`
*core · best practices · source 165*

Benji Hyam splits content marketing into two skill halves. His own 'grow' half is user and customer research, content strategy and ideation, content promotion, and pulling insight out of analytics. His co-founder Devesh's 'convert' half is customer research via surveys and Hotjar, operations and project management, editing, conversion optimization, and analytics setup. Hyam says the grow skills matter most for a first hire, and Devesh agrees in the article: without strategy and promotion driving traffic, conversion optimization has nothing to work on. The convert skills become important when you scale the operation, not when you start it. The practical consequence is that a candidate who can name pain points from customer calls and list promotion channels beats one whose portfolio is CRO tests, for hire number one.

> "the "grow" skills are the most important"

**Evidence:** Hyam grew content operations at Vistage, ThinkApps and Everwise, and ThinkApps went from 0 to 35,000 visitors in six months on the grow skill set.

**How to do it**

1. Write two columns before interviewing: grow skills (customer research, strategy and ideation, promotion, analytics interpretation) and convert skills (CRO, analytics setup, operations, editing).
2. Score every candidate separately on the two columns rather than one overall rating.
3. For a first hire, require evidence on at least three of the four grow skills and treat convert skills as a bonus.
4. Test customer research directly: ask what survey questions they wrote and what they learned from a customer call.
5. Test promotion directly by handing them one of your live articles and asking where they would place it.
6. Defer the convert profile to hire two, once traffic exists to optimize.
7. If you can only afford one person and they are grow-weighted, budget contractor hours for analytics and goal setup, which Hyam admits he cannot do himself.

**Tools:** Hotjar, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Hiring the conversion-optimization profile first leaves a blog with nothing to optimize. The signal is a well-instrumented site with a handful of posts and no distribution plan.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau adds content headcount, David should score candidates against the grow column first: can they interview practice owners, turn that into topics, and place articles in aesthetics communities. Pabau already has analytics, so the gap is research and promotion.

**Apply anywhere:** Split content marketing skills into grow (research, strategy, promotion, analytics reading) and convert (CRO, analytics setup, editing, ops). Score candidates on both columns and hire the grow profile first, because conversion work needs traffic to act on.

### 13. Write H1s with target keyword plus an explicit benefit  `36.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 36*

A weak H1 states only what the thing is (a bare category label), while a strong H1 adds the concrete benefit the visitor gets, and this single change is credited with taking one landing page's revenue from $1,000 a month to $25,000 a month with no change in traffic volume or quality. Three cross-vertical examples are given: a local roofing business should replace a generic company-name headline with one naming the service, city, and speed of delivery; a SaaS product should replace a bare product-category headline with one stating the specific outcome and time-to-value; and an ecommerce mattress brand should replace a bare product-name headline with one stating the physical benefit. The formula is target keyword first, then the benefit the searcher gets, and this benefit framing matters most on bottom-of-funnel commercial pages, while on purely informational content the benefit can be implicit in the keyword itself.

> "focusing on having a clear H1, a clear top headline"

**How to do it**

1. Audit every existing page's H1/top headline and flag any that state only a category or product name with no stated benefit.
2. For each flagged page, identify the page's target keyword and the single strongest concrete benefit a visitor gets (time saved, speed, a specific outcome, comfort, a number).
3. Rewrite the H1 as target keyword plus a specific stated benefit, following the pattern of naming the service and a concrete outcome in one line.
4. For bottom-of-funnel or commercial pages, make the benefit explicit and concrete in the H1 itself, since these visitors are closer to a buying decision and need the payoff stated plainly.
5. For purely informational or top-of-funnel pages, it is acceptable for the benefit to be implied naturally by the keyword rather than spelled out. (inferred)
6. After rewriting, monitor bounce rate and conversion rate on the page for at least a few weeks to confirm the change improves engagement rather than just changing wording. (inferred: use Google Analytics or the site's existing CRO tool)

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Writing an H1 that only names the product or category instead of pairing the keyword with an explicit, concrete benefit — this single fix is credited with a 25x revenue change on one landing page with no change in traffic.

### 14. Write a 150-word modular executive summary sized for Crunchbase  `73.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 73*

Barnard's solution to platforms having different description length limits is a modular description system. The base unit is an executive summary of about 150 words, sized because that is what fits on Crunchbase, and because every other platform they use allows that length or more, Twitter being the obvious exception. Below it he writes the long description as separate chunks, each with a heading and a paragraph, each treating one distinct facet of the business or person. To fill a platform, you paste the executive summary, then append whichever chunks are relevant to that platform's audience and drop the rest. His example: a line about serving people fluffy unicorns and rainbows can be cut for Crunchbase because it is irrelevant to that audience. The payoff is two-sided - each human audience gets the information that matters to them, and Google and Bing see very consistent descriptions across the web without every copy being byte-identical.

> "executive summary which is about I think it's 150 words"

**Evidence:** Barnard's team built exactly this system for the client that reached a knowledge panel in four to five months, with Crunchbase as the platform they sized to.

**How to do it**

1. Write one executive summary of roughly 150 words containing the semantic triple, what you do, who you serve, where, and why you are credible.
2. Confirm it fits Crunchbase's field limit, and treat that as the hard cap.
3. Write the long description as separate modules, each with its own heading and a single paragraph covering one facet.
4. Keep each module self-contained so removing it does not break the surrounding text.
5. For each platform, paste the executive summary first, then append only the modules relevant to that platform's audience.
6. On LinkedIn and other long-field platforms, paste the executive summary plus every module.
7. On Crunchbase, put the executive summary in the short field and the full assembled text in the long description field.
8. Store the master document in one place and edit only there, so every platform update starts from the same source.

**Tools:** Crunchbase, LinkedIn, Twitter/X

**Pitfall:** Writing a bespoke description per platform. You lose the consistency signal that raises entity confidence, and updating a fact later means hunting down a dozen unrelated wordings.

**Apply at Pabau:** Create one master Pabau description document: a 150-word executive summary plus headed modules for aesthetics practices, healthcare clinics, the mobile app and the reporting side. Paste the summary plus relevant modules into each directory rather than rewriting.

**Apply anywhere:** Build one 150-word executive summary sized for Crunchbase, plus headed one-paragraph modules for each facet of the business. Assemble each platform's description from the summary plus the relevant modules, and edit only the master.

### 15. Write a differentiator inventory before drafting any GEO article  `82.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 82*

Grow and Convert argue the level of detail in your public content dictates how LLMs talk about your product, so they fix the positioning before writing. For Level AI they worked from four named differentiators: semantic intelligence that reads full conversation context rather than keywords, emotion detection in customer conversations, auto-scoring of all conversations against the client's QA rubrics, and deep analysis that surfaces hidden issues without surveys or sampling bias. Each one is stated as a contrast with what competitors do, not as an adjective. Those four lines then appear across every article, and ChatGPT reproduces them almost word for word when recommending the product. The artifact matters as much as the writing: an agreed list of differentiators, each with the competitor shortcoming it answers, that every writer copies rather than reinvents.

> "who it's for, use cases, features and benefits, pain points it solves"

**Evidence:** ChatGPT's Level AI recommendations reproduced the semantic-understanding and emotion-detection claims in nearly the same words as the published articles.

**How to do it**

1. Interview the product and sales teams and list every claimed differentiator.
2. Cut any that a competitor could also claim, and any that is only an adjective.
3. Rewrite each survivor as a contrast sentence naming what most alternatives do instead.
4. Attach a hard figure or a named mechanism to each one where possible.
5. Add the customer fit alongside them: who it is for, the use cases, the pain points solved.
6. Publish the list internally as the single source writers copy from, verbatim.
7. Insert the relevant differentiator lines into every bottom-funnel article rather than paraphrasing them per page.
8. Every quarter, prompt ChatGPT for a recommendation in your category and check whether its wording matches your list; rewrite the weakest line if not.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Letting each writer paraphrase the differentiators produces slightly different wording per page, which weakens the consensus signal and gives the model no single phrase to reuse.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau needs a written differentiator sheet for the content team covering the every-feature-included subscription, the structured onboarding and the aesthetic and healthcare specialism. Every blog and template page should use those exact lines rather than fresh phrasing.

**Apply anywhere:** Agree a written differentiator sheet before writing. State each one as a contrast with what competitors do, attach a figure, and have every article reuse the exact wording.

### 16. Write a disruption story to convert readers who never search  `91.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 91*

Grow and Convert have used disruption stories for six years as the non-search half of their content program. A disruption story captures why the business exists: the problem the founders hit and the better solution they built. It carries no keyword and no SEO angle, and it reaches its audience through paid and organic social instead of the SERP. The point is conversion from people who are not actively searching but who share the pain. They contrast it with their Pain Point SEO methodology, which ranks for high-intent keywords to catch people already looking. They also separate it from ordinary top-of-funnel content such as whitepapers, ebooks and guides, because those never sell the product while a disruption story is entirely about the product and why it was created.

> "using disruption stories as a way to drive conversions from readers that are not actively searching"

**Evidence:** Six years of use across dozens of client stories; Grow and Convert's own 'why we started' article produced a steady stream of agency leads in early 2024 while being promoted on paid social.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the single problem the founders set out to solve and the better way you solve it, in two or three sentences.
2. Confirm the story is about the product itself, not an industry primer, so it can sell rather than just inform.
3. Assign no keyword and no search volume target to the piece; it is not an SEO page.
4. Draft the piece as a narrative with a provocative title and a pain-naming intro (see the title and intro rules).
5. Publish it on your own blog so you own the URL and can link to it from other posts.
6. Promote it through paid social and organic social posts rather than waiting for rankings.
7. Track conversions from the article specifically, since conversion is the only success metric that matters here.
8. Keep it alongside, not instead of, your bottom-of-funnel keyword pages.

**Pitfall:** Teams file this under top-of-funnel and strip the selling out of it, which turns it into another ungated guide that converts nobody. If the draft never gets into your product's features and how they fix the stated pain, it is not a disruption story.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish one disruption story on pabau.com about why Pabau was built for aesthetic and healthcare practices, naming the specific admin pain the founders faced. It sits outside the /blog/ keyword plan, carries no target keyword, and gets promoted through paid social to clinic owners instead of waiting on rankings.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish one article that says plainly why your product exists: the problem you hit, why the usual way of solving it is broken, and how your product fixes it. Give it no keyword target and promote it on paid and organic social, then measure it on conversions rather than traffic.

### 17. Write a one-scene reader narrative before drafting any introduction  `127.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 127*

Devesh at Grow and Convert opens the piece by refusing to judge two CRM intros until the reader has been put inside the target persona. He does not write a persona bullet list. He writes a short second-person scene: it is a Wednesday evening, you are on the train home scrolling email and social, your team is doing okay but should do better, you are 41 with 20 years in sales, you want the bonus, and your actual live problem is that your team's reporting is completely messed up. Only after that scene does he say the choice between the two intros has a right answer. The point of the exercise is that the scene surfaces two things a persona sheet does not: what the reader already knows cold, and the one narrow problem they have right now. Both of those decide the first sentence.

> "your team's reporting is completely messed up"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use the sales-manager commute scene to make the reader agree that intro #2 wins before any argument is made.

**How to do it**

1. Write a 100-150 word second-person scene of the reader in the moment they meet your post, before you draft a word of the intro.
2. Fix the time and place in the scene, for example a Wednesday evening commute scrolling a phone, not 'at work'.
3. State the reader's age and years of experience in the topic, so you can see what they already know.
4. Name their career or commercial stake in solving this, for example hitting target, the bonus, the raise.
5. Name the one specific live problem that made them click, phrased in their words.
6. Add the line that they are thinking about 400 other things, so you write for a distracted skim not a captive audience.
7. Keep the scene at the top of the brief and check every intro sentence against it.
8. Reject any opening sentence that tells the person in that scene something they already know.

**Pitfall:** Persona sheets built from firmographics (industry, company size, job title) do not surface what the reader already knows, so writers keep opening with category explanation. If your persona doc has no sentence about experience level, your intros will be written below the reader.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should add a short reader-scene block to the top of every Pabau brief. For a clinic-software article it is a practice owner between patients, twelve years in aesthetics, already running a booking system she dislikes. That scene rules out openings that explain why patient records matter.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a short reader-scene block to the top of every content brief: a specific time, place, experience level, commercial stake and the one live problem behind the click. Write the introduction against that scene and cut any sentence that tells that person something they already know.

### 18. Write down searcher intent before drafting any SEO page  `47.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 47*

Edward describes a specific pre-writing exercise he introduced for his own SEO-focused company after noticing their bottom-of-funnel landing pages weren't performing as well as they could: before writing any SEO content, spend a couple of minutes explicitly answering, in writing, who is searching this target keyword and what they're trying to achieve or want. He says just forcing yourself to write that down as a short note before drafting the actual content measurably improves the content's quality, compellingness, and ability to satisfy searcher intent, which is his own working definition of quality content, meaning content where searchers are satisfied and don't pogo-stick back to the search results. He contrasts this deliberate intent-first approach with simply handing a keyword to ChatGPT or Claude and asking it to write a page, arguing the few minutes of human thought produces meaningfully better content than an AI-only pass.

> "writing it down as a note for yourself"

**How to do it**

1. Before drafting any new SEO landing page or article, open a blank note and write down the exact target keyword you're optimizing for.
2. Answer in writing: who specifically is searching this keyword, considering their role, situation, and level of knowledge.
3. Answer in writing: what is this searcher trying to achieve or solve, and what do they want out of the page.
4. Spend no more than a couple of minutes on this note - the goal is quick, deliberate framing, not a full research document.
5. Only after completing the note, begin writing, or prompting an AI tool to help draft, the actual SEO content, using the note to guide structure and emphasis.
6. After publishing, monitor whether the page shows signs of pogo-sticking, such as high bounce-back-to-search-results behavior or low average engagement time, as a rough signal of whether the intent note matched the real searcher (inferred).
7. Apply this exercise to every single piece of SEO content going forward, not just a one-off pilot, since it is meant as a standing habit change, not a one-time project.

**Pitfall:** Handing a target keyword straight to ChatGPT or Claude and asking it to write the page, without first doing this couple-of-minutes intent-framing step, produces measurably weaker, less compelling content than doing the human intent exercise first, even though both approaches technically use AI to help write.

### 19. Write foundational GEO pages from internal interviews, not AI or freelancers  `79.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 79*

Grow & Convert state flatly that the foundational bottom-of-funnel content should not be AI generated, and should not be written by a freelancer who does not know the product. Their method is interview-based: they interview the most knowledgeable people in the organization about how the product should be positioned, naming sales, founders, product leaders and the head of marketing. The output has to cover who the product is for, how it is used, what pain points it solves, benefits and features in detail. Their reasoning is that LLMs act like your SDRs, summarizing and pitching your product to a user via this content, so a good page is a good pitch and a vague page is a vague pitch. The detail is also what lets you match the extremely specific queries a buyer types into ChatGPT.

> "we use our interview-based writing method to interview"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert's stated firm belief and their standing interview-based writing method used across client engagements.

**How to do it**

1. Book 30 to 45 minutes each with a salesperson who closes deals, a founder, a product lead and the head of marketing.
2. Ask each for the exact objections buyers raise and the words buyers use, not the marketing positioning.
3. Ask specifically who the product is wrong for, since non-ideal customers are part of the detail that helps a model match correctly.
4. Pull the differentiators against named competitors and the numbers behind them.
5. Draft the page from the transcripts so the claims trace to a named internal source.
6. Cover features, use, pain points and benefits in concrete detail rather than category-level prose.
7. Have the sales interviewee review the draft as if it were a pitch they would send a prospect.

**Pitfall:** AI-generated or freelance-written foundational pages read as category-level prose with no specifics, so a model has nothing distinctive to repeat and recommends whoever wrote in detail instead.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau this means the practice-management-software pillar pages should be built from interviews with Pabau sales and product staff about which practice types Pabau fits and which it does not, then written up. That is a change to the brief stage, not the writing stage.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your pillar commercial pages from interviews with the people who sell and build the product, covering who it fits and who it does not, then write from those transcripts.

### 20. Write from two sources: what already ranks plus a 30-minute self-interview  `56.4`
*core · ai workflows · source 56*

For bottom-of-funnel articles (X versus Y, X alternative, X review) Cody's first draft is AI-written, but never from the model's general knowledge. He scrapes everything currently ranking on page one for the target keyword and puts it in the context window, then records himself talking for about 30 minutes about that category - his own opinions, his experiences, where he thinks the market is going. Both go in as source material. His reasoning is that the scraped pages tell you what Google already considers a good answer for that query, and the interview supplies the point of view that makes the piece not generic. He also repeats the same advice in his solo videos: the interview transcript is what turns a mid article into a good one, and he mentions Cloudflare having just shipped a scraper that returns whole HTML pages as JSON for exactly this step.

> "I'll interview myself for like 30 minutes"

**How to do it**

1. Pick the target keyword, weighted toward bottom-of-funnel patterns: X vs Y, X alternative, X review, best X for Y.
2. Scrape the full text of everything ranking on page one for that keyword and load it into the context window as source material.
3. Record yourself (or the subject-matter expert) talking freely for about 30 minutes on that category - opinions, war stories, market predictions, objections you hear from buyers.
4. Transcribe that recording and add it as a second, clearly labelled source alongside the scraped pages.
5. Add your style guide and tone-of-voice rules as a third input.
6. Ask for an outline first that consolidates everything the ranking pages cover, then have it write section by section against that outline.
7. Have a human edit the result, then publish and run the internal-link and CTA pass over it.

**Pitfall:** The complaint that AI content is low quality is, in his view, almost always a prompt problem - 'write me a blog post about X' writes to the average of the bell curve. Without the ranking pages and the interview transcript you get exactly that.

Grow and Convert are blunt that most freelance writers cannot write an effective disruption story, especially when handed only a brief or outline. ### 22. Write the draft first, then run Clearscope, never the reverse  `140.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 140*

Lewis is explicit about the order of operations with Clearscope, and it is a deliberate reversal of common practice. She writes the whole article before generating the Clearscope report, then optimizes against it, aiming for an A+ grade without changing the context of the article. Her reason is that building the report first turns writing into keyword placement. Writing first lets her decide which points she wants to make with no pressure about where terms should go. The constraint she puts on the optimization pass is the important half: she will not take a grade improvement that changes the meaning of a sentence. This is a useful discipline for anyone whose editorial process has a content-scoring tool in it, and it contradicts the standard brief-then-write workflow where the term list is handed to the writer up front.

> "allows for me to think through which points"

**Evidence:** Lewis aimed for an A+ Clearscope rating without changing the context of the article across the ADA Compliance Pros campaign that produced 5-15 leads a month.

**How to do it**

1. Plan the article from the interview notes and the outline, not from a term list.
2. Write the full draft with no scoring tool open.
3. Generate the Clearscope report only after the draft is complete.
4. Add missing terms only where a sentence was going to say that anyway, and skip terms that would need a fabricated sentence.
5. Stop optimizing the moment a change alters the meaning or the argument of a paragraph.
6. Accept a lower grade rather than insert a term that reads as placed.
7. Log which terms you refused and why, so the same argument is not reopened in review.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Handing the writer the term report first produces articles built around a word list. The signal is paragraphs that exist only to house a phrase, and a reader who cannot say what the article argued.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should keep the entity and term list out of the writer's hands until the Pabau draft exists. Run the term check as an edit pass on the finished article, and drop any term that would need a sentence invented to hold it.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the complete draft before you open a content-scoring tool. Generate the report afterwards and add terms only where a sentence was already heading that way. Refuse any term that requires inventing a sentence, and accept a lower grade rather than degrade the argument.
