### 1. Make promotion scope explicit before signing a content agency  `101.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 101*

Step four of the vetting process asks what the candidate actually does to get traffic to the articles, whether it is included or sold separately, and what it consists of. Grow and Convert dismiss the common answer — sharing the piece on your own social channels — as something you could do yourself. The real questions are whether anything drives traffic in the months before a page ranks, and whether they build links to speed ranking up. Their own model is two-pronged: paid ads for short-term traffic and conversions while the page is young, and manual link building for long-term organic growth. They say they fund both from their own budget with no extra client spend. Whatever the answer is, it decides how much promotion work falls back on you.

> "we use a two-pronged promotion strategy"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run paid ads plus manual link building from their own budget as part of the retainer.

**How to do it**

1. Ask whether promotion is included in the retainer or billed separately.
2. Ask what specifically happens in the first 90 days, before anything ranks.
3. Reject 'we'll share it on your LinkedIn' as a promotion answer.
4. Ask whether they build links manually and how many per month per article.
5. Ask whether paid ad spend comes from their budget or yours.
6. Write the uncovered promotion work into your own team's plan before signing.

**Pitfall:** Content that ranks in six months has no distribution for six months; buyers who never ask discover the gap after the invoices start.

**Apply at Pabau:** For a new pabau.com template or blog page, David should plan a short-term traffic source — paid social or a newsletter placement — for the months before it ranks, rather than waiting on organic alone.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask exactly what a content vendor does for promotion, whether it is included, and what drives traffic during the months before a page ranks. Social sharing on your own channels is not promotion.

### 2. Make the interview prove specificity your reader cannot find on Google  `124.3`
*useful · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert frame the interview as the only source of information a reader cannot get elsewhere. Without one, the writer is left with Google, YouTube, AI tools and the client website, and they note that what others say is not always valuable to your reader. Their worked example is a fictional accounting service for startups writing for best accounting software for startups. Even a writer who has covered accounting software before still needs to know what startups need most from accounting services, how they manage accounting before shopping for software, and how this specific client solves those problems. They add two secondary benefits: the writer learns from an expert instead of sifting mirage content, and fewer edits come back because the writer already knows which points matter most.

> "particularly the features they value most"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list topics their writers covered through this process, including route optimization software, hyperhidrosis, digital asset management, concussions, the US healthcare billing system and time clock software.

**How to do it**

1. Before accepting an assignment, tell the client the interview is a condition of doing the topic properly.
2. Prepare three questions in this shape: what does the buyer need most, how do they cope today without a product, and how does this client solve it specifically.
3. Ask which product features the target customer values most, and rank them.
4. Push past topic-level knowledge you already have and ask only about what is specific to this client and its customers.
5. Record the call so you can quote exact phrasing rather than paraphrasing from memory.
6. Ask the expert to explain any technical term in context, so you are not guessing nuance from search results.
7. Ask which points matter most, and weight the draft accordingly to cut review rounds.
8. Use the client website and search results only to check facts, never as the substance of the piece.

**Pitfall:** Skipping the interview because you have written about the category before. The draft reads competently and still fails to say how this particular product solves the problem, which is the only part that converts.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should make the interview non-negotiable for Pabau articles that position Pabau against alternatives. Prior knowledge of practice management software is not a substitute for knowing which features aesthetic clinics actually value most.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat the expert interview as the only source of detail a reader cannot get from search. Ask what the buyer needs most, how they cope today, and how this specific product solves it, and record the call for exact phrasing.

### 3. Match each launch idea type to the promotion channel it can carry  `123.13`
*useful · best practices · source 123*

Across the three case studies Hyam pairs a specific asset type with a specific distribution route, and the pairs are not interchangeable. The mega project of commissioned design work went to journalists who covered the news event, because it was news and had visuals. The opinion piece plus public challenge went to communities, because a point of view invites discussion and a challenge invites people to follow along. The influencer guest series went out through the influencer's own Twitter and email, because the distribution was the point of the deal. He lists the general options as pitching publications, enlisting an influencer, or posting where your target audience gathers. The choice of asset therefore decides the promotion plan, so pick both together.

> "pitch to publications, enlist the help of an influencer in your space"

**Evidence:** Hyam's three launches each used a different pairing: design mega project to journalists, opinion piece plus challenge to marketer communities, influencer series to the influencer's own Twitter and email list.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the three routes: press pitching, influencer distribution, community posting.
2. Ask which route your idea can actually carry: press needs news and visuals, community needs a point of view or a story to follow, influencer needs a deal you can pay for in access.
3. Discard any idea whose only available route is your own social feed.
4. Choose the asset and the route in the same decision, before production starts.
5. For press, tie the asset to a current news event so it has a reason to be covered now.
6. For community, build in something readers can follow over time, such as monthly updates.
7. For influencer, define the deliverable and the sharing commitment in writing.
8. After launch, note which route produced links, which produced subscribers, and reuse accordingly.

**Pitfall:** Producing an asset first and then hunting for a channel. A well-made piece with no natural route, such as an opinion post with no news hook and no community to post it in, dies with no audience.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content calendar should carry a promotion route in each row. A regulation-change explainer goes to aesthetics press, a practitioner data piece goes to practitioner communities, and a partner-authored piece goes out through the partner.

**Apply anywhere:** Pick the asset and its promotion route in the same decision. Press pitching needs news and visuals, community posting needs a point of view or an ongoing story, and influencer distribution needs a deal you can pay for in access; drop any idea whose only route is your own social feed.

### 4. Match writer hiring to the exact task, not to a background  `97.8`
*useful · best practices · source 97*

Grow and Convert reject 'a background in' as a hiring standard. They say it is not enough for someone to have a background in marketing, sales, software development, art or writing. You need a writer with actual experience doing the task you are having them write about. They spell it out by vertical: an SEO blog needs writers who have done SEO for themselves or clients; a sales blog needs writers who have held a role as an SDR, BDR, account executive or sales leader, not someone who sold lemonade at five; a software development blog needs designers, front-end engineers and back-end engineers who have built software. Without that, you get high school essay intros, high-level platitudes, and readers who sniff out the BS. They also note the best content comes from the in-house team, whose limit is time rather than knowledge.

> "hire a writer with actual experience doing the task"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert give per-vertical hiring standards and name the failure modes: essay intros, platitudes, lost reader trust.

**How to do it**

1. Write the hiring requirement as a task, such as 'has run technical SEO migrations', not as a field such as 'SEO background'.
2. Name the acceptable past job titles explicitly in the listing, the way they list SDR, BDR, AE and sales leader for a sales blog.
3. Ask candidates for one story of doing the task, with the specific outcome and what went wrong, before reading any writing samples.
4. Reject candidates whose samples contain no named companies, numbers or screenshots, regardless of prose quality.
5. Check your in-house team first for anyone with the experience and enough time to write, since that is the stronger source.
6. Where nobody in-house has time, switch to the interview model rather than lowering the experience bar for the writer.

**Pitfall:** Hiring on writing quality alone gets you polished platitudes. The tell is a draft with a high-school-essay intro and advice that restates the goal instead of the method.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau clinical and compliance articles, David should require writers who have worked in a practice or handled the actual workflow, and route everything else through interviews with staff who have.

**Apply anywhere:** Hire writers who have done the exact task you want written about, listing acceptable past job titles in the ad, and check your in-house team before going outside.

### 5. Mine five internal sources for content ideas that convert  `111.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 111*

Grow and Convert argue the biggest determinant of whether a blog post converts is not the call to action inside it but the idea behind the post. Their ideation sources are internal and customer-facing rather than tool-driven: specific questions asked of the sales team, the customer service team, and the CEO and founders; feedback elicited from existing customers over email; ideas found in online communities where the audience gathers; and the account's Adwords data. The Adwords source is the sharpest of the five, because it shows terms the company already pays for and knows convert. Their related framing, Customer-Content Fit, targets the same failure: the topics B2B marketers choose to write about do not match the topics that appeal to an advanced B2B buyer persona.

> "how to find ideas through online communities and your Adwords account"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's ideation article covers questions for sales, customer service and founders, customer email outreach, online communities and Adwords data.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the sales team which question comes up most on first calls, and write it verbatim.
2. Ask customer service for the top five support tickets by volume from the last quarter.
3. Ask the founders what buyers misunderstand about the category.
4. Email 20 existing customers asking what they were trying to fix when they found you.
5. Export the Adwords search terms report and pull the converting queries you do not have organic pages for.
6. Read the subreddits and forums your buyers use and log the questions asked more than once.
7. Merge all five lists and dedupe, keeping the wording the source used.
8. Rank by how directly the idea names a problem your product fixes, then brief the top items.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Running only the tool-based half. Keyword tools return market vocabulary, so the ideas that convert best, the ones from sales calls and support tickets, never enter the calendar.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's converting Google Ads queries should be exported quarterly and checked against existing /blog/ and /templates/ pages. Any converting query without an organic page becomes the next brief.

**Apply anywhere:** Build the topic list from five internal sources: sales calls, support tickets, founder interviews, customer emails and your paid search terms report. Any paid query that converts without an organic page is your next article.

### 6. Name a competitor's exact plan limit instead of a vague comparison  `71.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 71*

The moment Devesh reacted to most strongly in the whole draft was a specific competitive number. He had just flagged a paragraph about uploading sources as too much detail, then read the next sentence, which compared his tool's source limit to Jasper's Starter plan capping you at five knowledge assets. He reversed his cut immediately, saying he liked it and that it tears them down. The named plan and the exact figure do work that a general claim about flexibility cannot. It also shows why the detail survived: a general statement about generous limits is unverifiable and forgettable, while a specific competitor plan limit is checkable and lands as a comparison the reader can act on.

> "Compare that to Jasper Starter caps you at five knowledge assets"

**Evidence:** Devesh had marked a paragraph for cutting as too detailed, then kept it once he read the following sentence naming Jasper's Starter plan cap of five knowledge assets.

**How to do it**

1. For each capability you claim as an advantage, find the equivalent constraint on a named competitor's published plan.
2. Take the figure from the competitor's own pricing or documentation page, never from memory or a third-party summary.
3. Write the comparison with the plan name and the exact number in the same sentence.
4. Place it immediately after the sentence describing your own capability, so the contrast is adjacent.
5. Record the date you checked, and recheck before any refresh, since plan limits change.
6. Do not soften the comparison into a generic claim about being more flexible.

**Tools:** Jasper

**Pitfall:** Competitor plan limits change without notice, so an uncited or unchecked figure ages into an inaccuracy. Only publish numbers pulled from the competitor's own current pricing page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau comparison and listicle pages should carry exact competitor plan limits taken from the competitor's own website, in line with the existing pricing-table rule, rather than vague statements about Pabau including more.

**Apply anywhere:** Back every capability advantage with a named competitor plan and its exact published limit, taken from that competitor's own pricing page and rechecked on refresh.

### 7. Neutralize the in-house product-knowledge advantage with per-article interviews  `180.11`
*useful · best practices · source 180*

Grow and Convert say the single biggest reason companies choose an in-house content hire over an agency is product knowledge. The assumption is that an employee will understand the product and company deeply, and so be trusted to write about it, whereas agencies assign articles to writers with no domain knowledge and no process for acquiring it. Their argument is that this is the only real advantage, and a per-piece interview process removes it. Once it is removed, the remaining comparison favors the agency on speed to start, breadth of skills and access to a strategist who has actually built signup-driven programs. The practical version for any buyer is to stop treating in-house versus agency as a values question and treat it as one testable question: does the outside team have a repeatable way to get expertise out of your people and onto the page?

> "more deeply understand the details of their product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say most agencies assign articles to writers without domain knowledge or any process to acquire and express expertise, which is why companies lean in-house.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the one advantage you believe an in-house hire gives you; it is almost always product knowledge.
2. Ask each agency candidate to show the specific mechanism that supplies that knowledge per article.
3. Require a recorded expert interview per piece, with the transcript delivered alongside the draft.
4. Name the internal experts who will be interviewed and confirm they have the hours available.
5. If the agency has no such mechanism, the in-house argument holds and you should hire.
6. If it does, compare on the other axes: time to first published article, the range of skills covered, and proven signup-driven strategy experience.
7. Set a review at month six on whether the interviewed detail is actually reaching the published articles.

**Pitfall:** Assuming the interview process exists because the agency says writers 'talk to your team'. Ask for the transcript alongside the draft; if none exists, the interview is a kickoff call being recycled.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's advantage is internal clinical and product knowledge. David should make the interview transcript a required deliverable for any outsourced article, so the knowledge transfer is auditable rather than assumed.

**Apply anywhere:** The main reason to hire in-house is product knowledge. Test whether an outside team can supply it with a recorded expert interview per article and a transcript delivered with the draft. If they can, judge them on speed and skill breadth instead.

### 8. Never brief a writer from the client's own marketing site copy  `92.15`
*useful · best practices · source 92*

Grow and Convert make a warning that reverses the default research method. Many of their clients have explicitly told them not to use the copy on their own website, because it is bad, outdated, poorly written or improperly positioned. The reason is structural rather than a one-off: marketing site copy is hard to change because it requires agreement from executives and a bunch of key stakeholders, so it sits with poor or outdated copy for months or years. That means the source most writers treat as authoritative is systematically the stalest description of the product. The same applies to third-party listings such as a plugin directory entry, which is just vendor copy at one more remove. The reliable sources are interviews with customer-facing employees and the product's own technical documentation, which changes when the product changes.

> "many of our clients have actually told us not to use the copy on their website"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report this across many clients, and attribute the staleness to marketing copy needing executive and stakeholder sign-off, leaving it unchanged for months or years.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the client or product owner directly whether their site copy is current before using it as a source.
2. Ask when the homepage and product pages were last rewritten, and treat anything over a year old as unverified.
3. Source feature facts from help docs, changelogs and release notes instead, since those update with the product.
4. Source positioning and emphasis from sales and support interviews, not from the site.
5. Use the marketing site only to check naming and terminology consistency.
6. Flag in the brief any claim taken from the site so the fact-checker verifies it against docs.
7. Feed back to the client any site copy your research shows is wrong; it is useful to them.

**Pitfall:** A writer briefed with a homepage link produces copy that repeats a positioning the company itself has abandoned. Nobody catches it because the source looks authoritative.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau writers covering competitors should treat competitor homepages as unverified and prefer help centers and changelogs. The same rule applies to Pabau's own pages when writing about Pabau features.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat any company's marketing site as an unverified source, including your own. Prefer help centers, changelogs and release notes, which update when the product does.

### 9. Pair each marketer with a named sales buddy on a daily cadence  `177.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 177*

Alongside the group session, the author prescribes a one-to-one structure: pair each marketer with a buddy on the sales team and have them talk daily or weekly. This is deliberately smaller than a meeting. A named counterpart means questions get asked in the moment, without a marketer having to book time with a whole department to check one phrase. The underlying claim in the piece is that the biggest obstacle to the two teams working together is a lack of understanding of each other's role, and that understanding accumulates in small conversations rather than in scheduled reviews. For content teams the practical value is that a buddy is someone you can send a draft to and ask whether a prospect would actually say this.

> "Pair marketers with a buddy on the sales team"

**How to do it**

1. Assign every marketer one named sales counterpart in writing, not an open invitation to the team.
2. Set the cadence explicitly at daily or weekly, and put it in both calendars.
3. Give the pair a standing purpose: the marketer asks about calls, the rep asks about campaigns.
4. Have the marketer send drafts of bottom-funnel copy to the buddy before publishing.
5. Rotate pairings every two quarters so knowledge does not stay in one relationship.
6. Escalate anything the pair cannot resolve to the monthly joint session, rather than letting it sit.

**Pitfall:** An unnamed pairing collapses within weeks because neither person owns the conversation. If nobody can say who their buddy is, the program has already stopped.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should pair whoever writes Pabau's bottom-funnel and comparison pages with one named sales rep, and route draft claims about competitors past that rep before publishing.

**Apply anywhere:** Pair every marketer with one named counterpart in sales and set a daily or weekly cadence in writing. Use the buddy as the reviewer for bottom-funnel copy before it publishes.

### 10. Paste the AI draft into Google Docs instead of using markdown export  `71.16`
*useful · ai workflows · source 71*

Devesh's tool offers a copy-as-markdown option and he says he does not even use it. He copies the rendered draft and pastes it straight into a Google Doc, because Docs automatically picks up the right headings. He then edits in the Doc rather than in the tool's own editor, partly so viewers could see the edits, and transfers the finished piece to the CMS at the end. Two things travel with the choice. Editing in a document with suggestion history makes the changes visible and reviewable, which is what an editorial pass needs. And the tool's inline links survived the paste, which he checked and was pleased about, since a link that breaks in transfer is the usual cost of moving a draft between systems.

> "Google Docs will automatically kind of pick the right headings"

**Evidence:** Devesh skipped his tool's copy-markdown option, pasted into Google Docs, and confirmed mid-edit that the generated inline link to the product homepage had carried over correctly.

**How to do it**

1. Copy the rendered draft from the AI tool rather than exporting markdown.
2. Paste into a fresh Google Doc and confirm the heading levels came through correctly.
3. Check that inline links survived the paste before you start editing.
4. Do the whole editorial pass in the Doc so the changes are visible and reviewable by someone else.
5. Transfer the finished piece into the CMS as the last step, after editing is complete.
6. Re-verify every link once more after the CMS transfer, since that is the second place links break.

**Tools:** Google Docs, Wave Writer

**Pitfall:** Links and heading levels are what break when a draft moves between systems. Devesh explicitly checked that the tool's inline link had survived, and it is worth checking again after the CMS import.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau drafts that pass through an intermediate document should have their links and heading levels verified twice, once after the paste and once after the WordPress import, since the block contract depends on correct heading levels.

**Apply anywhere:** Paste AI drafts into a document editor rather than exporting markdown, verify headings and links survived, edit there, and re-verify links after the CMS import.

### 11. Pay a referral bonus to your team for writer referrals  `164.9`
*useful · concrete actions · source 164*

Grow and Convert say referrals from existing team members have arguably been their number one source of good writers. The reasoning is simple: lots of people are writers, so lots of people know writers, and a referral arrives with an implicit vouch from someone who already understands your standard. They pay a referral bonus, but tie it to outcome rather than to the introduction: the bonus is paid if the referred candidate works out as a long-term writer. That structure matters, because a bonus paid on application encourages volume while a bonus paid on retention encourages judgement. They recommend other companies do the same. This sits alongside their other channels, marketplaces, job boards, social media, all of which they describe as more hit and miss.

> "We offer a referral bonus to our team"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name team referrals as arguably their number one source of good writers over six years.

**How to do it**

1. Announce a written referral bonus for writer introductions to the whole team.
2. Pay the bonus only once the referred writer has worked out as a long-term contributor, not on hire.
3. Give referrers the same standards document you use to score applications, so they self-filter.
4. Route referrals through the same three filters as everyone else, with no exemptions.
5. Re-announce the bonus at the start of each hiring round rather than assuming people remember.
6. Compare referral retention against board and marketplace hires before spending more on either.

**Pitfall:** Paying the bonus on introduction or on hire. That rewards volume, and you end up screening a pile of loosely-vouched candidates while the referrer carries no downside.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's marketing and clinical-content contacts almost certainly know writers with aesthetics or healthcare experience. A retention-linked referral bonus is the cheapest way to open that channel.

**Apply anywhere:** Offer your team a referral bonus for writers, paid only when the referred writer works out long term. Referrals arrive pre-vouched and often outperform every paid sourcing channel.

### 12. Pay a referral bonus, since referrals produce the best writers  `167.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 167*

Grow and Convert say referrals of new writers from existing team members have arguably been the number one source of good writers for them. The reasoning is simple: lots of people are writers, so lots of people know writers. They pay a referral bonus, and they pay it on outcome, tying it to a candidate who works out as a long-term writer rather than to anyone who applies. They recommend other companies do the same. This sits alongside their assessment of the other channels. Marketplaces like Upwork, Fiverr and Crowd Content may contain a few good writers but have no strict filter, so you must apply your own. General job boards like Remote OK, Craigslist and Wellfound are hit and miss, with dedicated writing boards such as ProBlogger doing better. Social media works only in proportion to how many writers are in your network.

> "the #1 source of good writers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert call team referrals arguably their number one source of good writers and pay a bonus contingent on the referral working out long term.

**How to do it**

1. Announce a referral bonus to your existing writers and internal team with a specific figure.
2. Pay it only when the referred writer has completed a stated period or number of published pieces, not on hire.
3. Ask each existing writer directly for names rather than waiting for referrals to arrive.
4. Run ProBlogger or an equivalent dedicated writing board before general boards like Remote OK or Wellfound.
5. Treat Upwork, Fiverr and Crowd Content as unfiltered pools and apply your full three-filter process to anyone from them.
6. Post on LinkedIn or X only if your network genuinely contains writers, since yield tracks network composition.
7. Log the source of every hire and retire the channels that never produce a writer who lasts.

**Tools:** Upwork, Fiverr, ProBlogger, Wellfound, LinkedIn

**Pitfall:** Paying a referral bonus at the point of hire. It rewards volume, and Grow and Convert are clear that the value is in writers who last long term, not in applicants.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should ask Pabau's existing content contributors and marketing team for writer names before posting anywhere, and attach a bonus paid after a set number of published Pabau articles.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask your existing writers and staff for referrals first and pay a bonus contingent on the new writer lasting. It reliably beats marketplaces and general job boards.

### 13. Pick promotion channels by durability, not by immediacy  `107.19`
*useful · concrete actions · source 107*

Grow and Convert treat publish-and-promote as a required step, essential for originality-led posts that will get no organic traffic and still useful for SEO posts, since SEO can take up to a year and promotion shortens that. They name three options: social media, paid search, and link building covering both external and internal links. Their assessment of social is that posts on Twitter, Facebook or LinkedIn drive traffic immediately after publication but the results are spikey, dying down quickly, and hard to sustain because algorithms change, it does not scale, and there is heavy competition for attention. That is why their own services focus on paid search and link building, which they describe as easily scalable and a good way to drive more immediate results. The practical reading is to use social for the launch spike but not to fund it as the main channel.

> "organic social traffic is spikey"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say SEO results can take up to a year, which is the window promotion is meant to bridge.

**How to do it**

1. Assign every new post a promotion plan before it publishes, not after.
2. Use social posts for the launch window only, and expect the traffic to decay within days.
3. Point paid search at the post's target high-intent keyword to sustain traffic while the ranking builds.
4. Add internal links from your existing pages that already rank to the new post as part of the promotion step.
5. Add the new post to the external link-building target list if it is a bottom-funnel asset.
6. Do not judge the post's performance in the first two weeks, since organic results can take up to a year.

**Pitfall:** Building the promotion plan on organic social produces a launch spike and nothing afterwards, and the effort has to be repeated for every post with no compounding.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's promotion step for a new bottom-funnel article should be internal links from already-ranking pages plus paid on the target term, with social treated as a launch-day extra rather than the plan.

**Apply anywhere:** Plan promotion before publishing. Use social for the launch spike only, and rely on paid search and internal and external links for the sustained effect while the ranking builds.

### 14. Plan to coach every writer rather than hoping for a perfect fit  `167.15`
*useful · best practices · source 167*

Grow and Convert reject the belief, which they say circulates online, that you can find writers who happen to write exactly the way you want from day one. It is possible but extremely rare. The likelier outcome is a talented writer who becomes excellent after coaching. They name three specific things they coach toward: clarity of thought in structuring a piece and reasoning through arguments, a simple writing style without fluff, and compelling sales copywriting that is direct but not over the top. An experienced content strategist works with the writer at every stage, reviewing the questionnaire, giving guidance and providing feedback. All their strategists were previously Grow and Convert writers, so they know the standard. Coaching is heaviest early and tapers as writers gain experience, and almost every writer on their team has been heavily coached.

> "An experienced content strategist works closely with the writer at every stage"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say almost every writer on their team has been heavily coached, and staff their content strategist roles exclusively from former Grow and Convert writers.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the two or three qualities you coach toward, for example clarity of argument, no fluff, and direct sales copy.
2. Assign each new writer a named coach who has actually written for you before.
3. Have that coach review inputs before drafting, not only the finished draft.
4. Front-load coaching in the first several pieces and taper deliberately as the writer stabilizes.
5. Budget the coach's hours into the cost per article, since they are part of the real price.
6. Promote your strongest writers into the coaching role rather than hiring editors from outside.
7. Judge a candidate on whether they improve under coaching, not on whether they arrive matching your voice.

**Pitfall:** Searching for a plug-and-play writer who already matches your voice. That search runs indefinitely while the calendar stays empty, and it also causes you to reject coachable writers who would have been excellent within a few pieces.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should expect to coach an outside writer through several articles on voice rules and product framing before output is publishable, and budget that editor time rather than treating it as a hiring failure.

**Apply anywhere:** Assume you will coach every writer. Name the two or three qualities you coach toward, assign an experienced coach, and taper the support rather than looking for a perfect fit.

### 15. Prioritize CRO and refresh effort only on pages already ranking  `13.11`
*useful · best practices · source 13*

Rather than applying every CRO tactic to every page on the site, Irwin and Edward agree the efficient approach is to let content launch and accumulate organic traction first, identify which pillar and child pages are already ranking well via analytics, and concentrate conversion investment and content refreshes only on those proven performers, while considering removing pages that never gain traction. Freshness itself is treated as a ranking and conversion factor: content should be dated and explicitly updated, such as changing 'latest results for 2025' copy once the calendar turns to 2026, rather than left static, since stale-looking content sends visitors back to search results.

> "Invest in making the ones that are already ranking well convert more"

**How to do it**

1. Pull organic traffic and ranking data for all pillar and child pages over a rolling recent period (inferred: last three to six months) from GSC or Analytics.
2. Rank pages by traffic and ranking performance and identify the top tier that is already ranking well.
3. Apply CRO enhancements such as the 1-2-3 CTA rule, tools, TL;DRs, trust signals, and video to this top tier first rather than site-wide.
4. For pages showing no traction after a reasonable period (inferred: six to twelve months post-publish with no meaningful impressions or clicks), evaluate for consolidation, rewrite, or removal rather than continued investment.
5. Set a recurring content-freshness check (inferred: quarterly) to update any year-specific or time-sensitive language on the top-tier pages.
6. Re-pull the same traffic and ranking report after each refresh cycle to confirm the refreshed pages maintain or improve position before moving to the next batch.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Spreading CRO effort evenly across the whole site instead of concentrating it on proven-performing pages, and leaving date-specific claims like 'latest for 2025' unrefreshed, which signals staleness to users and search engines alike.

### 16. Publish at high volume to find winners, then remix only the winners  `68.7`
*useful · best practices · source 68*

Cody's volume numbers are extreme and he gives them plainly: he schedules about 70 tweets a week and around five LinkedIn posts a day, and told a friend he puts out roughly 150 unique pieces of content a week across accounts he owns. The reasoning is not that volume ranks; it is that volume creates surface area to discover what people are interested in. He then sorts by what performed, looks for the common ideas between the winners, and makes more content about those ideas, which he explicitly compares to how you optimize paid ads. He is careful about the ramp: three years earlier he could not have produced that volume, and it is something you build up into rather than start at. He frames content as a lifestyle choice, like eating well or training, and says the real question is whether you can keep it going for 24 months.

> "I schedule out 70 tweets a week"

**Evidence:** 70 scheduled tweets a week, five LinkedIn posts a day, roughly 150 unique pieces of content a week across owned accounts.

**How to do it**

1. Set a weekly output target you can actually sustain, then raise it gradually rather than starting at your ceiling.
2. Publish across the platforms your buyers use, from a single source of raw material such as an episode transcript.
3. At the end of each week, sort posts by engagement rather than reading them chronologically.
4. Identify the common idea, angle or format between the top performers, not just the top posts themselves.
5. Write more content on those winning ideas and drop the themes that consistently flop.
6. Repeat the sort-and-remix cycle weekly so the content mix shifts toward what the audience wants.
7. Judge the system on whether you can still run it in 24 months, not on any single week's numbers.

**Tools:** LinkedIn, Twitter

**Pitfall:** Starting at 150 pieces a week burns you out in a month. Cody says he could not have done this three years earlier and treats the capacity as something built up over time.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat social output as a discovery instrument for what practice owners care about, and feed the winning themes back into the blog editorial calendar rather than planning topics purely from keyword tools.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish more than feels comfortable, then sort by performance and double down only on the ideas that won. Volume is how you find the signal; the remix is how you use it.

### 17. Publish case studies as GEO assets, one per topic branch  `78.7`
*useful · best practices · source 78*

Grow and Convert make case studies an explicit step in the topic map build, not an afterthought. Source 12 already covers building a case-study library off the topic map; what is new here is placing case-study production as a standing step in the same pipeline as the interviews and keyword matching, and the reason given, which is pattern matching between story and user situation.

> "LLMs are smart enough to connect the dots"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list case study production as step five of their client GEO process and say it is their experience and belief that case studies help.

**How to do it**

### 18. Publish real case studies on real companies rather than beginner explainers  `183.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 183*

Grow and Convert exists because of one piece of advice. Benji Hyam wanted to write a book about driving high-value deals from content. > "publishing real case studies of doing this on real companies"

**Evidence:** The case-study-first premise built an audience from a standing start in 2015 and produced both the training business and the agency that followed.

**How to do it**

2. Pick real clients or your own business as the subject and get permission to name them.
3. Publish the method as it ran, including the numbers, the timeframe and the tests that failed.
4. Write for the practitioner who could hire you, not the beginner searching for definitions.

### 19. Publish the influencer's first post on relaunch day itself  `123.10`
*useful · concrete actions · source 123*

Hyam coincided the launch of the new Executive Street brand with the publishing of Chris Brogan's first post. That is a scheduling decision that does real work. The remaining posts in the three-post series then ran across the following week, which gave the new brand awareness with an audience Vistage did not previously have. > "We coincided the launch of our new brand with the publishing of his first post"

**Evidence:** Publishing Brogan's first post on relaunch day drew over 3,000 visitors on day one; his remaining posts across the week reached an audience Vistage did not previously have.

**How to do it**

### 20. Publish the spreadsheet template, then argue why a template is not enough  `93.8`

> "generated more than 40 free trial signups (via organic visitors) across 9 months"

### 21. Publish three articles a month, each aimed at a single keyword  `153.14`
*useful · best practices · source 153*

Grow and Convert state their production cadence plainly: for most clients they publish three articles a month, each targeting a single SEO keyword. That constraint is what makes their rankings-per-post measurement work, because one post maps to one keyword and any extra rankings are a bonus rather than a muddled attribution. They note the ratio can exceed 1.00 because some posts rank for multiple target keywords. The pairing matters. A modest, steady cadence over years is what produced 100-plus first-page rankings after three years, not bursts of volume. It also keeps enough capacity per piece to do the SERP analysis and originality work they say actually decides rankings.

> "we publish 3 articles per month, each targeting a single SEO keyword"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish three articles a month per client, one keyword each, and report 100+ first-page rankings after three years on that cadence.

**How to do it**

1. Set the cadence at three articles a month and hold it rather than batching.
2. Assign exactly one target keyword per article and record it against the URL.
3. Refuse briefs that list several primary keywords, since that breaks both the measurement and the intent match.
4. Budget the freed capacity into SERP analysis and the originality source for each piece.
5. Count any additional keywords a post ranks for as upside in the ratio, not as a second target.
6. Check for overlap before commissioning so two articles never chase the same keyword.
7. Review the twelve articles from a year ago each month to see which have crossed into the top 10.

**Pitfall:** Raising the cadence to hit a traffic target. The per-piece research and originality work is the first thing cut, and the rankings-per-post ratio falls even as the raw count rises.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should hold a steady cadence with one target keyword per /blog/ or /templates/ page, recorded in the same sheet used for the rankings-per-post ratio. David should treat any request to raise volume as a request to cut the SERP and originality work.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish at a steady modest cadence with exactly one target keyword per article, recorded against the URL. Extra keywords a post picks up are upside; multiple primary keywords per brief break both the intent match and your measurement.

### 22. Publish through the CMS API so the agent never needs a human  `77.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 77*

Schneider's fourth stage uploads the finished article to the CMS over its API, naming Strapi as the example. This is the step that decides whether the pipeline is an automation or a tool: as long as a person has to copy a draft into an editor, the throughput ceiling is that person's day. Doing it over the API is also what makes the 30-day refresh stage possible, because the agent needs to be able to read the existing article back and write an updated version to the same record. The practical requirement is that the CMS exposes read, create and update on posts, and that the agent stores the returned post ID so it can find the article again a month later.

> "uploads the article the the CMS like strapi via api"

**Evidence:** Schneider names Strapi and specifies the upload happens via API.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm the CMS exposes create, read and update on posts over its REST or GraphQL API.
2. Create a dedicated API credential for the agent with write access limited to the content collection.
3. Have the publish stage POST the article body, title, slug, meta description and any taxonomy in one call.
4. Set the status field to draft rather than published so a human approves before the URL goes live.
5. Capture the returned post ID and write it to the state file next to the keyword and publish date.
6. Have the refresh stage GET the article back by that ID rather than re-scraping your own live page.
7. Add an idempotency check on the slug so a retried run updates the existing record instead of creating a duplicate.

**Tools:** Strapi

**Pitfall:** Without an idempotency check on the slug, a retried or crashed run creates a second post for the same keyword, and you have built a cannibalization generator. The signal is two of your own URLs alternating in the SERP for one query.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's agent should write to the WordPress REST API with status set to draft, storing the post ID so the refresh cycle can PUT an update to the same article rather than creating a new one.

**Apply anywhere:** Give the agent API write access to your CMS so it can publish and later update the same record. Always create as a draft, and key on the slug so retries update instead of duplicating.
