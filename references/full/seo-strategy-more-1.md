### 1. A founder-run blog can acquire customers at a tenth of typical CAC  `152.12`
*useful · general insights · source 152*

The most striking point on Grow and Convert's salary curve is the zero end. When founders do the content themselves and are funding their own salary from outside the business, the model puts CAC around $20, against the $100 to $500 typical for self-serve B2C SaaS. They call that an unfair advantage and a home run, while warning plainly that building a blog with steady, consistently converting traffic is not easy. They connect it to a visible trend: serial entrepreneurs and online personalities maintain a personal blog and email list across ventures, so each new project launches to a built-in audience. Building that asset can take years, but it pays off across every business that follows, which is why the effort survives the failure of any single product.

> "A blog that gets your customers at $20 is an awesome unfair advantage"

**Evidence:** Model baseline: $0 monthly salary spend gives $20 CAC versus the $100-$500 typical range for B2C self-serve SaaS.

**Pitfall:** The $20 figure hides unpaid founder time. It is real only when the founder's salary genuinely comes from outside the business; otherwise the cost has been moved, not removed.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat founder and clinician-expert bylines on the blog as a cost advantage rather than a nice-to-have. Content written by in-house experts on time already committed carries a much lower marginal cost than commissioned pieces.

**Apply anywhere:** Content written by founders or in-house experts on time already committed carries a far lower marginal acquisition cost than commissioned pieces. Treat it as a cost advantage, not a nice-to-have.

### 2. Accept that the content winning AI leads was written years earlier  `83.14`
*useful · best practices · source 83*

Grow and Convert make a point about timeframe that changes how you justify GEO spend. They did not know their financial sector client would ask ChatGPT for an SEO agency, and they did not create content for her prompt. What earned the recommendation was ten years of writing about how most content marketing does an awful job of generating leads and how they fix it. The implication is that the return on this work is not attributable to a campaign and does not arrive on a quarterly cycle. Coverage compounds, and the payoff shows up as leads whose origin you cannot trace without asking. Teams that demand per-page attribution before funding use-case content will never fund the thing that works.

> "We've just been writing about how most content marketing does"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert attribute a ChatGPT-sourced client win to ten years of writing on a theme, not to any page created for that prompt.

**How to do it**

1. Set the review horizon for use-case and case-study content at twelve months, not one quarter.
2. Measure the programme on topic-level AI visibility, not on per-page conversions.
3. Add a how-did-you-hear-about-us question to every form so untraceable wins get recorded.
4. Keep publishing on the same core theme rather than rotating topics each quarter.
5. Log every AI-sourced lead against the topic it relates to, not the page it landed on.
6. Report the count of customer situations covered as the programme's leading indicator.
7. Refuse to cut the programme on attribution grounds without first checking the how-did-you-hear data.

**Pitfall:** Killing a content programme after two quarters because no single page attributes to the AI leads. The leads are real and the pages are working, but the mechanism is aggregate coverage, not one ranking page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should judge use-case and case-study pages on aggregate topic visibility and on how-did-you-hear-about-us answers, not on per-page conversions. Set the review horizon at a year, not a quarter.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge use-case and case-study pages on aggregate topic visibility and on how-did-you-hear-about-us answers rather than per-page conversions, and set the review horizon at a year rather than a quarter.

### 3. Adjust the five-step process by business type before reusing it  `102.18`
*useful · best practices · source 102*

Grow and Convert are clear that their five-step process is broadly applicable but that the details change by business type. In SaaS, there are specific high-intent keyword frameworks that reliably drive trial and demo signups. In B2B, the requirement is producing content that speaks credibly to advanced audiences, which is where their expert interview stage carries most of the weight. In B2C and ecommerce, the nuance sits in which product keywords you choose to target. They also flag that steps 3 and 5, content production and measurement, are the blog-specific parts of the process; steps 1, 2 and 4 apply to video, webinars, podcasts, templates, infographics and white papers largely unchanged. That split is the practical part: the customer workup, the ideation and the promotion plan transfer across formats.

> "steps also largely apply to video and other types of content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish separate SaaS, B2B and B2C guidance and say steps 3 and 5 of their process are blog-specific.

**How to do it**

1. Identify which of the three profiles you are: SaaS, B2B services, or B2C and ecommerce.
2. For SaaS, build the keyword list around trial and demo intent frameworks rather than category education.
3. For B2B, budget more time for the expert interview, since the audience detects surface-level content fast.
4. For B2C and ecommerce, spend the effort on product keyword selection rather than on the production workflow.
5. When applying the process to video or templates, reuse steps 1, 2 and 4 unchanged and rebuild only production and measurement.
6. Write down the adjusted version as your own process document rather than reusing a generic five-step template.

**Pitfall:** Copying a generic content process without adapting the keyword frameworks to the business model. B2B audiences in particular reject content written from competitor research alone.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sits in the SaaS profile serving B2B practice owners, so the keyword list should be built around demo intent and the expert interview should be treated as mandatory rather than optional.

**Apply anywhere:** Adapt a general content process to your business model before adopting it: SaaS needs demo-intent keyword frameworks, B2B needs deeper expert input, and ecommerce needs product keyword selection. Steps outside production and measurement transfer to other formats unchanged.

### 4. Aim organic content at the paid terms you want to stop buying  `114.12`
*useful · best practices · source 114*

Grow and Convert set an explicit long-term objective for the content program: over time, have organic blog posts ranking for the terms you currently pay for, so you become slowly less reliant on ad spend and get those leads for free. That reframes the relationship between paid and organic. Paid search is not just a channel, it is the discovery mechanism and the proof of intent, and organic content is the mechanism for retiring the cost. They call the technique powerful specifically because the conversion evidence exists before you write anything. The discipline it implies is a shared list between the paid and content teams, reviewed on a schedule, where every high-spend converting term has a named organic page against it and a rank being tracked. Bids come down as the pages take hold rather than all at once.

> "organic blog posts that rank for the terms you're paying for"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state the goal directly: rank organically for the terms you pay for so you become less reliant on ad spend and obtain those leads for free.

**How to do it**

1. Export the paid terms with the highest converting spend from the last twelve months.
2. Assign a named organic page to each term and record the owner and publish date on a shared sheet.
3. Publish the page and track its position for that exact term weekly.
4. Wait until the page holds a stable top-three position before touching the bid.
5. Reduce the bid on that term in stages and watch total conversions for the term, paid plus organic, not paid alone.
6. Stop reducing if combined conversions fall, since organic click share may not cover the paid volume.
7. Reinvest the freed budget into testing new terms that have no organic coverage yet.
8. Review the sheet quarterly with both the paid and content owners in the room.

**Tools:** Google Ads, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Cutting the bid the moment the page ranks costs you conversions, because organic click share on a commercial SERP is smaller than the paid position was delivering. Watch combined conversions, not the ranking alone.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep one shared sheet mapping every converting Google Ads term to a planned or live organic page, with rank tracked. Terms around booking software and patient management are the ones worth retiring first.

**Apply anywhere:** Maintain a shared sheet mapping every high-converting paid term to a named organic page, track that page's rank, and only step bids down once it holds a top-three position. Judge the change on paid plus organic conversions combined.

### 5. Answer how long content takes by fixing the two metrics first  `141.11`
*useful · best practices · source 141*

Grow and Convert say the most common question on their sales calls is how long content marketing takes to work, and that the standard answers of three months, six months or a year are worthless. Their position is that the answer depends on what you measure success by, the business model, and whether the company has product-market fit, with further variability from domain authority, competitiveness of the niche, niche-specific promotion channels and the fame of the founders. So they refuse to answer until success is defined. Their own definition is two metrics in strict order: leads or signups first, traffic second. Leads come first because content that does not convert is not worth funding. Traffic comes second because conversion measurement is imperfect and only ever gives a lower limit, so traffic completes the picture. Everything else on their list, ranking, click-throughs, time on site, shares and comments, they call vanity metrics.

> "most of them are vanity metrics"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this as one of the top questions on sales calls and refuse to give a figure, naming domain authority, niche competitiveness, promotion channels and founder fame as the variables that make a single number meaningless.

**How to do it**

1. Before quoting any timeline, write down the single conversion action the business gets paid for.
2. Rank success metrics as leads or signups first, traffic second, and state that ordering to the stakeholder in writing.
3. Explicitly name the metrics you will not report on: rankings alone, click-throughs to other blog pages, time on site, shares, comments.
4. Record the four variables that move the timeline: domain authority, niche competitiveness, available promotion channels, and founder or executive reach.
5. Give a range tied to those variables and to the funnel split, not a single number.
6. Frame traffic as the completing signal for conversions you cannot attribute, never as the goal itself.
7. Revisit the definition at the first quarterly review rather than defending the original estimate.

**Pitfall:** Quoting a generic three-to-six-month timeline sets an expectation against a metric nobody has agreed on, and the engagement then gets judged on rankings or shares instead of leads.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David is asked how long a Pabau content push takes to show results, define the metric first: demo requests attributed to content, then organic sessions to those pages. Give the range against Pabau's actual authority and the competitiveness of practice-management terms rather than a stock number.

**Apply anywhere:** Refuse to quote a timeline until success is defined. Rank leads or signups first and traffic second, name the vanity metrics you will not report, and give a range that reflects your domain authority, niche competitiveness, promotion channels and founder reach.

### 6. Apply the funnel-order finding beyond SaaS to any business model  `132.13`
*useful · general insights · source 132*

Grow and Convert are explicit that Geekbot's status as a self-service B2B SaaS does not limit the finding. They state the learnings in the article apply to any type of business, naming B2C and ecommerce directly. What travels is the ordering rule and the ratio, not the absolute rates: a 4.78 percent trial conversion is a property of low-friction self-service software, while the 25x gap between buying-intent and informational search is a property of intent. The company detail worth carrying across models is the pain description. Geekbot's whole keyword strategy rests on one specific pain, that standup meetings run long and interrupt engineers' work, and both the bottom-funnel and top-funnel lists are derived from that single sentence. Any business can write that sentence, and the keyword lists fall out of it the same way.

> "but the learnings in this article apply to any type of business"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state the Geekbot learnings apply to B2C and ecommerce as well as B2B SaaS, and describe the pain as standup meetings being long and disruptive to employees' work time.

**Pitfall:** Porting the absolute conversion rates rather than the ratio sets impossible targets for high-consideration or high-ticket categories, where a 4.78 percent conversion on a first visit is not achievable.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sells to practices with a longer consideration cycle than a Slack app, so David should expect much lower absolute conversion rates and still expect the same multiple between buying-intent pages and guides. Start by writing Pabau's pain in one sentence and derive both keyword lists from it.

**Apply anywhere:** Carry the ratio between funnel stages across business models, not the absolute conversion rates, which depend on how much friction your purchase has. Write your customer's pain in one sentence first; both your buying-intent and your informational keyword lists should be derivable from it.

### 7. Apply the replaced-behavior test before claiming a new category  `110.7`
*useful · best practices · source 110*

Grow and Convert set a strict bar for the new-category claim. Inventing a new thing is not enough. You have to be inventing a new thing and not replacing an existing behavior or solution that people already search for online. You have to be creating genuinely new behavior. Their proof is that even the standard examples fail it. Airbnb, had it started with an SEO strategy, could have ranked for hotel-related terms. Uber could have ranked for taxi-related terms. Both replaced an existing behavior that already had search demand attached to it, so both had a bottom-funnel keyword set available from day one. The practical use of the test is to stop a founder's technology story from becoming a content strategy, because almost every product replaces something people already search for.

> "you can't just be inventing a new thing"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert argue Airbnb could have ranked for hotel terms and Uber for taxi terms, so neither would have needed a top-funnel-first strategy despite inventing new business models.

**How to do it**

1. Write one sentence describing what your customer did before your product existed.
2. If that sentence names a tool, service or manual process, search its name and check for search volume.
3. If volume exists, you are replacing a behavior, so you are not a new category and bottom-funnel terms are available.
4. List the terms people use for the replaced behavior and treat them as your first keyword bucket.
5. Only claim new category status if no query describes the prior behavior at all.
6. Re-run the test annually, because categories that start new stop being new within a year or two.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Confusing a differentiated product with a new category. The signal is that you can name what customers used before you and it has search volume, which means the bottom-funnel keywords were there all along.

**Apply at Pabau:** Practices ran on paper diaries, spreadsheets and generic booking tools before Pabau. Those replaced behaviors are the keyword set, so pabau.com should target terms around switching from paper records and spreadsheets rather than educating the market on what practice management software is.

**Apply anywhere:** Before claiming you are creating a new category, name what your customers did before your product existed. If that prior behavior has search volume, you are replacing something and bottom-funnel keywords already exist for you.

### 8. Apply the same buying-intent test to paid keywords as to organic  `158.15`
*useful · content insights · source 158*

Grow and Convert open by saying the two recurring failures they see in client Google Ads accounts over seven years are targeting keywords without buying intent and not being intentional enough about campaign setup. They explicitly connect the first to their organic work: they have covered buying intent extensively for organic search and say it is just as true for paid. This matters for anyone running both channels, because it means one keyword classification exercise serves both. A term that fails the SERP intent test for a paid bid is also a poor organic target for a page meant to convert, and a term that converts in paid is a proven organic target. The difference is only in how fast you find out, since paid charges you immediately while organic wastes months instead.

> "We've covered this extensively for organic search"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite seven years of client Google Ads accounts showing the same two recurring mistakes.

**How to do it**

1. Build one keyword sheet shared by paid and organic, with a buying-intent column.
2. Classify each term by reading the SERP, marking commercial or informational.
3. Use commercial terms as both paid keywords and bottom-of-funnel page targets.
4. Use informational terms as organic content targets only, never as bids.
5. Feed converting paid search terms back into the sheet as proven organic targets.
6. Feed organic pages that convert into the paid account as landing pages for the matching term.
7. Review the sheet quarterly and move terms between the two lists as SERPs shift.

**Pitfall:** Paid and organic teams keeping separate keyword lists means the same low-intent term gets funded twice, once in spend and once in writing time.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep one keyword sheet covering the blog, template pages and any paid search, with buying intent judged from the SERP. Converting paid terms become the next bottom-of-funnel article targets.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep one keyword sheet for paid and organic with a buying-intent column judged from the SERP. Commercial terms get bids and conversion pages, informational terms get content only, and converting paid terms become your next organic targets.

### 9. Argue the content budget on compounding cost per lead, not on volume  `102.16`
*useful · general insights · source 102*

Grow and Convert give three reasons content beats paid channels, and the middle one is the budget argument. SEO content compounds: as you stack pieces and acquire rankings, the monthly investment stays roughly the same while the body of pages producing traffic and leads keeps growing, so cost per result falls over time. Paid is fixed by comparison, since the traffic and leads you get for a monthly budget do not grow. The other two reasons are that you do not pay per click once a page ranks, so a ranking piece keeps producing free traffic and conversions, and that search traffic is far more targeted than display or paid social, where people passively ignore ads while searchers actively look for the topic. They caveat the whole argument: it only holds if the content actually ranks.

> "SEO content marketing has a compounding effect"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert give three reasons content beats paid: no cost per click once ranking, compounding returns on a flat monthly spend, and far higher intent than display or paid social.

**How to do it**

1. Track cost per lead from content by dividing the monthly content spend by conversions attributed to content.
2. Plot that number monthly rather than quarterly, so the downward curve is visible to whoever holds the budget.
3. Compare it against the same month's cost per lead from paid search, which will stay roughly flat.
4. Present the compounding case with the caveat attached: the curve only bends if the pieces rank.
5. Keep a count of pages ranking in the top 10 as the leading indicator behind the cost curve.
6. If the cost per lead is not falling after two quarters, the problem is ranking or topic choice, not budget.

**Pitfall:** Making the compounding argument while publishing content that never ranks. Then the cost per lead stays flat, the argument collapses, and the budget goes back to paid.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should report content cost per demo request as a monthly trend line, alongside the count of pages ranking in the top 10, rather than reporting article volume published.

**Apply anywhere:** Justify content budget with a falling cost per lead over time, backed by a rising count of top 10 rankings. Volume published is not the argument, and the compounding claim fails if the pages do not rank.

### 10. Ask to see pages ranking in the top three for their stated target keyword  `179.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 179*

Grow and Convert give three questions for testing whether an agency actually builds pages that rank, rather than sprinkling keywords across general content. Ask what their approach is to getting pages to rank for specific keywords. Ask them to show examples of pages they created that are ranking highly for their target keyword. And ask how they decide whether a keyword gets its own dedicated page or gets folded into an existing one. That third question is the discriminating one, because an agency with a real one-page-per-keyword philosophy has a rule for it, while an agency using the sprinkle method has no rule at all. They add why the bar is the top three specifically: that is where the majority of traffic sits, so page-one presence lower down is not the proof it appears to be.

> "ranking highly in Google Search for their target keyword"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert credit their one-page-per-keyword approach with positions one to three for clients, which they say is where the majority of traffic comes from.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the agency to name three pages they created and the single keyword each one targets.
2. Search each keyword yourself in an incognito window and record the actual position, rather than accepting a screenshot.
3. Discount anything below position three, since that is where Grow and Convert say the majority of traffic sits.
4. Check that the page title contains the stated target keyword; if it does not, the keyword was probably assigned after the fact.
5. Ask how they decide whether a keyword gets a dedicated page or is folded into an existing page, and require an actual rule.
6. Ask them to show a case where they chose to fold a keyword in, and why.
7. Check whether the example keywords carry buying intent or are broad advice terms picked for easy ranking.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What is your approach to getting pages to rank for specific keywords? Can you show us examples of pages you've created that are ranking highly for their target keyword? How do you decide whether a keyword gets its own dedicated page or gets folded into an existing page?
```

**Pitfall:** Verifying rankings from the agency's own screenshots or rank tracker export. Check the SERP yourself, and check that the keyword shown is one a buyer would actually search, not a long-tail term with no commercial intent.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should apply the same three questions to any vendor, and hold its own pages to the same bar: each template and comparison page names one target keyword and is judged on reaching the top three for it.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask an agency to name three pages they built and the single keyword each targets, then check the live SERP yourself and discount anything below position three. Confirm the target keyword appears in the page title. Then ask how they decide whether a keyword earns a dedicated page or gets folded into an existing one. A real methodology has a rule; keyword sprinkling has none.

### 11. Audit a competitor blog for top-of-funnel waste before pitching a strategy  `112.16`
*useful · concrete actions · source 112*

Grow and Convert open their worked example by auditing a rival's blog rather than their client's. Looking at a sports nutrition company's topic list, they pick out 'transitioning from plastic to steel packaging' and 'training for snow sports' as perfect examples of top-of-funnel content: plausibly interesting to the audience, but giving no indication the reader wants to buy a supplement. That audit is what makes the case for the alternative strategy concrete, because it shows a named competitor spending its budget on terms that convert around 25X worse. Used this way, a competitor blog audit is a sales and planning tool: it identifies both what the market is wasting effort on and, by omission, which purchase-intent terms nobody in the category is covering.

> "Two of these topics are perfect examples of TOF content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's audit of a sports nutrition competitor's blog surfaced packaging and snow-sports training topics as top-of-funnel, supporting their Truwild plan.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the last 30 to 50 blog post titles from two or three direct competitors.
2. Label each title as product, pain point, use case, or lifestyle, using only the title.
3. Count the lifestyle share; a majority means the category's purchase-intent terms are probably uncontested.
4. For each lifestyle title, write the buying question it fails to answer, and check whether anyone answers it.
5. Cross-check the uncovered questions against a keyword tool for demand.
6. Present the audit as the case for your plan, naming the competitor titles as the counterexample.
7. Repeat the audit annually, since a competitor switching to bottom-of-funnel content is an early warning.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Judging by title alone can mislabel a post that is actually product-led. Open any ambiguous one before counting it.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this on the two or three closest practice-management competitors before setting Pabau's quarterly calendar. Where their blogs are full of general aesthetics-industry pieces, the comparison and pain-point terms are open, and that is where Pabau's next set of pages should go.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit two or three competitors' recent post titles and label each as product, pain point, use case, or lifestyle. A lifestyle majority means the purchase-intent terms in your category are uncontested, and the audit doubles as the argument for going after them.

### 12. Benchmark a 16-month SaaS content program at 600 conversions a month  `131.10`
*useful · general insights · source 131*

The Smartlook engagement gives usable benchmarks for a bottom-funnel SaaS content program. Grow and Convert grew conversions from their content to more than 600 per month within 16 months, tracking free account sign-ups and demo requests. Organic traffic to their posts alone reached over 18,000 a month. Of 50 targeted keywords they took 11 number one rankings, 20 top three, 37 top 10, with an average position of 7. Their three keyword types, category, competitors and alternatives, and jobs to be done, accounted for every piece in the top 15 by conversion volume. The client started with limited US brand recognition against Hotjar and FullStory. These numbers are useful mainly as a shape: expect the ramp measured in quarters, not weeks, and expect roughly three quarters of targeted keywords to reach the top 10.

> "more than 600 per month in 16 months"

**Evidence:** Smartlook: 600+ monthly conversions in 16 months, 18,000+ monthly organic sessions to the program's posts, 37 of 50 keywords in the top 10, average position 7.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Quoting these figures as a forecast rather than a shape. Smartlook had product depth, a dozen internal experts and 50 keywords behind it; a program with none of those will not hit the same curve.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David sets expectations for Pabau's content program, use quarters rather than months, and set the target as share of targeted keywords reaching the top 10 rather than a traffic figure. A 70% top-10 hit rate on a well-chosen bottom-funnel list is a reasonable ceiling to aim at.

**Apply anywhere:** Set content-program expectations in quarters, and measure the program by the share of targeted keywords reaching the top 10 rather than by a raw traffic number. Roughly three quarters reaching the top 10 over 16 months is a strong outcome.

### 13. Benchmark page-one arrival at three months, not the usual six  `135.13`
*useful · general insights · source 135*

Grow and Convert give their own delivery benchmark from this engagement. They published 31 blog posts and 22 landed in positions 1 to 10, which is more than half of everything published. Most posts reached page one in under three months, which they note outperforms the six-month benchmark they normally see. The average position across all posts was 9. They attribute the speed to keyword choice rather than to link building or technical work: specific, low-competition bottom-funnel and mid-funnel terms in a space where competitors were not writing for the qualified variants. The numbers are useful as a planning expectation for a similar program, and as a warning sign if a comparably specific keyword set is still nowhere after six months.

> "22 of them landed in positions #1-10 on Google search"

**Evidence:** 31 posts published, 22 in positions 1 to 10, most reaching page one in under 3 months against a normal 6-month benchmark, average position 9, 120x organic traffic growth and 120+ conversions.

**Pitfall:** Setting a three-month expectation on head terms. This benchmark only holds for specific, qualified keywords in a SERP where competitors have not built tailored pages.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should set a three-month page-one expectation for narrow qualified terms and six months for anything closer to a head term, and review any qualified page still off page one at month four for intent or title mismatch.

**Apply anywhere:** Expect specific qualified keywords to reach page one within three months and treat six months as the benchmark for broader terms; a stalled qualified page after four months signals a targeting problem, not a patience problem.

### 14. Blend your brand into an exact-match domain instead of stacking keywords  `67.9`
*useful · best practices · source 67*

Dirk still uses exact-match domains, but says the naming approach has to change. He argues against bestonlinecasinos.com style construction: drop the generic modifier and put the brand you are actually building in its place, so an affiliate called Affiliate would register affiliateonlinecasino.com rather than the pure keyword string. His reasoning is that ranking is moving toward brand recognition and AI systems, so even an affiliate needs a brand around it. He is explicit that the domain is only one building block and that on-page, off-page and internal linking still decide the outcome. He also warns off the other end of the market: do not assume a domain at a genuine DR 50 with an aged strong link profile is your flagship asset, because he would not invest in that.

> "Instead, integrate your brand in it"

**Evidence:** Dirk says the 2012 update removed EMDs with crap content while sites with legitimate content kept ranking, and names the 2025 update as the next big cull.

**How to do it**

1. Choose the keyword you want the domain to carry, then delete the generic superlative from it, such as best or top.
2. Put your actual brand name in the slot the superlative occupied, keeping the category keyword.
3. Check the resulting name is pronounceable and usable as a real brand, not just a string.
4. Target a keyword with decent volume but not head-term competition, since Dirk says do not aim an EMD at a highly competitive keyword.
5. Register the country TLD version too where you want geo strength, since a .ca or .co.uk is a stronger geo signal than a .com.
6. Treat the domain as one building block: build the on-page, internal linking and link profile as if the domain gave you nothing.
7. Do not pay a premium for an aged DR 50 EMD on the assumption it is a flagship, which Dirk explicitly advises against.

**Pitfall:** Buying a pure keyword EMD and expecting it to carry the site. Dirk says the 2012 update killed exact-match domains with thin content, not exact-match domains as such, and that survivors are the ones with clean on-page and correct internal linking.

**Apply at Pabau:** For any Pabau microsite or campaign domain, build the name around the Pabau brand plus the category term rather than a pure keyword string, and register the ccTLD in the target market.

**Apply anywhere:** Build any keyword domain around your brand plus the category term rather than a pure keyword string, register the country TLD in your target market, and treat the domain as one building block rather than the strategy.

### 15. Budget five months to first-page rankings, then expect it to accelerate  `95.17`
*useful · content insights · source 95*

Matt Goolding gives Grow and Convert's own benchmark from studying client sites: the first pieces of content on a site usually take around 5 months to reach the first page of Google, and after that it picks up quickly because the site has built topical authority. Their mini-volume comparison pages beat that badly, hitting the top 5 within 30 days and sometimes within days. Their pages targeting keywords above 1,000 monthly searches took 3 to 5 months to reach the top 3, matching the benchmark. He observes the same pattern across multiple clients. The planning use is to set two separate expectations in the same content calendar rather than one blended timeline, and to judge a page against the benchmark for its own volume band.

> "usually around 5 months for the first pieces"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: around 5 months to page 1 for a site's first pieces, 3 to 5 months to top 3 for keywords above 1,000 volume, under 30 days to top 5 for mini-volume comparison pages.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Applying a single ranking timeline to the whole calendar makes mini-volume pages look like flukes and head-term pages look like failures at month two, so budget gets cut from the wrong work.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content reporting should set separate rank-timeline expectations by keyword band: weeks for competitor alternatives pages, months for broad practice management terms. Reviewing every new page at the same 90-day checkpoint hides both the fast wins and the slow ones still on track.

**Apply anywhere:** Set separate ranking expectations by keyword band. Low-volume comparison pages should be checked at 30 days, head terms above 1,000 volume at 3 to 5 months, and a new site's first pieces at around 5 months.

### 16. Budget one to six months before most channels show measurable results  `177.8`
*useful · general insights · source 177*

The author sets an explicit expectation for how long marketing takes to work, and splits it by channel type. Paid ads, PR depending on the case, and direct response can show more instantaneous results. Everything else he puts at between one and six months before measurable results appear. He ties this directly to the credibility problem. Marketing gets treated as a support function when an organization has never seen it drive business, which is most likely in early-stage companies, or when leadership attributes most revenue to sales effort. Naming the window in advance is what stops month two being read as failure.

> "Most other channels will take between 1-6 months"

**Evidence:** The author's split: paid ads, PR and direct response can be near-instant, while most other channels take one to six months to show measurable results.

**How to do it**

1. Classify each planned channel as fast-feedback (paid, direct response, some PR) or slow (content, SEO, organic social).
2. Put the one-to-six-month window in the plan in writing before the first spend, not after the first review.
3. Name the leading indicator you will report in months one to three, since revenue will not be available yet.
4. Budget explicit research time for the fast channels too, because targeting them well needs user understanding.
5. Schedule the first real judgement point at month six for slow channels.
6. Report against the stated window every month, including the months that look bad.

**Pitfall:** Early-stage companies that have never seen marketing produce revenue judge it on a sales team's timescale. If leadership asks for the number in month two, the window was never agreed in writing.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should state the ramp window when a new Pabau content cluster starts, with a leading indicator such as indexed pages ranking in the top twenty for months one to three, so pre-breakeven months are budgeted rather than defended.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the one-to-six-month ramp window into the plan before spending, name a leading indicator for the early months, and report against the stated window every month including the bad ones.

### 21. Cap founder pay and route all service margin into the new product  `170.9`
*useful · concrete actions · source 170*
