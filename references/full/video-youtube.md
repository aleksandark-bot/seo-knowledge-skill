### 1. 1,000 views a video is enough in B2B - the maths of the daily VSL  `57.13`
*core · content insights · source 57*

Jackie's YouTube numbers are counterintuitive and he gives them precisely. He gets about 1,000 views per video, which he calls weak, and his channel looks small - but in the last year YouTube directly attributed about $500k in profit. The mechanic he names is that every video is functionally the video sales letter on a landing page, except you get in front of the same person every single day: his returning viewership is 73%. He also frames the paid comparison - 46,000 views across his channel in 28 days would cost roughly $45k at a $10 CPM, so the organic equivalent is $45k of ad spend for free. Cody adds the targeting argument: YouTube organic puts you in front of the right people far more effectively than you could target them with paid, so you get a better customer at lower volume. Jackie's constraint is important: this only works because of the niche - it works in B2B and wouldn't work for farming or raising dogs - and his audience are digital natives who are resistant to ads, so organic works much better on them.

> "directly attributed from YouTube, it's like 500k in profit"

**How to do it**

1. Stop judging a B2B channel on view count; judge it on returning viewership and attributed revenue.
2. Publish on a cadence frequent enough that the same viewer sees you repeatedly - he was around 300 consecutive days.
3. Treat each video as a video sales letter: deliver value and make the offer, rather than only entertaining.
4. Calculate the paid equivalent of your organic views at your category's CPM to size what the channel is actually worth.
5. Add a trackable CTA link so revenue can be attributed to specific videos.
6. Accept that this leverage is niche-dependent - it works where the audience is commercially valuable and ad-resistant.

**Tools:** YouTube

**Pitfall:** He is explicit that the returns come from the niche, not the format - the same channel discipline in a low-commercial-value niche produces views without revenue.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, a small-audience YouTube channel aimed squarely at clinic owners could be worth far more than the view counts suggest - the metric to watch is returning viewership and demo requests attributed to video, not views.

**Apply anywhere:** A small-audience channel aimed squarely at your buyer can be worth far more than the view counts suggest - the metric to watch is returning viewership and attributed pipeline, not views.

*core · concrete actions · source 35*

> "we did a YouTube video with James Dooley to show"

**How to do it**

2. Produce a YouTube video directly targeting that query/topic, ideally featuring a recognized name or collaborator in the niche to add credibility signals.
3. Write a detailed, keyword-rich YouTube description covering the query topic thoroughly, since detailed descriptions are what the source says 'influences AI too.'
4. Publish the video and monitor rankings quickly, since video can rank within a couple of hours rather than the days or weeks typical of blog content.
5. Search the target query in Google afterward and check whether an AI Overview appears and which sources it cites.
6. If the video isn't yet the cited source in the AI Overview, strengthen its description, on-page context around the embed, or authority signals and re-test rather than assuming citation is guaranteed. (inferred)
7. Replicate this test across other priority queries relevant to the business to build a repeatable video-to-AI-Overview-citation playbook. (inferred)

**Tools:** YouTube, Google AI Overviews, Google Search

**Pitfall:** Assuming only written/blog content can earn an AI Overview citation — this deliberate test shows video can be engineered specifically to become the top cited source for a chosen query.

### 3. Each synthetic query expects a specific content format  `20.14`
*core · content insights · source 20*

Beyond topical relevance, each synthetic sub-query inside a fan-out appears to carry its own implicit expected content format — some queries expect a video answer specifically; if no video exists for that query, the AI system falls back to whatever's available, but if a relevant video does exist, the system will preferentially pull and use it. Reddit and YouTube are named as the two most-cited source types overall right now, with LinkedIn Pulse also showing up frequently, meaning content strategy needs a genuine format mix, not text-only assets. There isn't yet good tooling to systematically detect which format a given synthetic query expects, so the recommended approach is manual observation of what's currently being pulled/cited for your target queries.

> "there's a content format expectation for each of the synthetic queries"

**Evidence:** 'there's a content format expectation for each of the synthetic queries... if you're the one who has a video, they will definitely use that video when pulling results,' with Reddit, YouTube, and LinkedIn Pulse named as the most commonly cited source types.

### 4. Name a podcast for what people search inside Apple and Spotify  `68.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 68*

Cody treats Apple Podcasts and Spotify as search engines and names shows accordingly. His example is a friend's company he advised: they wanted to appear for biotech and biotech startups inside Apple Podcasts, so the show is literally called the Biotech Startups Podcast, and it comes up when you search biotech startups. He says the ranking factor inside those platforms is downloads, plus keyword placement in three fields: the show title, the show description and the episode show notes. He calls this podcast SEO. He also credits the same move for Greg Isenberg's Startup Ideas Podcast, arguing the rename let it rank for startup ideas and reach entrepreneurs, a far larger body of people than those already running businesses. This is a naming decision you cannot cheaply reverse later.

> "They call it podcast SEO within Apple"

**Evidence:** The Biotech Startups Podcast ranks for biotech startups in Apple Podcasts; Cody cites Greg Isenberg's rename to Startup Ideas Podcast as the same play.

**How to do it**

1. Search your candidate keywords inside Apple Podcasts and Spotify before naming the show and see what already ranks.
2. Name the show as the keyword phrase plus the word podcast, not as a clever brand name.
3. Put the same keyword in the show description.
4. Write per-episode show notes carrying the topic keywords for that episode.
5. Drive downloads deliberately, since downloads are the ranking lever inside both platforms.
6. Check your position for the target phrase inside both apps monthly and adjust the description copy if it slips.
7. Prefer a broader audience term over a narrow one when the two are close in volume.

**Tools:** Apple Podcasts, Spotify

**Pitfall:** Naming the show after your company or a pun leaves you invisible to in-app search, and renaming later resets the audience's recognition of the show.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau launches a show, name it for what aesthetic and healthcare practice owners actually type, such as a practice-management or clinic-growth phrase, not a Pabau-branded title.

**Apply anywhere:** Name your podcast after the phrase your buyers search inside Apple and Spotify, repeat it in the description and show notes, and push downloads. In-app search is a real acquisition channel most shows ignore.

### 5. One long-form video, cut into shorts, distributed everywhere - YouTube is the VSL  `58.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 58*

Jesper's YouTube model treats the long-form video as the destination rather than the distribution. He records one 10-15 minute long-form video, chops it into shorts, and posts those shorts on YouTube, TikTok, Facebook, Instagram and X. The shorts get far more views than the long-form - his numbers are 400-500 views on a long-form video against 1,500-2,000 on a short - and a lot of the people watching the shorts hop over to YouTube. Once they land on the channel they're captured: they subscribe, join the newsletter, or find the videos about his specific products. The important shift he names explicitly: the purpose is no longer to rank the YouTube video on page one for long-tail keywords - most of his traffic now comes from social channels rather than Google. He is also experimenting with an AI avatar of himself made with HeyGen, and Cody points at Julian Goldie publishing several AI-generated long-form videos a day using the same approach.

> "starting point is I'm creating a long form video"

**How to do it**

1. Record one long-form video of 10-15 minutes on a topic your buyers care about.
2. Cut it into shorts and post them on YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Facebook, Instagram and X.
3. Make the long-form video the destination the shorts point to, not the other way round.
4. Give the channel a clear next step for people who arrive: newsletter, subscribe, or a product video.
5. Expect shorts to outperform long-form on views by roughly 3-4x, and treat that as top-of-funnel.
6. Measure the channel on subscribers and enquiries, not on video rankings in Google.

**Tools:** YouTube, TikTok, HeyGen

**Pitfall:** The model depends on the long-form video being genuinely good, because the shorts only get people to the channel - Cody's contrast is that a video is a 10-minute video sales letter, so weak long-form wastes the distribution.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, one solid long-form video a week cut into shorts across channels is a far better use of production time than separate content per platform - and the long-form video is where the demo request actually happens.

**Apply anywhere:** One solid long-form video a week cut into shorts across channels is a far better use of production time than separate content per platform - and the long-form video is where the enquiry actually happens.

### 6. Only Gemini reads YouTube video transcripts natively, not ChatGPT or Claude  `19.9`
*core · content insights · source 19*

When publishing or promoting user-generated video content on YouTube, including Shorts, Gemini is specifically called out as the only major LLM with direct access to a YouTube video's full transcript. ChatGPT does not have this access, and neither does Claude. That means for any AI model besides Gemini, the video's on-page text description is the only way its content becomes visible to that model at all. So any UGC or brand video aimed at broad AI-answer visibility needs a long, detailed written description rather than a short caption, since a thin description effectively makes the video invisible to ChatGPT- and Claude-driven answers.

> "Gemini has access to all the transcripts for a YouTube"

**Evidence:** Direct statement in the interview: "Gemini has access to all the transcripts for a YouTube video, but Gemini's the only one that does... ChatGPT doesn't, Claude doesn't."

**Apply:** For any Pabau YouTube content, such as product demos, webinar clips, or customer stories, write long, genuinely descriptive video descriptions rather than short captions, since that description, not the spoken content, is what ChatGPT and Claude actually have to work with when deciding whether to reference or cite the video.

### 7. Post short TOFU videos with a memorable-domain CTA redirect  `55.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 55*

The funnel's entry point is a short (under 2 minutes, ideally under 1 minute) mobile-format video posted natively to TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, and Facebook, each one targeting a single top-of-funnel keyword phrased as a real question people search (e.g., "why am I always hungry when dieting," "how to reduce admin time in a dental practice," "why is my dog always itchy"), answered directly on camera, ending with a call to action pointing to a cheap, memorable domain (the host bought freeseoknowledge.com for about $11) that 301-redirects to a lead-capture form. This is positioned as best suited to high-ticket offers ($200-$1,000+/month SaaS, $100-$300/month subscriptions) because the high-touch funnel that follows is only worth the setup effort when a single sale is valuable.

> "It's a keyword and a question, something that people really search"

**How to do it**

1. Choose an SEO research tool (Moz, Ahrefs, or Semrush) to find top-of-funnel question keywords relevant to your offer.
2. Identify keywords phrased as real questions (e.g., "why is my dog always itchy") rather than generic head terms.
3. Buy a short, memorable, easy-to-spell domain purely for this funnel's call to action (roughly $10-15/year) and set it to 301-redirect to your lead-capture form.
4. Record a mobile-format video under 2 minutes (aim for under 1 minute) directly answering the target question, filmed vertically on a phone or via a computer camera cropped to mobile dimensions.
5. End the video with a spoken call to action naming the memorable domain (e.g., "head over to yourdomain.com").
6. Publish the same video natively to TikTok, Instagram, YouTube (including Shorts), Facebook, and LinkedIn.
7. Repeat with a new question/keyword regularly, prioritizing questions with little existing video competition over generic high-competition head terms.

**Tools:** Moz, Ahrefs, Semrush, TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Facebook, LinkedIn

### 8. Publish a deep tutorial video for a tiny niche and take the inbound leads  `68.24`
*core · concrete actions · source 68*

Cody's claim is that a YouTube video showing exactly how to do a specific job for a specific business type generates leads even at very low view counts. His worked example: spin up a channel doing Google Ads for auto body shops, and record yourself walking through the exact process step by step. The owners who watch it conclude it looks like a lot of work, and the CTA is a link below to schedule a discovery call. He says he cannot express how powerful such a video is even at 300 views, and guarantees it is generating leads for whoever made it. The mechanism is qualification by depth: the only people who sit through a detailed tutorial for one narrow trade are people in that trade with that exact problem, so the audience is small and perfectly filtered. The same video also surfaces the recurring problem that becomes your product.

> "maybe it only has like 300 views"

**Evidence:** Cody guarantees a 300-view deep tutorial generates leads for the person who made it, and traces the auto body shop example through to a product idea.

**How to do it**

1. Pick one narrow business category and one job that category needs done.
2. Record yourself performing the whole job on screen, without withholding the method.
3. Title the video for the exact category and task, such as how to run Google Ads for auto body shops.
4. End with a spoken CTA to book a discovery call, and put the booking link in the description.
5. Write a detailed description covering the process, since that is what non-Gemini models read.
6. Publish and ignore view count; measure discovery calls booked instead.
7. Note the problems commenters and callers raise repeatedly, since that is your future product.

**Tools:** YouTube

**Pitfall:** Holding back the method to force a call kills the effect. The video works because the viewer sees the full process and decides it is more work than they want, so a partial walkthrough converts worse, not better.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau could publish narrow walkthrough videos for practice operations tasks, such as setting up a treatment consent workflow or a recall campaign, and judge them on demo requests rather than views.

**Apply anywhere:** Record a genuinely complete tutorial for one narrow trade and one specific task, end with a booking link, and measure booked calls not views. Three hundred of exactly the right viewers beats a broad audience.

### 12. Automated pipeline to repost old viral videos with AI descriptions  `19.2`
*useful · ai workflows · source 19*

Kaleigh runs a repurposing pipeline built from three tools chained together: reusevideo.com automatically copies every new TikTok she posts over to Instagram Reels, and separately auto-uploads a copy of each posted video into Google Drive so her whole video library is backed up in one place. From there, she opens the video in Google Drive and uses Gemini's built-in access to Drive files to transcribe the video and generate a new, keyword-optimized description, placing her target keyword at the very start of the description. She used this to revive a two-year-old short-form video, about how Apple invented product-unboxing as an industry, that had gone viral once before; reposting it with a fresh Gemini-written description took about two hours end-to-end and the repost had already reached 20,000 views by the time of the interview.

> "an automation through reusevideo.com that makes it so"

**How to do it**

1. Set up an account with an automation tool such as reusevideo.com and connect your TikTok and Instagram accounts so every new TikTok post is automatically cross-posted to Instagram Reels.
2. In the same automation, connect a Google Drive destination so every posted video is automatically archived into a Drive folder (inferred exact connection step: Drive is added as an integration inside reusevideo.com).
3. Periodically browse your Google Drive video archive to find older videos that previously performed well and haven't been reposted in six months or more.
4. Open the chosen video file in Google Drive and invoke Gemini, Drive's built-in AI, on the file.
5. Ask Gemini to transcribe the video and draft a new description for the platform you're reposting to.
6. Edit Gemini's draft description so your target keyword sits at the very beginning of the text.
7. Repost the video natively on the target platform using the new keyword-led description.
8. Track the repost's view count over the following hours or days to confirm the revival is working before repeating the process on other archived videos.

**Tools:** reusevideo.com, Google Drive, Gemini

### 13. Get YouTube channel verified early to lift daily upload caps  `28.5`
*useful · concrete actions · source 28*

A new, unverified YouTube channel has a strict daily upload cap, recalled as roughly 25-50 videos per day, with the effective ceiling for this catalog stalling around 250 total videos, which becomes a hard bottleneck when migrating a large existing video library quickly. The fix was requesting channel verification, specifically providing company registration documents to prove the channel belongs to a legitimate, active business; verification raised the daily cap to roughly 120 videos a day. The team leveraged an existing Google Ads account rep to fast-track this, but believes a standard verified-badge application process achieves the same result without needing an ad-spend relationship.

> "we need your company registration, basically to make sure"

**How to do it**

1. Before starting a bulk video upload, check whether your YouTube channel is verified, since unverified channels are capped at roughly 25-50 uploads per day and the cap can shrink further if early videos get low view counts.
2. Apply for YouTube channel verification directly through YouTube's own verification process (inferred: via YouTube Studio, Settings, Channel, then the verification option), providing company registration documents proving the channel represents a real, active business.
3. If your company already runs Google Ads on the same account or business entity, contact your Google Ads account representative to ask whether they can expedite verification given the existing advertising relationship.
4. Once verified, re-check the new daily upload cap, expecting a substantially higher limit such as roughly 120 per day, before scheduling a bulk-upload pipeline.
5. Plan the bulk migration timeline around the verified cap rather than the unverified one, since attempting to upload a large catalog before verification will stall for weeks at the lower limit.

**Tools:** YouTube, Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Starting a large-scale video migration before checking or requesting channel verification — the unverified cap can shrink further over time if early uploads don't get enough views to prove you're not spam, compounding the bottleneck.

### 14. Jump-start a YouTube channel with one-cent Twitter CPC ads  `68.8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 68*

Cody's method for training YouTube's algorithm on a new podcast channel, since podcasts are not built for that algorithm and it needs to learn who wants long-form. He buys deliberately cheap off-platform traffic on Twitter: target the whole world rather than a country, set the CPC max bid at one cent, and run it from an account that looks like a normal person, not a brand. The creative is a screenshot of the YouTube video with copy in the register of an organic post, roughly: just watched X podcast, learned Y thing, crazy tbh. He reports link clicks landing around 0.3 cents. He is frank that the traffic quality is terrible, but the share of clicks coming from the US and Commonwealth countries is high enough that subscribers arrive very cheaply, and he says this can take a channel from zero to a thousand subscribers. He has run the same play on news channels but says it works best for podcasts.

> "put the CPC max bid at one cent"

**Evidence:** Link clicks at about 0.3 cents; Cody says the process can take a channel from zero to a thousand subscribers, and has been run on news channels too.

**How to do it**

1. Set up a Twitter ad account and run the campaign from a profile that reads as a real person rather than a brand page.
2. Set targeting to worldwide rather than a specific country, which is what makes the price collapse.
3. Set the CPC max bid to one cent.
4. Use a screenshot of the YouTube video as the creative.
5. Write the copy as an organic-looking post, such as 'just watched X podcast, learned Y thing, crazy tbh'.
6. Send the click straight to the YouTube video, not to a landing page.
7. Watch cost per link click, expecting roughly 0.3 cents, and watch what share of clicks come from the US and Commonwealth countries.
8. Stop once the channel has enough subscribers and watch time for YouTube to start serving the long-form content organically.

**Tools:** Twitter, YouTube

**Pitfall:** Most of this traffic is junk. If you judge it on engagement rate or watch time per click you will kill it early; the only reason it works is that the absolute cost per real subscriber stays low.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau builds a video channel for practice owners, this is a cheap way to seed the first subscribers, but the worldwide targeting conflicts with a business selling into specific markets, so treat it as algorithm training only and never as lead generation.

**Apply anywhere:** Seed a new YouTube channel with worldwide Twitter ads at a one-cent CPC bid, using an organic-looking screenshot post from a personal-looking account. The traffic is low quality but cheap enough to teach the algorithm who your content is for.
