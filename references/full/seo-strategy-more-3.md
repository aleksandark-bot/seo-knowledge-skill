### 1. Expect sales to outrun traffic when you switch to intent-led topics  `138.13`
*useful · general insights · source 138*

Cup & Leaf's headline numbers are worth holding as a shape rather than a target. Over two months, sales from SEO rose more than 300% while organic traffic rose about 175%. Sales grew close to twice as fast as traffic. That gap is the whole argument for the intent-led approach: the extra traffic was better qualified and the paths from article to store were shorter. It also gives a diagnostic. If you make the same changes and sales grow at the same rate as traffic, the topic selection worked but the conversion path did not, and the collection or destination work is where to look. If traffic grows and sales do not, you have added more 'green tea shot' pages.

> "we're getting more than 300% more sales from SEO"

**Evidence:** Cup & Leaf, two months: SEO sales up more than 300%, organic traffic up about 175%.

**Pitfall:** Reading 300% as a benchmark to hit. It came off a small base on a store with an obvious product-to-topic mapping; the transferable part is the ratio between the two growth rates, not the absolute figure.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau reports on an intent-led content push, chart demo requests and sessions on the same axis. Demos growing faster than sessions is the signal the approach is working; equal growth means the destination pages need work.

**Apply anywhere:** Chart conversions and sessions together after an intent-led content change. Conversions outpacing sessions confirms the topic shift worked; matching growth points at the conversion path, and traffic-only growth means the topics are still wrong.

### 2. Expect the demand-generation inflection point around the two-year mark  `130.9`
*useful · general insights · source 130*

Grow and Convert give a full benchmark for a three-year pain point SEO engagement on an all-in-one platform. They produced more than 120 pieces of content, each tailored to one specific valuable keyword. Around 80% rank on page 1 for their target keyword, split as 78% page 1, 16% page 2 and 5% page 3. By position, 41% sit in positions 1-3, 37% in positions 4-10 and 21% in positions 11-50. Content now produces over 150 demo signups a month, with 30-50% counted as qualified leads by the client. The timing detail matters most: the graph shows an inflection point around the two-year mark, when demo signups spiked over 100 a month and kept growing. Anyone budgeting a program on twelve months of data will read it as flat when it is pre-inflection.

> "the inflection point that happened around the"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: 120+ pieces over nearly 3 years, 78% on page 1, 41% in positions 1-3, 150+ demo signups a month, 30-50% MQL rate, inflection at the two-year mark.

**How to do it**

1. Set the program's reporting horizon at three years, with the inflection expected around month 24.
2. Report cumulative conversions per month, not traffic, so the compounding curve is visible.
3. Track the share of published pages on page 1, in positions 1-3, and on page 2, as the leading indicators.
4. Aim for roughly 40 pieces a year, each targeting one specific keyword rather than a theme.
5. Agree an MQL rate with sales up front; 30-50% of demo requests being qualified is the benchmark here.
6. Do not cut the program at the twelve-month review if page-1 share is climbing while conversions are still modest.
7. Keep a backlog of unclaimed buying-intent keywords so the curve does not flatten from running out of targets.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Judging a pain point SEO program at twelve months. The conversion curve is still shallow then, and cutting it kills the compounding just before the inflection Grow and Convert observed at two years.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should set Pabau's content reporting to three years with a month-24 expectation, and report page-1 share and positions 1-3 share as the early indicators. Cumulative demo requests per article, not sessions, is the metric that shows the curve.

**Apply anywhere:** Budget a buying-intent content program over three years and expect the conversion inflection around month 24. Report page-1 share and top-3 share as leading indicators, cumulative conversions as the outcome, and do not cut the program at the one-year review while ranking share is still climbing.

### 3. Expect to compete in search with audience-overlap brands, not product rivals  `93.10`
*useful · content insights · source 93*

Goolding names search competitors as a distinct type: companies writing about the same subjects without selling the same product, because they target the same audience. His example is Monday.com publishing extensively about remote work, since remote teams are an audience segment for their software. A small app that helps remote teams record time would not list Monday.com as a product competitor, yet on Google that is exactly who it faces, along with other large companies chasing remote workers. His point is practical: this makes ranking harder than a product-competitor analysis predicts, so the difficulty assessment has to be done against whoever actually holds the SERP. He does not treat it as a reason to stop, pointing to Underdog SEO as the approach for competing against large sites.

> "Similar audiences, different product use cases"

**Evidence:** Monday.com's extensive remote-work content, which competes with any product built specifically for remote teams.

**Pitfall:** Difficulty is assessed against known product rivals, so a topic looks winnable until the SERP turns out to be held by three large platforms that share your audience but not your category.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should assume SERPs for practice topics are held by large healthcare and business platforms that share the audience, and assess difficulty from the live results rather than from the practice-management competitor set.

**Apply anywhere:** Assess keyword difficulty from whoever actually holds the SERP, since large brands targeting your audience with a different product will often be the real competition.

### 4. Filter junk leads out before any channel comparison  `158.11`
*useful · best practices · source 158*

Every number Grow and Convert report in this case study is calculated after junk leads are removed, and they define junk explicitly as solicitous form fills rather than businesses actually interested in the service. This matters twice. It is why the previous agency's 26 leads over three months collapsed to 2 MQLs, and it is why the Search Partners comparison is credible: both the 7.6% and the 24% MQL rates were computed on cleaned data. The client also volunteered that junk lead volume fell sharply once Search Partners was off. Without this filtering step, agencies and vendors pitching your form fills are counted as demand, and any before-and-after comparison is measuring spam volume rather than campaign quality.

> "solicitous form fills rather than businesses who were actually interested"

**Evidence:** 26 raw leads over three months became 2 MQLs once junk and unqualified prospects were removed.

**How to do it**

1. Add a required field to the lead form that outbound spam will not fill correctly, such as company or practice name.
2. Review every form fill weekly and tag each as junk, unqualified or MQL in the CRM.
3. Define junk narrowly as solicitations and vendor pitches, and unqualified as a real prospect that fails your criteria.
4. Exclude junk from every reported count, including channel comparisons and before-and-after tests.
5. Track junk volume as its own line, since a spike signals a targeting or placement problem.
6. Recalculate historical baselines with the same filter before comparing any new period to them.
7. Ask sales each month whether inbound conversation quality changed, as a check on the tagging.

**Pitfall:** If junk is filtered in the new period but not the baseline, an improvement can be entirely an artefact of the cleaning. Apply the filter to both sides of any comparison.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should tag every /book-demo/ submission as junk, unqualified or qualified in the CRM, and report blog and paid performance on the qualified count only. Agency solicitations to a software company are high volume and will otherwise inflate content ROI.

**Apply anywhere:** Filter solicitations and vendor pitches out of your lead counts before reporting anything, and apply the same filter to the baseline period. Track junk volume separately, since a spike points to a placement problem.

### 5. Force specifics whenever a candidate says the word rankings  `163.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 163*

Grow and Convert supply three questions to break through vague ranking talk in an SEO pitch. First, what tangible results can we expect after 6 to 12 months. Second, whenever they say something about rankings, ask for the specifics: how many keywords can we rank for, how will we rank for those, and which pages on our site or which new pages will you use to rank for them. Third, how much weight do they place on technical SEO versus link building versus keyword strategy and content, and what portion of their time and effort goes to each. The point of the second question is that a real strategy already implies a page-to-keyword map. Someone who cannot name candidate pages is selling activity rather than a plan, and you will not be able to hold them to anything at review time.

> "how many keywords can we rank for? How will we rank for those?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list these as the specific questions to ask, drawn from client accounts of vague vendor promises.

**How to do it**

1. Ask what tangible results to expect at 6 and at 12 months, and write the answer down.
2. Every time the candidate says rankings, ask how many keywords, which ones, and by what mechanism.
3. Ask which existing pages and which new pages they would use for each named keyword.
4. Ask how they split time and effort across technical SEO, link building, and keyword strategy plus content.
5. Convert their answers into a one-page keyword-to-page map and get them to confirm it before signing.
6. Review against that map at month six, so the conversation is about named pages, not a traffic line.

**Pitfall:** Agreeing to 'improve rankings' with no keyword list. At the six-month review neither side can say whether the engagement worked, so it renews by default.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should require any SEO proposal for Pabau to arrive as a keyword-to-URL map with target positions, and use that map as the review document at month six.

**Apply anywhere:** Require any SEO proposal to arrive as a keyword-to-URL map with target positions, and use that map as the review document at month six.

### 6. Fund skill-building on the same line item as software spend  `175.5`
*useful · best practices · source 175*

The article's closing instruction is not just to cancel tools but to move the recovered money into improving your craft. The author's model for how strategy is actually learned is concrete: enlist a mentor, take a course, practice the skill, read, and test things you will probably fail at until you succeed. That is the alternative use of a cancelled subscription budget. The reason it matters is compounding. A tool's value stops the month you stop paying, while a strategy skill stays with the person and improves every future decision. For an SEO team this means treating a paid course, a consultant day or a set of deliberately designed tests as a line item that competes directly with the software renewal list.

> "start investing it into improving your craft"

**Evidence:** The author frames it as a direct swap: take the money saved from cancelled tools and invest it into improving your craft.

**How to do it**

1. Total the annual cost of every tool you cancelled in the stack audit.
2. Put that number into a single skills line in next quarter's budget.
3. Split it across the four learning modes the author names: a mentor, a course, reading, and paid practice.
4. Pick one skill per quarter rather than spreading the budget thin, for example SERP intent analysis or internal-link planning.
5. Design two or three tests a quarter to practice the new skill on real pages, and accept that some will fail.
6. Write up what each test taught, so the skill lands in the team, not one person.
7. At the next renewal cycle, compare that skills line against any new tool request and force a choice.

**Pitfall:** Cancelling the tools and quietly keeping the savings. The money disappears into the general budget and the skill gap stays exactly where it was.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should create a standing skills line in the Pabau marketing budget funded by cancelled subscriptions, and spend it on one SEO skill per quarter with tests run against real pabau.com pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Create a standing skills line funded by cancelled subscriptions, and spend it on one SEO skill per quarter with tests run against real pages on your own site.

### 7. Gate SEO investment on product-market fit and a describable customer  `181.3`
*useful · best practices · source 181*

Grow and Convert put an explicit qualification gate in front of the work. They say SEO works best once a company has product-market fit and a clear understanding of its ideal customer, and that if you are still figuring out who the customer is, content marketing may be premature. The mechanism is their own process dependency: an interview-driven method needs a team that can describe real customer pain points, product differentiators and use cases. Without that, the interview produces nothing. They add a decay argument: if you are still iterating on the product or the target audience, the keywords and the positioning the content is built around go out of date quickly, so the investment is written off before it compounds. Most of their clients have established products and existing customers, and are adding organic alongside paid.

> "If you're still figuring out who your customer is, content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say most of their clients have established products with existing customers and are scaling organic alongside paid acquisition.

**How to do it**

1. Ask whether the team can name a specific customer type, their pain points and the differentiator that wins deals, in a single sitting.
2. If they cannot, stop and run customer interviews or paid acquisition first rather than commissioning content.
3. Check whether the product roadmap is still changing the core positioning in the next two quarters.
4. If positioning is still moving, limit content to evergreen category pages you would keep under any positioning.
5. Confirm there are existing customers a writer can interview or read call recordings from.
6. Only then commit to a bottom-funnel content program, and revisit the gate whenever positioning shifts.

**Pitfall:** Publishing before positioning settles means rewriting the whole library later. The signal is finding that last quarter's articles describe a use case the product no longer leads with.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau is past this gate, so the useful half is the decay warning: when Pabau's positioning on a module changes, David should schedule a refresh of the articles built around the old framing rather than leaving them to age.

**Apply anywhere:** Only commit to a content program once positioning is settled and you have customers a writer can interview, and re-audit published articles whenever the positioning behind them changes.

### 8. Get a second opinion on your keyword choice before you commit content to it  `139.18`
*useful · best practices · source 139*

The single decision that produced Brandon's best result came from outside review, not from his own analysis. He had settled on 'business expense tracker' as the homepage target and only changed it because he brought the whole use case keyword group to a live Q&A and had Benji and Devesh tell him to switch to '1099 expense tracker'. He says the ability to ask questions and get one-to-one feedback on the calls was worth the cost of the course alone, and names support, not training material, as the main value he got. The general point is structural: keyword selection errors are invisible from inside the project because the term looks relevant and the volume looks good. A reviewer who does not share your assumptions catches the intent mismatch before you spend months of writing on it.

> "Being able to ask questions whenever I needed help"

**Evidence:** The '1099 expense tracker' homepage swap, which took the page from ranking for nothing to position one, came from external feedback on a monthly Q&A call, not from Brandon's own research.

**How to do it**

1. Write your keyword strategy into one document: the target audience, the three frameworks, and the homepage target.
2. Identify a reviewer outside the project - a peer, a paid community, or a consultant - who has no stake in your reasoning.
3. Send them the shortlist with the intent argument for each term, not just the volume figures.
4. Ask one specific question: is the searcher of this term the customer we described.
5. Escalate any term where the reviewer's read of the searcher differs from yours, and check the live SERP.
6. Change the target before commissioning content rather than after publishing.
7. Repeat the review at each major strategy shift rather than only at kickoff.

**Pitfall:** Internal review does not work here, because everyone on the project already shares the assumption that made the term look right. If nobody has ever pushed back on a keyword choice, the review is not independent.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau commits a quarter of writing to a keyword set, David should have someone outside the content team read the shortlist and say who they think is searching each term.

**Apply anywhere:** Have someone outside the project review your keyword shortlist and say who they think searches each term, before you commission content against it.

### 9. Google fixes ranking clusters, not individual bad SERPs  `03.12`
*useful · general insights · source 03*

The article closes on Google's Automatic Query Pattern Generation patent, which describes how Google generates new query templates and constructs indexes from them, reinforcing the query-template thesis that Google organizes and corrects search at the level of query patterns and clusters, not individual queries. This is backed by a quote attributed to Google's Paul Haahr, from his Improving the Search over Years speech: if a single query returns wrong results, Google will not fix it in isolation, but if that query belongs to a broader cluster, Google will fix the cluster, because pattern-based ranking solves problems faster than fixing individual SERPs one at a time. NavBoost is cited as a related hybrid ranking concept combining clicks, topicality, and PageRank into one factor, underscoring that Google's ranking systems are built around patterns and combined signals rather than page-by-page or query-by-query evaluation.

> "Automatic Query Pattern Generation patent explains how Google generates new query templates"

**Evidence:** Quoted statement attributed to Google's Paul Haahr in his Improving the Search over Years speech: individual wrong-result queries are not fixed unless they belong to a fixable cluster or pattern; supported by the existence of Google's Automatic Query Pattern Generation patent.

**Apply:** Because Google appears to evaluate and correct rankings at the query-cluster/pattern level, fixing or improving one single underperforming page in isolation is less effective than fixing the whole pattern of similar pages/queries at once, which validates investing in topical maps and query-template coverage as a structural strategy rather than optimizing individual articles independently.

### 11. Have founders write the blog until the topic outgrows them  `168.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 168*

For CPC Strategy's first several years, Nii Ahene and one partner were the blog's only writers, and it was years before anyone else earned a byline. The stated reason was cost control during the recession, since handling content creation themselves kept operating expenses low. But the second-order effect mattered more. The content that built the agency's authority was industry news and proprietary benchmark data, both of which required the founders' direct access to vendors, rate cards and client accounts. A hired writer in year one could not have produced either. The rule this suggests is that founder-written content is correct while the differentiating information lives only in the founders' heads, and delegation becomes safe once the information is captured in a dataset or process someone else can query.

> "Nii and one of his partners served as the main writers"

**Evidence:** CPC Strategy's blog was written by two of the four founders for several years, which kept operating expenses low through the recession while building the agency's authority.

**How to do it**

1. List what makes your content different from a competent freelancer's version: vendor relationships, client data, or first-hand operating experience.
2. If those inputs exist only in founders' heads, keep founders writing rather than hiring a writer who will produce generic coverage.
3. Cap founder writing time at a fixed weekly slot so it does not expand into the whole week.
4. Prioritize the pieces only a founder can write, meaning news you hear first and data only you hold, and skip generic explainers entirely.
5. Capture the differentiating inputs into something queryable as you go: a performance dataset, a vendor contact list, a documented process.
6. Hand off to a hired writer only once that capture is complete, and give them the researcher role first.
7. Keep founder bylines on the news and data pieces even after hiring, since those carry the authority.

**Pitfall:** Founders keep writing long after the information has been captured, which caps output at whatever hours they can spare and stalls the program at a few posts a month.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's most defensible articles are the ones drawing on internal product and practice data. Those should carry named internal authors rather than a generic staff byline, and the internal data should be documented so writers can use it without a founder in the loop.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep founders writing while the differentiating information lives only in their heads, cap the time to a fixed weekly slot, and hand off only once that information is captured in a dataset or documented process.

### 12. Hire an individual contractor only when the pages already exist  `163.9`
*useful · best practices · source 163*

Grow and Convert are specific about the one scenario where an individual SEO specialist beats an agency or an in-house team: you already have a lot of content on the site and want better performance from pages that exist. Their example is an ecommerce site with dozens or hundreds of product pages, where a contractor provides ongoing technical maintenance and on-page optimization. They apply the same logic to themselves, saying they are not the right fit for a company already ranking for many buying-intent keywords that needs 100-plus existing URLs maintained and improved. The decision rule is about which job you actually have. Optimizing an existing inventory is maintenance work suited to one skilled person. Creating dozens of new pages that rank and convert is production work that needs a team.

> "looking to get the best performance out of their"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name themselves as the wrong fit for a company needing 100-plus existing URLs maintained, and point that work to an in-house SEO or technical contractor instead.

**How to do it**

1. Count the URLs on your site that already target commercial keywords and get impressions.
2. Decide which job you have: improving existing pages, or creating pages that do not exist yet.
3. If the job is improvement across a large existing inventory, hire an individual technical and on-page contractor on a retainer.
4. If the job is creating dozens of new buying-intent pages, do not hire a single contractor; you need writing and strategy capacity too.
5. Scope a contractor engagement around a fixed page list and a measurable before-and-after on those URLs.
6. Revisit the decision once the existing inventory is optimized, since the job then changes to production.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Hiring a technical contractor when the real gap is that the pages for your money keywords have never been written. Months pass improving pages that were never going to rank for them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has a large existing library of template, blog and code-reference pages, which fits the contractor profile for on-page and technical maintenance. New buying-intent pages remain in-house work.

**Apply anywhere:** A large existing page library fits the individual-contractor profile for on-page and technical maintenance. Creating new buying-intent pages is a different job that needs strategy and writing capacity.

### 17. Most agencies optimize for traffic while most buyers want sales  `178.16`
*useful · general insights · source 178*

Grow and Convert state the structural mismatch behind most failed content engagements. In their experience most companies want leads and sales, meaning real customers and measurable ROI, while most content agencies are built to grow traffic and email marketing lists. Neither side is wrong, but the two require genuinely different strategies, and the buyer often does not realize a choice is being made. Some arrive with a vague desire to do inbound marketing and assume content exists only for traffic and brand awareness. Grow and Convert's point is that this is a choice, and it should be made explicitly at the start. They add a useful asymmetry: if you genuinely want traffic growth, you will find a matching agency easily, because that is what the market is built to sell. The scarce supply is the sales-optimized strategy.

> "most content agencies are built to grow traffic and email marketing lists"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name this mismatch as the cause of the biggest complaint they hear from companies who had bad agency experiences.

**How to do it**

1. Write down which of three outcomes you are buying: traffic, email signups, or leads and purchases.
2. Say the answer out loud in the first agency call rather than waiting to be asked.
3. Accept that a traffic-optimized agency is easy to find and a sales-optimized one is not, and budget search time accordingly.
4. Ask the agency which of the three their existing case studies actually report on; that reveals what they optimize for.

**Apply anywhere:** Sequence the program by intent, not by volume: template keywords, then competitor comparisons and alternatives, then the apps and tools variants of your software terms, and top-funnel pain point pieces last. Skip the category tier if your product pages already hold it.

### 22. Pick SEO for its two structural advantages, not for being free  `129.22`
*useful · content insights · source 129*

Grow and Convert justify SEO as a channel on two properties other channels lack, and it is worth being precise about both because they set the terms of the ROI argument. First, the results are evergreen: once you rank for keywords that bring traffic, leads and customers, they keep producing organic traffic for as long as the rankings are maintained, which they say can be years when pages are crafted to deeply match search intent. They put 'free' in quotes deliberately, because getting and maintaining rankings takes people and resources — what is genuinely absent is any incremental cost per visitor. Second, the traffic is highly targeted. Unlike display or social advertising, where audiences come across marketing passively, organic search reaches people actively searching to buy the type of product you sell or to solve the problem it solves, which is why they see much higher conversion rates than other channels when the keywords are right.

> "organic search allows you to reach people who are _actively searching to buy"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report much higher conversion rates from organic search than from many other channels when the right keywords are targeted, and rankings holding for years when pages deeply match search intent.

**How to do it**

1. When comparing channels, score SEO on zero incremental cost per visitor rather than on being free.
2. Score it separately on searcher intent: the visitor is actively looking, unlike paid social or display.
3. Require the second property in the keyword plan, since it only holds if you target keywords buyers use.
4. Budget the ongoing people cost of maintaining rankings as a permanent line, not as a launch cost.
5. Compare SEO's conversion rate with your paid channels on matched intent, not on blended site averages.
6. Reject any SEO business case that leans on the word free rather than on those two properties.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Selling SEO internally as free traffic. It is not free, it takes people and resources to get and hold, and the promise collapses the first time a stakeholder sees the monthly invoice next to the word free.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should make the channel case on intent quality and zero incremental cost per visit, and compare organic demo-request rates against paid on matched high-intent queries rather than against sitewide averages.

**Apply anywhere:** Argue for SEO on two properties: no incremental cost per visitor once you rank, and visitors who are actively searching to buy rather than passively exposed. Both are conditional — rankings need maintaining, and the intent advantage only exists if you target buyer keywords. Never sell it internally as free traffic.

### 23. Pick either a small controlled footprint or a large uncontrolled one  `73.25`
*useful · general insights · source 73*

Barnard ran an experiment comparing how well machines could analyze his own digital footprint against someone with a far larger but badly organized one. His footprint is large and, by his own description, less impressive - fewer big newspapers writing about him - but incredibly well organized. The other person's is much bigger and much messier. Both strategies worked, which he says disappointed him because he wanted only his own to work. His conclusion is that there are two viable positions and a difficult middle: either keep the footprint small and keep it under control, or splurge and go for the big play. His argument against the big play is that you cannot control it and it takes on a life of its own, though the compensation is that you do not have to look after it. His example is Sting, who does not need to manage his digital footprint or an AI's opinion of him because the sheer volume of news does the work.

> "both strategies work"

**Evidence:** Barnard's own comparison test: his well-organized but less impressive footprint performed as well as a far larger, badly organized one under machine analysis.

**How to do it**

1. Decide honestly which position you are in: a small controllable footprint or the budget to generate overwhelming coverage.
2. If controllable, cap the number of profiles and mentions you create at a number you can genuinely maintain and correct.
3. Keep every one of those mentions identical in wording and linked back to the entity home.
4. If going large, accept you will not control the message and budget for volume rather than for consistency.
5. Avoid the middle: enough mentions to be uncontrollable but too few to overwhelm ambiguity is the worst position.
6. Re-audit the footprint size against your maintenance capacity annually and prune anything you cannot keep current.

**Pitfall:** Landing in the middle. A footprint too large to police but too small to drown out contradictions leaves the machine reconciling conflicting versions, which is the exact ambiguity that suppresses confidence.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sits in the controllable band, so cap the profile and directory footprint at what the marketing team can keep current, and put the effort into wording consistency rather than volume of listings.

**Apply anywhere:** Choose deliberately between a small, tightly controlled footprint and an overwhelming one. The middle is the worst place: too big to police, too small to drown out contradictions. Match footprint size to maintenance capacity.

### 24. Pick search-demand-led content unless you have a year of original ideas  `107.15`
*useful · best practices · source 107*

Grow and Convert reject the usual SEO versus non-SEO framing. All content should be written for users and all content benefits from being discoverable. 4. For each originality piece, decide the promotion platform before writing, because it will not get organic traffic on its own.
5. Measure search-demand pieces on compounding rankings and conversions, not on launch-week traffic.
6. Do not judge the two types by the same metric or the originality pieces will always look better in week one.
