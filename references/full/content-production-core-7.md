### 1. Sort the CRM by value, sales cycle and retention to find best customers  `122.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 122*

Grow and Convert's quantitative half of the best-customer exercise is a CRM sort on exactly three metrics: account value (the price the customer pays), sales cycle (how long the account took to close) and retention (how long they have stayed). A customer that scores well on all three is typically a great fit, and those companies get prioritized. For service businesses they add a fourth, softer input by asking account managers which companies were the most enjoyable to work with. The point is to stop writing for any customer and start writing for the ones who are easier to convert, pay the most and churn the least. This is a data pass, not a persona workshop, and it happens before pain point research.

> "sort by 3 key metrics"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use these three metrics on every engagement and describe an account clearing all three as typically a great fit.

**How to do it**

1. Export closed-won accounts from the CRM with contract value, close date, first-contact date and churn date.
2. Compute sales cycle in days as close date minus first contact.
3. Compute tenure in months for every still-active account.
4. Rank accounts on each of the three metrics separately, then keep only accounts in the top band on all three.
5. For service businesses, ask account managers to name the clients that were most enjoyable to work with and flag those rows.
6. Cross-check the shortlist against the qualitative names the research session produced.
7. Write the surviving accounts into a single best-customer list before any topic ideation starts.

**Pitfall:** Sorting on account value alone. A large account that took a year to close and churned in nine months is a bad template for content, and targeting more like it makes the pipeline worse, not better.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should pull the same three columns for its practice accounts and see which clinic size, country and treatment mix clears all three. Blog and template pages should then be aimed at that profile rather than at every aesthetic clinic.

**Apply anywhere:** Rank closed accounts on contract value, sales cycle length and retention, keep the ones strong on all three, and treat that group as the audience your content is written for.

### 2. Source differentiating detail from internal interviews before writing  `86.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 86*

Grow & Convert's stated fix for undifferentiated content is that the content performing best for AI visibility is written by humans who actually understand the product, informed by interviews with people inside the organization. They name the roles: sales, founders and product leaders, the people who can speak to specific features, use cases and differentiators you will not find anywhere else online. This is the input that makes a page unrepeatable, and unrepeatable is the condition for an LLM to have a reason to recommend you rather than the nearest competitor. It also feeds the four things a page must articulate: who it is for, what specific problems it solves, how it solves them differently, and proof it works.

> "informed by interviews with people inside your organization"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert attribute their best-performing AI-visibility content to human writers informed by interviews with sales, founders and product leaders.

**How to do it**

1. Before drafting a bottom-of-funnel page, book a 30-minute call with a salesperson who sells that use case.
2. Ask which objection or competitor comes up most on that exact type of deal and record the answer verbatim.
3. Ask a product lead which feature handles the workflow and what it does that the competing product does not.
4. Collect one named customer example with a specific result for the same use case.
5. Write the page around those three inputs, quoting the internal language rather than paraphrasing it into category prose.
6. Have the interviewee read the draft and cut anything they would not say on a call.
7. Reuse the same specifics across the comparison, alternatives and category pages so every mention says the same thing about you.

**Pitfall:** Interviews get skipped because they are slow and depend on other people's calendars, so the writer falls back on competitor pages as the source, which guarantees the content repeats the category consensus.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's article briefs should include a required interview slot with a Pabau salesperson or product owner for any comparison or category page, and the harvested specifics should be stored so later refreshes reuse them.

**Apply anywhere:** Make an internal interview a required brief input for any comparison or category page, and store the harvested specifics so later refreshes can reuse them.

### 3. Spell out the traditional method step by step before showing yours  `135.7`
*core · best practices · source 135*

For the 'rough cut video editing' post, Grow and Convert wrote out the traditional workflow step by step in the introduction and put their own workflow beside it. The reader sees the difference in step count immediately rather than being told the new way is easier. This works because the audience for a definitional term already knows the old method, so a side-by-side is legible to them without explanation. Getting the comparison done in the intro also earns the rest of the post: once the reader accepts the new approach, the body can be a straight walkthrough of the software instead of a balanced category guide. The same pattern ran across 'how to edit an interview video' and 'how to edit video fast', where the intro explained why traditional editors are tedious and why the product was built.

> "we actually spelled out the traditional method step-by-step vs. our method"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's posts using this intro pattern reached page one at more than 50% across 31 published posts, most within three months.

**How to do it**

1. Write the conventional workflow for the task as numbered steps, using the real actions a practitioner performs.
2. Write your product's workflow for the same outcome as numbered steps, at the same level of detail.
3. Put the two lists side by side in the introduction, before the first H2.
4. Do not editorialize the difference; let the step counts and the actions carry it.
5. Keep the conventional list accurate and fair, including the parts it does well, so an expert reader trusts you.
6. Follow the comparison with one sentence stating who should switch.
7. Spend the rest of the post walking through your method in enough detail that the reader could do it today.
8. Close with the trial or demo link positioned as the immediate next action, not as a generic CTA.

**Pitfall:** Writing a vague claim that the old way is slow instead of listing its steps. Experienced readers, who are the ones searching jargon terms, spot the hand-waving and leave.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles on tasks a practice does in spreadsheets or on paper should carry the same side-by-side: the paper consent process step by step, then the Pabau process step by step, in the intro of the relevant blog or template page.

**Apply anywhere:** In the introduction, list the conventional workflow as numbered steps and put your product's workflow beside it, so the difference is visible rather than asserted, then use the rest of the post as a walkthrough.

### 4. Spend a content hire's first three months on research, writers and promotion tests  `165.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 165*

Hyam is specific about how a new content marketer's first one to three months should be spent, and none of it is publishing volume. The list is: talking to customers and doing customer research, building the writing team by hiring writers and testing them out, starting to produce content, and testing promotion channels. That ordering is the same sequence he treats as the core grow skill set, applied as a ramp plan. It also gives a manager something to review at 30 and 60 days that is not a traffic number, since traffic from a standing start is not a fair reading that early. The failure this prevents is a new hire who spends month one filling a content calendar with topics chosen from a keyword tool, before speaking to a single customer.

> "The first 1-3 months should be spent talking to customers"

**Evidence:** Hyam's ramp from running content operations at Vistage, ThinkApps and Everwise.

**How to do it**

1. Week one to four: schedule customer interviews and write the survey, and do not commission any articles yet.
2. Require a written summary of pain points, recurring questions and candidate topics at the end of month one.
3. Month one to two: source freelance writers and run paid test assignments on real briefs rather than hiring on portfolio.
4. Month two: publish the first pieces drawn from the customer research, not from a keyword tool alone.
5. Month two to three: test at least three promotion channels and record traffic per channel per post.
6. Review at 30 and 60 days on research output, writer bench size and promotion tests, not on traffic.
7. Only start judging traffic against baseline increments from month three.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Demanding published volume in month one squeezes out the customer research, and the whole content plan then rests on keyword volume with no evidence about what customers actually ask.

**Apply at Pabau:** A new Pabau content hire should spend month one interviewing practice owners and Pabau support staff, month two building a writer bench with paid test briefs, and month three testing promotion in aesthetics communities.

**Apply anywhere:** Give a new content hire three months on customer research, writer recruitment with paid test assignments, first articles and promotion channel tests. Review those outputs at 30 and 60 days rather than traffic.

### 5. Spot keyword sprinkling by checking one keyword per page  `178.6`
*core · best practices · source 178*

Grow and Convert say several clients arrived with bad SEO-agency experiences, and digging into the content revealed the same pattern: keyword sprinkling. The agency produces an SEO article by scattering a set of keywords through the copy and calls that optimization. Their counter-rule is blunt. For the most valuable and competitive keywords you need one single article or landing page completely optimized for one keyword, not one article carrying several. That rule doubles as the audit test on a supplier's existing work. If a draft targets a cluster of loosely related terms with no page fully committed to any of them, you are looking at sprinkling. It is also the cannibalization prevention rule stated from the supply side rather than the site side.

> "one single article or landing page completely optimized for a single keyword"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert identified keyword sprinkling repeatedly after examining content produced for clients by previous SEO agencies.

**How to do it**

1. Pull three sample articles from the agency's portfolio and ask which single keyword each one targets.
2. Reject any answer that lists four or five keywords for one page.
3. Check the title, H1, URL and opening sentence of each sample for that one keyword.
4. Search the sample's target keyword and see whether the page actually ranks for it.
5. Scan the body for keyword variants inserted without changing the argument; that is the sprinkling signature.
6. Set the rule in your own brief: one page, one primary keyword, secondary terms only where they are genuine subtopics.
7. Before commissioning a new page, check you do not already have one targeting that keyword.

**Pitfall:** A sprinkled article often ranks briefly for nothing in particular and creates cannibalization with the page that should have owned the term. The signal is two of your own URLs swapping positions for one query.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau article should have one declared primary keyword recorded before writing, and the /SEO keyword selection step should refuse a second page against a keyword already owned.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask a supplier which single keyword each sample page targets. Several keywords per page is sprinkling. Commit one page to one competitive keyword and check you do not already own it elsewhere.

### 6. Staff a blog team as three writers plus one full-time researcher  `168.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 168*

Tinuiti's blog runs on a dedicated full-time staff of four, and the split is the interesting part. Three are writers who do nothing but draft original pieces. The fourth does nothing but research. Separating research from drafting is what lets three writers produce over 150 articles a year while also feeding 50 to 60 white papers and webinars, because the writers never lose a day chasing sources. The agency keeps 95% of content in-house and barely used freelancers as CPC Strategy or as Tinuiti. Webinar coordination and outreach is a separate full-time role again, not something the writers absorb. The pattern is one specialized role per bottleneck rather than generalists who each do a bit of everything.

> "three full-time writers who focus only on drafting original pieces"

**Evidence:** Tinuiti runs four full-time blog staff, three writers and one researcher, producing over 150 articles a year plus 50 to 60 white papers and webinars, with 95% of content made in-house.

**How to do it**

1. Count how many hours a week your writers currently spend sourcing data, booking interviews and verifying claims rather than drafting.
2. If that number exceeds roughly a day per writer, hire or assign one dedicated researcher before hiring another writer.
3. Define the researcher's output as a completed source pack per brief: data, citations, interview notes, competitor coverage.
4. Keep writers on drafting only, and route every source request through the researcher rather than letting writers self-serve.
5. Set the production target from capacity, not ambition: a team of this shape sustained about 150 articles a year plus 50 to 60 larger assets.
6. Keep the majority of production in-house rather than on freelancers if the subject matter depends on internal expertise.
7. Staff webinar or event coordination as its own role rather than adding it to a writer's workload.

**Pitfall:** Adding a fourth writer instead of a researcher when writers are source-starved raises headcount without raising output, because every writer still loses the same day a week to research.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau scales its article output, the next hire should be a dedicated researcher who assembles source packs for clinical and regulatory claims. That is also the role that would prevent the fact-check findings the editing pass currently catches late.

**Apply anywhere:** When writers are losing a day a week to sourcing, hire a dedicated researcher before another writer. Define their output as a complete source pack per brief and keep writers on drafting only.

### 7. Start with purchase-intent topics, then work up the funnel  `160.3`
*core · best practices · source 160*

Grow and Convert's stated order of operations for a new blog is purchase intent first, then work your way up the funnel. The reason is diagnostic speed: bottom-funnel topics tell you quickly whether the blog will drive leads at all. They put a timeframe on it. If product/market fit is there and positioning is good enough, conversions should appear from the blog somewhere between one and six months. If nothing is converting after a few months, they say go back to the drawing board and look at product/market fit or positioning rather than at the content. That converts the funnel order into a test with a deadline instead of a preference.

> "focusing on topics that have purchase intent first and working your way up the funnel"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state that with product/market fit and adequate positioning you should see blog conversions somewhere between one and six months.

**How to do it**

1. List the topics a buyer searches when they are already looking to purchase your category, including comparison, alternatives, pricing and best-of terms.
2. Publish those first, before any definitional or how-to content.
3. Put the conversion mechanism on those pages from day one so the test is valid.
4. Track leads attributed to the blog monthly from first publication.
5. At three months, if there are no leads, stop adding articles and audit product/market fit and positioning instead.
6. Expect conversions between one and six months when fit and positioning are sound; only then expand upward into middle and top funnel topics.

**Pitfall:** Starting a new blog with top-funnel education. It delays the conversion signal by months, so you cannot tell whether the channel works or the offer is broken.

**Apply at Pabau:** For a new Pabau content cluster, David should publish the purchase-intent pages first: software comparisons, pricing explainers and the versus pages, and only then the how-to and definitional posts around them. Set a three-month check on demo requests from the cluster before expanding it.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish purchase-intent topics before educational ones on a new blog, with the conversion path live from the first post. If no leads appear in about three months, audit the offer and positioning rather than adding more articles.

### 8. State the innovation in the title and intro so unfit readers self-select out  `135.3`
*core · best practices · source 135*

Grow and Convert wrote every bottom-of-funnel post so the title and introduction immediately state what makes the product different. Their online video editor post was titled 'Online Video Editor | Edit Your Videos with a Transcript'. The intro named the traditional approach, timeline editors like iMovie, acknowledged its strengths, described the pain of finding the exact timestamp, then explained how transcript editing removes it. The point is qualification in both directions. Readers who want a full effects suite leave before engaging, and readers who want the new method know a deep dive is coming. This is deliberate friction on a search listing, and it trades click-through for conversion rate, which is the right trade on a bottom-of-funnel keyword where the traffic is small anyway.

> "made sure the titles and intros immediately state our innovative solution"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's posts written this way averaged a 2.7% conversion rate on specific bottom-of-funnel keywords, against roughly 0.5% for top-of-funnel terms.

**How to do it**

1. Write the title as the keyword plus a short clause naming the mechanism, using a pipe or parenthesis, as in 'Online Video Editor | Edit Your Videos with a Transcript'.
2. Open the intro by naming the tool or method the reader already expects to be recommended.
3. Credit that method honestly with one sentence on what it does well.
4. Name the specific friction in that method, at the level of the actual action, such as finding the exact timestamp to cut.
5. Introduce your approach as the removal of that friction, in one or two sentences.
6. Say plainly who the post is for and who it is not for, so the wrong reader exits before scrolling.
7. Keep all of this above the first H2 so it is visible without scrolling.
8. Check the title against the SERP snippet width so the differentiator clause survives truncation.

**Pitfall:** Hiding the differentiator until the middle of the post to protect click-through. You collect clicks from people the product cannot serve, your conversion rate drops, and the pogo-sticking hurts the ranking you bought the clicks with.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's comparison and category posts should name the differentiator in the title, not just the keyword. Titles like 'Practice Management Software | Charting, Booking and Payments in One Record' let a searcher wanting a standalone booking widget leave before clicking.

**Apply anywhere:** Put the mechanism that makes you different into the title and the first paragraph, and name the conventional alternative you replace, so readers who are a poor fit leave before they cost you engagement signals.

### 9. Stop briefing marketers to invent the thought leadership themselves  `125.2`
*core · best practices · source 125*

Grow and Convert call outsourcing thought leadership the cardinal sin. Their reasoning is direct: your agency, freelancers and even your in-house marketing team did not found the company, do not set strategy and did not design the product, so they are not the source of disruptive ideas in your niche. Asked to produce thought leadership with no unique thoughts supplied, writers look sideways at competitors and produce educational content, which is the exact opposite of thought leadership. They name three recognizable outputs: the basic ultimate guide on sales processes from a sales SaaS, the CMO whitepaper rehashing tropes every CMO already knows, and the top ten industry trends post for a busy business buyer. The window dressing is often excellent, and it still fails, because nobody shares it.

> "trying to outsource it to content marketers or writers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's three named me-too archetypes seen repeatedly across B2B SaaS: the basic sales-process ultimate guide, the CMO whitepaper of obvious tropes, and the top ten trends post.

**How to do it**

1. Rewrite any brief that says produce thought leadership into a brief that names the internal person whose idea is being written up.
2. Refuse the assignment if no internal source is named on the brief.
3. Book a recorded interview with that person before the writer opens a document.
4. Give the writer the transcript as the substance and let them own structure, not ideas.
5. Screen every draft against the three me-too formats: the beginner ultimate guide, the trope-rehashing whitepaper, and the annual trends roundup.
6. If a draft matches one of those, send it back to the internal source rather than to the editor.
7. Judge the finished piece on whether anyone shared or linked to it, not on design quality.

**Pitfall:** The failure hides behind production quality. The ebook is well designed and the blog page is beautiful, so the piece passes internal review even though the ideas lead nothing and prospects ignore it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau brief that promises a point of view should name the clinician, product manager or account manager it came from. If David cannot name a source, the piece should be reclassified as a standard guide rather than dressed up as leadership.

**Apply anywhere:** Never brief a writer or agency to come up with the thought leadership itself. Name the internal expert whose idea is being written up on the brief, and reject any brief that leaves that line blank.

### 10. Stop editing at good enough rather than rewriting every AI sentence  `71.15`
*core · best practices · source 71*

Devesh sets an explicit stopping rule for editing AI output. On a sentence he was only so-so about, he decided to keep it, reasoning that if he tried to get every sentence to his standard he would just spend twelve hours writing it from scratch, and there is some level of good enough is good enough. He repeats the check twice more, telling himself he is probably over-editing and later that he might need to start editing less fastidiously or he will over-edit. The trade he describes is that with high-quality AI output you could rewrite everything, but that defeats the purpose. In practice he concentrated the effort where it pays: about ten minutes on the intro alone, and roughly forty minutes for the whole post.

> "good enough is good enough here"

**Evidence:** Devesh spent roughly 10 minutes editing the intro and about 40 minutes on the whole post, and twice interrupted himself mid-edit for over-editing.

**How to do it**

1. Decide before editing which sections deserve heavy work, normally the intro and any section carrying the main argument.
2. Spend disproportionate time there, around ten minutes on the intro of a single post.
3. On every other section, edit only for repetition, false claims and AI tells, and leave merely so-so sentences alone.
4. When you catch yourself rewriting a sentence that is already acceptable, stop and move to the next section.
5. Set a total time budget for the post, around forty minutes end to end for a piece of this size.
6. Ship when the budget is spent, on the basis that rewriting everything means writing it from scratch.

**Tools:** Google Docs

**Pitfall:** Over-editing is the failure mode with good AI output, not under-editing. The signal is rewriting sentences that carry no error, no repetition and no AI tell, purely because you would have phrased them differently.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial pass should be scoped to the intro, the Key takeaways and the argument-bearing sections, with the rest checked only against the fact and AI-tells rules, so per-article editing stays near an hour rather than a half day.

**Apply anywhere:** Concentrate editing on the intro and the argument-bearing sections, and leave merely acceptable sentences alone everywhere else. Set a time budget and ship when it is spent.

### 11. Stop publishing new pages, start refreshing declining ones  `10.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 10*

Rather than only publishing new content, systematically revisit existing pages whose traffic is slowly declining and refresh them with lightweight edits: update the visible or meta publish date, rewrite a couple of sentences, swap out an image, and add a couple of new list items or a short new section, then force a reindex of the page. Charles's own link-building-services page is the proof case: it was getting about 22 visits a month at the start of the year, he did a two-paragraph update plus two new list items and a date change in February, and it is now getting about 5,000 uniques a month with an Ahrefs-estimated traffic value of $20,000 for that single page. This kind of outsized bounce-back generally only works on pages that already carry historical topical authority and past ranking signals; a brand-new page can't replicate the same jump because it lacks that accumulated trust and relevancy history.

> "stop publishing, and start refreshing"

**How to do it**

1. Pull your site's organic-traffic-by-page report, for example in GSC or Ahrefs, and sort to find pages with a slow, sustained traffic decline over recent months.
2. Prioritize declining pages that already have historical topical authority, meaning older pages that once ranked well, over brand-new pages, since refreshes work best on pages with accumulated trust signals.
3. Open the page and rewrite roughly two paragraphs of existing copy as a real content update.
4. Add one or two new items to an existing list or section to reflect new information.
5. Swap out at least one image for a new or updated one.
6. Update the visible or meta "last updated" date on the page.
7. Save or publish the change, then request reindexing via Google Search Console's URL Inspection tool (inferred exact GSC mechanism for "force reindex").
8. Track the page's clicks and impressions in GSC and its estimated traffic value in Ahrefs over the following weeks to measure the lift.
9. Repeat this refresh cycle across other declining-but-historically-authoritative pages rather than defaulting to writing net-new content (inferred repeatable cadence).

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** This scale of result, going from 22 visits a month to 5,000 uniques a month, generally isn't achievable by publishing a brand-new page instead — it depends on the page already having topical authority and historical ranking signals to bounce back on.

### 12. Stop treating a writer hire as a content marketing hire  `101.8`
*core · best practices · source 101*

Grow and Convert's first misconception: companies that decide to invest in content marketing think they need to hire a writer. Writing is one part. The rest is content ideation and strategy, usually with an SEO component, plus promotion, analytics and attribution. They say this combination is uncommon in writers, and that even a person titled content marketer usually holds only a subset. The specific failure they attribute to the gap is that most content marketers produce only top-of-funnel introductory guides, which do not generate conversions or qualified leads. Their own delivery model puts three to five people on an account: SEO strategists, content writers, a PPC specialist, a project manager, and a designer. Their claim is they could not produce their results without those roles.

> "hiring a writer alone is not enough"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert staff each client account with three to five specialists across SEO, writing, PPC, project management and design.

**How to do it**

1. List the five functions content needs: ideation and strategy, SEO, writing, promotion, analytics and attribution.
2. Mark which of the five you already cover in-house honestly.
3. Hire or contract specifically against the gaps, not against the job title 'writer'.
4. If you hire one person, make ideation and strategy the skill you test hardest.
5. Check any agency proposal names who covers each of the five roles.
6. Audit the last quarter's output: if it is all introductory guides, the strategy function is missing.

**Pitfall:** A team of good writers with no strategy owner defaults to top-of-funnel guides, which produce traffic and no qualified leads.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content operation needs a named owner for topic selection and SEO strategy separate from whoever writes, otherwise the blog fills with generic aesthetic-industry guides that never surface a demo.

**Apply anywhere:** Content marketing is ideation, SEO, writing, promotion and attribution. Map which of those you already have before hiring, and fix the gap rather than assuming a writer covers it.

### 13. Stop using outside experts for any bottom-funnel piece  `126.7`
*core · best practices · source 126*

Grow and Convert used outside expert interviews constantly in their early agency days and have now stopped almost entirely, for two reasons they name. First, outside experts do not hold the same opinions as the company, so you submit the piece and an executive says we do not want to say that, or the CEO disagrees with this. That risk is worst on bottom-funnel pieces, which sit closest to the product and where the company should control the message. Second, it is hard to tie in the product: an outside expert has no reason to explain how and why your software beats the alternatives, and would not know how to position it if they tried. Sometimes it is worse, because the outside expert spends the interview pitching whatever they are working on, which makes little sense to include.

> "led us to stop doing it almost entirely"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert stopped outside-expert sourcing almost entirely at their agency after hitting both problems repeatedly.

**How to do it**

1. Split your content calendar into bottom-funnel and top-funnel pieces.
2. Ban outside expert sourcing on everything bottom-funnel.
3. For those pieces, name an internal expert instead at kickoff.
4. If an outside expert is already committed, get the internal positioning separately and write the product section from that.
5. Strip any outside expert quote that pitches the expert's own product or service.
6. Escalate to the executive owner before publishing any outside-expert opinion about product features.

**Pitfall:** Discovering the mismatch at review. The signal is an executive rejecting a specific claim in a near-final draft, which means the whole argument spine has to be rebuilt.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not source aesthetic-industry commentators for articles about scheduling, records or payments. Those are product-adjacent, so the opinions must come from inside Pabau. Outside clinicians stay useful for clinical accuracy, not for positioning.

**Apply anywhere:** Ban outside expert sourcing on bottom-funnel, product-adjacent articles. Their opinions will not match yours, they have no incentive to position your product, and some will use the interview to pitch their own.

### 14. Structure an opinion piece as two reasons then your alternative method  `125.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert show the anatomy of their opinion article for a client in the assistant hiring space. The founders had applied behavioral science to assistant hiring and found flaws in the traditional process. Early in the engagement the agency learned that recruiting agencies were a core competitor and that recruiters use the same ineffective process everyone else uses. That struck them as a unique idea precisely because you would expect recruiters, whose whole job is hiring talent, to be reliable. The piece was titled Why You Shouldn't Use a Recruiter for Your Executive Assistant. The introduction stated the two reasons recruiting services are not good at this. Dedicated sections then expanded each reason, including why executive assistant recruiters are typically the least experienced recruiters. Only then did they present the client's own methodology: three research-backed predictors of performance, then the tools and steps used to evaluate candidates against them.

> "Then we created dedicated sections to expand on each of those problems"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client opinion piece Why You Shouldn't Use a Recruiter for Your Executive Assistant, built on the discovery that recruiters use the same ineffective hiring process, structured as two reasons, then a section each, then the client's three-predictor methodology.

**How to do it**

1. Find the belief in your market that is widely assumed but that your team's experience contradicts, ideally about a competitor category rather than a product.
2. Test it for surprise: the claim should run against what a reader would expect by default.
3. Title the piece as the recommendation, in the form why you shouldn't do X for Y.
4. State the reasons, usually two or three, in the introduction so the reader knows the argument up front.
5. Give each reason its own section with the mechanism, such as why that role attracts the least experienced people.
6. Include concrete examples of the flawed process in action rather than asserting it is flawed.
7. Only after the critique, present your own method, starting with the research-backed criteria it evaluates.
8. Close by describing the specific tools and steps you use to evaluate against those criteria.

**Pitfall:** Leading with your own method turns the piece into a pitch and the critique reads as motivated. The order matters: argument first, method last.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has defensible positions about how practices are usually run, for example paper consent forms or spreadsheet-based recall. David can build one opinion piece per position, arguing the flaw for two sections before Pabau's approach appears.

**Apply anywhere:** Structure an opinion piece as: a title that states the recommendation, two or three reasons in the introduction, one section per reason with the mechanism and examples, then your own method presented last.

### 15. TMZ's update-vs-new-post rule for breaking news stories  `29.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 29*

At TMZ, Hank found the newsroom was republishing a breaking story as seven or eight separate new posts throughout the day instead of updating the original, which let Google re-rank each new post against the full competitive set (including outlets that stole the story) and leaked away the original scoop's ranking equity. The fix was a decision rule: if new information continues the same narrative, update the original post; if it is a genuinely new or parallel storyline, publish it separately. Layered on top was a two-step monitoring process - get the story into Google's News box, then track how long it stays there, since stories typically fell out of the News box 6-8 hours after breaking (not in the first 2-3 hours), which was the signal it was safe to spin off a new piece. After proving the policy with one early case (an Aretha Franklin story), TMZ's traffic floor rose from 12-15 million to 18-20 million monthly visits in quiet months, and the ceiling on big breaking-news months rose from 28 million to 35 million.

> "not everything should be updated, but if it's a continuation"

**How to do it**

1. Treat the first post on a breaking story as the canonical URL for that narrative.
2. Monitor Google's News box ranking for that URL in the hours immediately after publishing. (inferred)
3. When new information about the same story arrives, update the original post rather than publishing a new one.
4. Before publishing anything further, classify it as either a continuation of the existing narrative or a genuinely new/parallel storyline.
5. Fold continuations into the original post only; publish genuinely new or parallel storylines as separate pieces.
6. Watch for the story falling out of the News box, which typically happens 6-8 hours after breaking, not in the first 2-3 hours.
7. Only introduce a new piece on the topic once the original has fallen out of the News box.
8. Prove the policy on one high-visibility story first (TMZ used an Aretha Franklin obituary) to win newsroom buy-in before mandating it site-wide.
9. Track monthly floor (quiet-month visits) and ceiling (big-story-month visits) over time to measure whether the policy is working. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Republishing a breaking story as multiple new posts throughout the day causes Google to re-rank the newest post against the full competitive set - including the outlets that stole the story - which leaks away the ranking equity the original scoop had already earned.

### 21. The four-part intent-matching formula: keyword, answer, media, single CTA  `36.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 36*

The episode closes with a synthesized checklist for matching search intent on any page. First, place the target keyword in four places: the page title, the URL slug, the H1, and the beginning of the first sentence, with the meta description as optional. Second, give as concise an answer as possible, as early in the content as possible, landing above the fold before any scrolling is needed. Third, place clear media above the fold too, an image for most SEO or bottom-of-funnel queries since visitors want a fast answer, or a video where deeper engagement is the goal, chosen so that reading only the H1 and looking only at the image conveys the entire offering. Fourth, place a single call-to-action button above the fold with no competing secondary button, since additional options create hesitation.

> "use your target keyword in these four places"

**How to do it**

1. Place the exact target keyword in the page title, the URL slug, the H1, and the first sentence of the body content; treat meta description placement as optional.
2. Write the direct answer to the target query as concisely as possible and position it above the fold, before any scrolling is required.
3. Add one clear media element, image or video, above the fold as well.
4. For bottom-of-funnel or commercial queries, default to an image rather than video, since these visitors want the fastest possible confirmation they are in the right place.
5. Choose or design that image so the H1 plus the image alone communicate the entire page offering without needing to read further.
6. Place exactly one call-to-action button above the fold.
7. Remove any secondary or competing CTA button from the above-the-fold area, since multiple options increase visitor hesitation.
8. Run this four-part check (keyword placement, above-fold answer, above-fold media, single CTA) against every new page before publishing. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Adding a secondary CTA button alongside the primary one above the fold — extra options create hesitation and reduce the conversion rate of the primary action.

### 22. Three-phase AI pipeline: research, structure, then draft  `07.10`
*core · ai workflows · source 07*

This is the full repeatable pipeline for turning banked keyword research into a published article using ChatGPT or Claude. It runs on a saved prompt template with three fill-in fields — Topic (pulled from the Datawise content-planner/content-map view of your informational keywords), Audience (written as specifically as possible, e.g. 'homeowners in Dallas, Texas who have just purchased their electric vehicle'), and Goals — and is run at a higher reasoning-effort setting when available. The model responds in three phases rather than drafting immediately: Phase 1 surfaces currently-ranking competitor pages and proposed high-quality sources to cite (often government sites for factual/statistical claims); Phase 2 proposes structure (headings, brief capsule answers, a table, an FAQ section, and internal-link placement suggestions); only after both are approved does the creator request 'give me the first draft,' at which point proprietary/experience details should be injected. The output is then hand-edited in the model's canvas/inline-editing mode before being copied into WordPress with a generated meta description.

> "fill in the topic, your audience, and the goals"

**How to do it**

1. Confirm your informational-keyword CSV exports (people-also-ask and fan-out queries) are already uploaded to the ChatGPT/Claude project sources.
2. Open the saved content-capsule writing prompt template and fill in the Topic field using a topic surfaced by Datawise's content-planner/content-map view.
3. Fill in the Audience field with a specific description, not a generic one.
4. Fill in the Goals field, then select a higher reasoning-effort setting if your plan offers one, and submit the prompt.
5. Answer any clarifying questions the model asks before it drafts; if it asks too many, explicitly tell it to limit further questions.
6. Review Phase 1 output: the competitor pages currently ranking for the topic, and the proposed source list; adjust the suggested title if offered.
7. Review Phase 2 output: the proposed headings, brief capsule-answer previews, at least one table, an FAQ section, and suggested internal-link placements; edit anything that doesn't fit before proceeding.
8. Once satisfied, prompt 'give me the first draft' and add any proprietary detail you have that isn't publicly available elsewhere.
9. Edit the returned draft in the model's canvas/inline editor, manually inserting first-person experience sentences the model could not have generated.
10. Verify every capsule answers its heading immediately and that every numeric/factual claim links to a real, resolving source (spot-check at least one link).
11. Copy the final draft into WordPress, set a title containing the main keyword, generate a meta description via a follow-up prompt, and publish.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Claude, Datawise, WordPress

**Prompt / template:**

```text
In the prompt you fill in the topic, your audience, and the goals. Meta description follow-up, given verbatim: Can you please give me the meta description for this blog post? Make it less than 150 characters.
```

**Pitfall:** Copying the AI's first draft (including its chosen structure and cited sources) straight into WordPress without review — the creator stresses going through it with common sense and manually injecting real experience, since blind trust skips the one thing that differentiates the content.

### 23. Title a disruption story by naming the pain plus we fix it  `91.5`
*core · best practices · source 91*

Grow and Convert give three working title formulas, all provocative but literal. The first names the pain and promises the fix: Vocal Video's 'Producing Video Testimonials Used to Be a Huge Pain. Here's How We're Fixing It.' The second says everyone else is doing it wrong and we do it differently: Rainforest QA's 'Asking Developers to Own QA is Broken. Here's a Better Way.' The third states the contrarian position outright: Mirascope's 'Engineers Should Handle Prompting LLMs (and Prompts Should Live in Your Codebase)'. They note the third only works when the solution can be stated very clearly and simply, and fails when the solution needs explanation or when it is not obvious the approach is genuinely different. They rule out artsy phrasing and clever wordplay, because this is not a billboard. The audience must know exactly what you solve while still being compelled enough to open it.

> "it's best to make it provocative so it raises some eyebrows"

**Evidence:** Three shipped client titles for Vocal Video, Rainforest QA and Mirascope, plus LAWCLERK's 'Why the Large Law Firm Business Model Is Dying and What We're Doing Instead'.

**How to do it**

1. Draft one title in the form 'X used to be a huge pain. Here's how we're fixing it.'
2. Draft a second in the form 'Doing X the usual way is broken. Here's a better way.'
3. Draft a third that just states your contrarian position as a should statement.
4. Use the third form only if your solution fits in one clause and is obviously different from the norm.
5. Strip out puns, metaphors and clever wordplay from all three.
6. Check each title against a stranger in your target role: do they know what problem it addresses without reading further?
7. Pick the version that names the pain most bluntly while still leaving the how unanswered, so the click is required.
8. Reuse the winning title as the social post copy rather than writing separate ad copy.

**Pitfall:** Withholding the solution works; withholding the problem does not. A title that is provocative but vague gets scrolled past because the reader cannot tell whether it is about them.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau writes a positioning piece, title it on the pain, for example naming what is broken about running a practice on separate booking, notes and payment tools, then 'here's a better way'. Keep the Pabau product name out of the title so the pain does the work.

**Apply anywhere:** Title a positioning piece with one of three formulas: name the pain plus 'here's how we're fixing it', call the standard approach broken plus 'here's a better way', or state your contrarian position flat out. Skip wordplay; the reader must recognize the problem instantly.
