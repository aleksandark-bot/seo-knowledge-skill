### 1. Change the opening depending on whether you own the blog  `127.7`
*useful · best practices · source 127*

In Step 4 of the formula, Grow and Convert split the recommendation by publication. If you are writing for your own blog, consider starting with a personal story, which they classify as originality. If you are writing for another blog, state the exact specific angle the post is taking instead. The logic follows from what each audience has agreed to. Readers on your own site have some tolerance for your experience and your history, and a first-person story is a form of originality nobody else can copy. Readers on a third-party site have no relationship with you, so a story reads as self-indulgence, and the fastest way to earn the read is to be blunt about the specific position the piece is taking. Same formula, different execution at the last step.

> "If you're writing for another blog, state the exact specific angle"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's Step 4 explicitly splits the advice: personal story for your own blog, exact specific angle for another blog.

**How to do it**

1. Note before drafting whether the piece runs on your own domain or a third-party site.
2. On your own domain, draft a first-person opener built on a real event, number or decision from your own work.
3. Check that the personal story contains a fact or figure a competitor could not have written.
4. On a guest post, drop the story and open by stating the specific position the article takes in one sentence.
5. Verify the guest-post opener matches the angle you pitched the editor, so it survives their edit.
6. Keep both openers on file when you are unsure, and let the host editor pick.
7. Never reuse the same personal-story opener across two publications.

**Pitfall:** A personal-story opener on someone else's blog reads as promotional filler and is the first thing an editor cuts. It also wastes the position where the specific angle should be, since a cold audience has no reason to care about your history.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog articles can open with a real practice's situation or a figure from Pabau's own data. Any guest article or contributed piece on an aesthetics-industry site should skip that and open by naming its specific claim.

**Apply anywhere:** On your own blog, open with a personal story built on real events or numbers, since nobody can copy it. On a guest post, cut the story and state the specific angle the piece takes in the first sentence, because a cold audience has no reason to care about your history yet.

### 2. Choose freelancer, in-house or agency by coordination capacity, not cost  `164.15`
*useful · general insights · source 164*

Grow and Convert compare the three routes for hiring blog writers. Freelancers are the cheapest and let you flex capacity, and they recommend starting there if you have never hired writers, but the catch is you need someone on your team to coordinate the work and give editing feedback, which can itself be outsourced. In-house writers suit larger companies with resources: they become subject matter experts, are available on demand, and may grow into a content marketer or SEO role, but salary plus benefits plus payroll taxes and other overhead can reach double a freelance writer's base cost depending on location. They add that subject matter expertise does not require an employee, because contractors who run in-depth client interviews develop it too. Agencies suit companies that want the whole content and SEO operation, not just drafting.

> "as much as double the base salary of a freelance writer"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert put in-house writer total cost at up to double a freelance writer's base salary once benefits, payroll taxes and overhead are counted.

**How to do it**

1. Ask first who on your team will coordinate writers and give editing feedback each week.
2. If nobody can, either budget a freelance editor or hire an agency rather than a lone freelancer.
3. Start with freelancers if you have never hired writers, so you learn your own standard cheaply.
4. Cost an in-house writer at up to double the base salary once benefits, payroll taxes and overhead are added.
5. Replace the in-house expertise argument with an interview process if that is the only reason for the role.
6. Choose an agency only when you want keyword choice, interviews, quality control and measurement included.

**Pitfall:** Hiring freelancers with nobody to coordinate them. The cost saving evaporates because unedited freelance drafts either get published as-is or pile up unpublished.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's model already has David coordinating and editing, so freelancers plus supplied interviews is the right shape. The constraint to watch is his editing capacity, not the per-article rate.

**Apply anywhere:** Pick your writer model by who will coordinate and edit, not by headline cost. Freelancers are cheapest but need an editor, in-house costs up to double base salary once overhead is counted, and an agency makes sense only when you want strategy included.

### 3. Conceptualize customer stories first and attach keywords afterwards  `137.6`
*useful · best practices · source 137*

Grow and Convert produced patient stories for Cognitive FX alongside the SEO pieces, and they are explicit about the order of operations: the story is conceptualized first, then a keyword is attached if a suitable one exists. Telling a great story takes precedence over what search engines want. These pieces are not SEO motivated, so ranking is not essential, but some rank anyway. Their story about a former figure skater whose 14 years of unexplained illness turned out to be concussion-related now ranks first for 'figure skating concussion', a low-volume term nobody would have picked from a keyword tool. The stories earn their place three other ways: emotional connection that helps buyers remember and decide, strong performance on paid and organic social, and a brand story about changing lives.

> "So telling a great story takes precedence over what search engines want."

**Evidence:** The Cognitive FX figure-skater story ranks number one for 'figure skating concussion' and brings leads from both paid and organic Facebook, despite not being SEO motivated.

**How to do it**

1. Collect candidate customer stories from the service team based on how dramatic the before-and-after is, not on keyword fit.
2. Interview the customer and write the story to be genuinely good, with the specific details of their life before and after.
3. Only after the draft exists, search for a low-competition keyword that describes the person or their situation, such as their sport or profession plus the condition.
4. Attach the keyword through the title and one heading, and stop there; do not restructure the story around it.
5. Publish and promote through paid and organic social, since emotional stories get better CPCs and more shares than utilitarian posts.
6. Measure these on assisted and social conversions, not on organic rankings.
7. Do not commission a story if no genuine transformation exists; a manufactured one reads as marketing.

**Pitfall:** Forcing a keyword onto a story before it is written flattens the story into another utilitarian post and loses the emotional value that justified writing it. If the draft reads like a landing page with a name in it, the order was wrong.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish clinic-owner stories on /blog/ written story-first, then attach a narrow keyword such as the practice type plus the problem solved, and push them through paid social rather than judging them on rankings.

**Apply anywhere:** Write customer stories for their own sake first, then attach a low-volume keyword that matches the person or their situation. Judge them on emotional impact and social performance, not on rankings.

### 4. Consolidate best accounts into a buyer profile by title, revenue and industry  `122.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 122*

After the session, Grow and Convert compile the list of named companies and do demographic research to find who the actual buyer inside each was and what those buyers share. They look for whether the buyers had similar titles, whether companies that came up repeatedly sat in similar revenue ranges or employee counts, and which industries they were in. The output is a narrowing: a long list of potential customers becomes a small example list of ideal customers. They describe this as a key milestone, the point at which a content strategy can target a specific group rather than anyone in the field. Crucially they stop here and refuse to start content, because pain point research still has to happen.

> "Did the buyers have similar titles?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this as the milestone where a strategy stops being scattershot, and explicitly say content creation cannot start yet.

**How to do it**

1. List every company named in the session and in the CRM top band in one sheet.
2. For each, identify the individual who actually signed, not the company, and record their job title.
3. Add revenue range, employee count and industry for each company from LinkedIn or a data provider.
4. Look for repeats: titles that appear more than twice, revenue bands that cluster, industries that dominate.
5. Flag companies that were named by more than one department as strongest signal.
6. Write the shared attributes into a one-paragraph best-customer profile with the example company list attached.
7. Do not start topic ideation yet; go to pain point research next.

**Pitfall:** Treating the demographic profile as the finished brief. It tells you who to write for but not what to write, and content built on demographics alone still misses the pain point.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau this means naming the actual signer at a best-fit clinic, usually the owner or practice manager, and their clinic size band. Template pages should then be written at that person's level of knowledge.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn the best-account list into a profile by finding the individual buyer in each, then looking for shared titles, revenue bands, headcounts and industries.

### 5. Convert an explanatory paragraph into a scannable list  `72.17`
*useful · concrete actions · source 72*

Faced with an intro paragraph that named three categories then explained each in prose, Khanal's instinct is to break it up. His stated reason is that in today's age nobody wants to read long stuff, and his rule is that if you say there are three buckets, just explain the buckets. He cuts the connective sentence between the setup and the list, in this case 'buying from the wrong category is the most common mistake we see', calling it unnecessary. The result is the claim, then a scannable list of the three items, each with a short clause on what it is built for. This sits alongside his tolerance for casual prose: informality is fine, but a list of three things should look like a list.

> "Can't we just make this scannable?"

**Evidence:** Khanal converted a three-category intro paragraph into a scannable list on air and deleted the connective sentence about buying from the wrong category.

**How to do it**

1. Find paragraphs that announce a number of items and then explain them in continuous prose.
2. Cut the linking sentence between the announcement and the items, especially if it states a consequence.
3. Break each item onto its own line with a short clause naming what it is for.
4. Keep each item's description to one clause; move any elaboration into the body sections.
5. Check the surrounding prose still reads naturally without the removed connective sentence.
6. Leave genuinely argumentative paragraphs as prose rather than listing everything by default.

**Pitfall:** Listing everything. Khanal only converts the enumerated passage; the argument paragraphs around it stay as prose, and turning those into bullets would strip the reasoning.

**Apply at Pabau:** In the Pabau editorial pass, look for paragraphs that announce a count then explain in prose, and convert only those to lists. This pairs with the existing capsule-answer approach without turning articles into bullet dumps.

**Apply anywhere:** Convert paragraphs that announce a count and then explain in prose into scannable lists, cutting the connective sentence, but leave argumentative paragraphs as prose.

### 6. Cost an in-house writer at up to double a freelancer before hiring one  `167.17`
*useful · general insights · source 167*

Grow and Convert lay out the three ways to get blog content and the trade-off in each. An in-house writer suits a larger company with resources: they become a subject-matter expert and are available whenever you need them, though Grow and Convert note they get that expertise through client interviews instead. The disadvantage is salary plus benefits plus payroll taxes and other employee overhead, which depending on location can reach double the base salary of a freelance writer. Freelancers are the cheapest option and flex with demand, and they recommend starting there if you have never hired writers. The catch is that someone must coordinate the work and give editing feedback, though that coordination can itself be freelanced. The third option, an agency, is for companies that actually want content as a lead channel rather than just words.

> "salary + benefits + payroll taxes"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert put employee overhead at as much as double the base salary of a freelance writer, depending on location.

**How to do it**

1. Cost an in-house writer at base salary plus benefits, payroll taxes and overhead, up to roughly double a freelancer's cost in some locations.
2. Start with freelancers if you have never hired writers before, so you learn your own standards cheaply.
3. Name who will coordinate freelance work and give editing feedback before commissioning anyone.
4. If nobody internal can do that coordination, hire a freelance editor or strategist for it rather than skipping it.
5. Only consider an in-house hire once volume is steady enough that a salaried writer is fully used.
6. Choose an agency when what you actually need is keyword selection, interviews, promotion and measurement, not drafting.
7. Re-run the comparison annually as volume changes rather than treating the first decision as permanent.

**Pitfall:** Comparing an in-house salary directly against a per-article freelance rate. The employer overhead can double the real cost, and an under-utilized in-house writer is the most expensive content you will buy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep drafting freelance or in-house-with-AI while volume is variable, and treat the coordinating and editing role, which David currently holds, as the position to protect.

**Apply anywhere:** Cost an in-house writer at up to double a freelancer once overhead is counted. Start freelance, and make sure someone owns coordination and editing before you commission anything.

### 7. Cover the seven content types LLMs need to recommend you  `78.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 78*

Grow and Convert give an explicit checklist of what a brand must publish for LLMs to recommend it in the right scenarios, and contrast it with generic 'what is project management' content. The seven items are: what types of customer your product is best for, what specific use cases and scenarios it handles, what pain points it solves and for whom, the features that matter and when they matter, the benefits and outcomes customers experience, how you compare with competitors and what makes you different, and case studies proving delivered results. This is a coverage audit you can run against an existing site in an afternoon, which makes it more immediately usable than the topic map itself.

> "You need to publish specific content that covers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list these seven as what LLMs need to recommend a product, against generic top-of-funnel content.

**How to do it**

1. List your live commercial URLs in a sheet with one column per checklist item.
2. Mark which of the seven each page actually covers with specific detail, not a passing mention.
3. Flag customer types and use cases with zero dedicated coverage as the first gap to fill.
4. Write a comparison page for each named competitor that states the specific difference, not a feature grid.
5. Add a 'when this matters' sentence to each feature description so the model knows the trigger scenario.
6. Attach an outcome with a figure to every benefit claim.
7. Publish at least one case study proving results for each major customer type.
8. Re-run the audit each quarter as the product and competitor set change.

**Pitfall:** Covering all seven at a shallow level across one long page. The model needs a specific page it can cite for a specific situation, and a single omnibus page gives it nothing to match.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should audit pabau.com's commercial pages against these seven and fill the gaps: practice types Pabau suits best, named competitor comparisons, and outcome figures on every benefit claim.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your commercial pages against seven items: best-fit customer types, use cases, pain points, features with their trigger scenarios, outcomes, competitor comparisons and case studies. Fill the gaps one dedicated page at a time.

### 8. Define the content engagement as eight delivered stages, not recommendations  `181.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 181*

Grow and Convert draw a line between deliverables and finished work. They list the eight stages they own end to end: keyword research, SERP analysis, expert interviews, writing and optimization, publishing to the client's CMS, link building, and conversion tracking. Their argument is that a strategy document or a brief still requires project management and execution on the client side, and that is where most programs stall. The list doubles as a scope checklist for anyone commissioning or running content in-house: if nobody owns publishing, nobody owns links, and nobody owns conversion tracking, the articles get written and then sit. Note the ordering, which puts SERP analysis before the interview, so the interview questions are shaped by what already ranks.

> "keyword research, SERP analysis, expert interviews, writing and optimization, publishing to your CMS, link"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this eight-stage scope as what separates finished published articles from deliverables that still need client project management.

**How to do it**

1. Write the eight stages down as a checklist and put a named owner against each one before the first article is commissioned.
2. Do keyword research first, then SERP analysis, so the interview questions target the gaps in what already ranks.
3. Run the expert interview only after the SERP read, and bring the gaps into the question list.
4. Keep writing and on-page optimization in the same hands so the keyword placement is not bolted on later.
5. Assign publishing into the CMS to the same owner as the draft, so no article waits on a handover.
6. Schedule link building as a stage that starts after publication, targeted at the pages you intend to convert.
7. Set up conversion tracking before the first article goes live, not after the first quarterly review.
8. Review the checklist quarterly and flag any stage with no owner as the reason the program is not producing.

**Pitfall:** Programs that stop at recommendations produce briefs that never become published, linked, tracked pages. The signal is a backlog of approved outlines and a flat publish rate.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the two stages most likely to have no owner are link building and per-article conversion tracking. David should name an owner for each before the next batch of blog and template pages is commissioned.

**Apply anywhere:** Put a named owner against all eight stages (keyword research, SERP analysis, interview, writing, on-page, publishing, links, conversion tracking) before commissioning content; the unowned stages are where the program stalls.

### 9. Delay outsourcing content until one pitch is proven to close  `160.5`
*useful · best practices · source 160*

Grow and Convert warn early-stage companies about delegating content to freelancers or agencies, and they place the blame on the client rather than the vendor. If positioning and messaging are not defined, the founder is not clear on the subtleties either, so anything an outside party writes will feel like it needs work. The problem is worst for products with multiple value propositions or multiple audiences, where which features to mention and which benefits to highlight get debated endlessly for every piece. In extreme cases they could not even agree with the client on topics. Their marker for readiness is blunt: companies with established sales channels know which pitch works and just want more people to present it to.

> "they know which pitch works, they just want content to bring them more customers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say this pattern has never been a problem with companies that have established sales channels and proven cold-audience sales.

**How to do it**

1. Before briefing an agency, write one page naming the single audience, the single primary value proposition, and the pitch that closes them.
2. Show that page to two people who have closed deals and confirm they agree with it.
3. If they disagree, keep content in-house until the disagreement is settled.
4. If your product has several value propositions or audiences, pick one for the first content engagement rather than covering all of them.
5. Budget for heavy editing on the first few outsourced pieces regardless, and treat repeated rewrites as a messaging signal rather than a vendor failure.
6. Only scale the outsourced volume after two consecutive articles need light edits.

**Pitfall:** Cycling through agencies because the drafts never feel right. The tell is arguing internally about which features and benefits belong in each article, and going back and forth even on the topic list.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has a settled pitch, so outsourcing writing is safe. But for a newly launched module or a move upmarket, David should keep the first few articles in-house until one page's messaging is agreed, then hand the pattern to writers.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not outsource content until you can write down one audience and one pitch that people who sell your product agree closes deals. Otherwise every draft reads wrong, and the cause is undefined messaging rather than the writer.

### 10. Delete the generic sections AI appends after the main list  `72.24`
*useful · concrete actions · source 72*

Khanal notes his tool tends to add sections at the bottom that he does not need, though some users like them. The specific example is a closing section on how to get genuinely good copy, whose first advice was to feed the tool as much business context as possible. He lets it generate so he can see it, then plans to delete it in the draft. His stated view is that after the list of tools, you do not need additional sections. The same instinct appears when he cuts a trailing qualifier from a heading, 'what to actually look for beyond templates and pricing', saying AI always wants to add a little bit extra after the point is made. The pattern is AI extending past the article's job.

> "wave tends to include some stuff at the bottom that I don't need"

**Evidence:** Khanal generated the closing section deliberately so he could evaluate it, then judged it unnecessary and planned to delete it from the draft.

**How to do it**

1. Generate the full draft including the optional closing sections so you can judge them.
2. Identify sections that appear after the article's main deliverable, such as generic how-to-do-it-well advice.
3. Ask whether the section serves the searcher's original intent or only adds length.
4. Delete generic closers that restate advice available on every competing page.
5. Trim trailing qualifier clauses from headings where the point is already made.
6. Keep a closing section only when it answers a question the body genuinely leaves open.
7. Check the page still closes with a clear next step for the reader after the deletions.

**Pitfall:** Keeping the closer because it looks substantive. Khanal's signal is that the section's advice is generic enough to appear on every competing page, which means it adds words and no differentiation.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a generated Pabau draft ends with generic best-practice sections after the main list, cut them. The article's required closing structure is the Pabau section, the CTA block and the Conclusion, not an appended advice section.

**Apply anywhere:** Delete the generic advice sections AI appends after your article's main deliverable, and trim trailing qualifier clauses from headings where the point is already made.

### 11. Do not add an FAQ just because everyone says AI needs one  `71.12`
*useful · best practices · source 71*

Reviewing the proposed outline, Devesh reached the FAQ section and rejected it flatly. His stated reason is that everyone just says you need an FAQ because of AI, but they do not actually know. He is pointing at a cargo-culted practice: the advice to bolt a question block onto every article to win AI Overview and LLM citations has spread faster than any evidence for it, and people repeat it without being able to say what mechanism it serves. The wider principle in his session is that every section has to earn its place by adding something the piece does not already say. An FAQ that restates the body fails that test regardless of what it is supposed to do for AI answers.

> "everyone just says you need an FAQ because of AI"

**Evidence:** Devesh removed the FAQ from his outline for 'AI content brief generator', saying people recommend it because of AI without actually knowing why.

**How to do it**

1. When an outline proposes an FAQ, ask what specific question the body does not already answer.
2. Keep the FAQ only if it holds at least three real questions with answers found nowhere else in the article.
3. Source those questions from actual searches, People Also Ask entries or customer messages, not from the model's invention.
4. Cut the FAQ entirely when every question restates a heading already in the piece.
5. Do not justify an FAQ on the grounds that it helps AI answers unless you can name the mechanism and have tested it.
6. If you keep an FAQ for schema reasons, still require each answer to be new information.

**Pitfall:** An FAQ that paraphrases the article adds length without information and gives the reader a second, thinner version of what they just read. The signal is questions whose answers are lifted from headings above.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's Yoast FAQ block should carry questions that appear nowhere else in the article, drawn from real practice-manager queries. If the proposed questions restate H2s, David should drop the block rather than pad it.

**Apply anywhere:** Only include an FAQ when it holds real questions the article does not already answer. Adding one because it supposedly helps AI answers, with no mechanism you can name, just duplicates your own content.

### 12. Do not expect an industry-expert writer to fix your voice problem  `126.10`
*useful · content insights · source 126*

Grow and Convert address the obvious objection head on: can you not just hire a writer who already has industry expertise? Their answer is that it beats hiring any writer but does not solve the problem, for two reasons. Outside experts have industry knowledge but do not think about the nuances of your space the way you do, so you end up with a blog full of their ideas rather than yours and the company has no voice of its own. They test it on themselves: would this blog written by someone else in content marketing have the same voice and ideas, and what would Backlinko be with some writer with SEO expertise instead of Brian Dean? Second, an outside industry expert does not have deep knowledge of your product, and the highest-converting topics are the ones closest to the product, so they will not know or care enough to position your solution properly.

> "you'll get a blog full"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use the Backlinko-without-Brian-Dean thought experiment to make the voice point.

**How to do it**

1. Decide whether the gap you are hiring for is writing skill, industry knowledge or product knowledge.
2. Accept that only the first two are hireable from outside.
3. Hire for writing and interviewing skill, then supply industry and product knowledge internally.
4. Write down the three or four positions your company holds that competitors do not, and give them to every writer.
5. Have the internal expert, not the writer, own how the product is positioned in each piece.
6. Review a hired industry expert's first two pieces specifically for whose opinions they carry.

**Pitfall:** Paying a premium for an industry-expert writer and still getting undifferentiated positioning, because the missing ingredient was product knowledge, not domain knowledge.

**Apply at Pabau:** Hiring an aesthetics-industry writer will not by itself make Pabau content sound like Pabau. David should write down Pabau's distinctive positions once, including all-features-in-every-subscription and structured onboarding rather than a free trial, and hand that to every writer alongside the interview transcript.

**Apply anywhere:** An industry-expert writer buys you domain fluency, not your company's voice or product knowledge. Write your distinctive positions down and keep product positioning with an internal owner, whoever writes the words.

### 13. Do not hire beginner writers just because the audience is beginners  `112.15`
*useful · best practices · source 112*

Grow and Convert identify a specific B2C reasoning error. B2B marketers generally know they sell to a technical audience and so accept they need advanced content, even if many still ship white papers that are too beginner. Consumer brands make the opposite mistake: because their customers are largely beginners in the subject, they conclude any writer can produce the content. Grow and Convert say this leads directly to uncompelling, undifferentiated content that does not reflect well on the brand and does not rank for the strategic keywords that drive business value. Their illustration is the basic '10 tips for runners' post built from cliches about stretching and consistency. The distinction they are drawing is between the reader's expertise and the writer's: a beginner reader still needs a writer who knows more than they do.

> "they can hire any writer to produce their content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contrast B2B awareness of technical audiences with B2C brands hiring any writer, and cite the cliche '10 tips for runners' post as the output.

**How to do it**

1. Judge writers on subject knowledge and access to practitioners, not on whether the audience is expert.
2. Ask every candidate for a piece containing something a search-only writer could not have produced.
3. Give writers a brief that names the specific product attribute or pain point the piece has to address.
4. Require at least one original element per article, an interview quote, a first-hand test, or proprietary data.
5. Reject drafts whose advice the target reader has already heard many times, regardless of how well written.
6. Route strategic purchase-intent keywords to your strongest writers, not to whoever has capacity.

**Pitfall:** The failure shows up as clean, readable articles that rank for nothing strategic and give the brand nothing to be known for. Word count and grammar hide it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's readers are practice owners and clinicians, so a writer without clinic operations knowledge produces exactly this failure. David should keep strategic pages with writers who can pull real workflow detail, and use the originality-nugget requirement as the hiring test.

**Apply anywhere:** A beginner audience does not license a beginner writer. Hire for subject knowledge and access, and require every article to contain something a writer with only a search engine could not have produced.

### 14. Do not hire outside industry experts to supply your point of view  `108.9`
*useful · best practices · source 108*

Grow and Convert name a failure mode distinct from the usual warning about freelance writers. Companies that recognize their writers lack product knowledge often respond by hiring writers with industry expertise instead. Grow and Convert say that does not fix the problem. An outside expert has general industry knowledge but does not think about the nuances of your space the way you do. Rely on their perspective and you end up with a blog full of their ideas rather than yours, and the company never develops a distinctive voice or point of view. Their alternative is not to hire differently but to interview: before writing, get on a call with the founder, product manager, sales lead or customer success manager who owns the topic, and ask about the problem, how the product solves it, what makes the approach different, and which capabilities matter most. The expertise comes from inside; the writing skill comes from outside.

> "you'll end up with a blog full of their ideas, not yours"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's agency practice of interviewing a client-side expert before every piece, contrasted with what they call Google Research Papers.

**How to do it**

1. Stop screening writers for industry credentials and screen them for interviewing and structuring ability instead.
2. For each article, name the internal owner of the topic: founder, product manager, sales lead or customer success manager.
3. Book a call before any drafting, not after a first draft exists.
4. Ask four fixed questions: what the problem is, how the product solves it, what makes the approach different, and which capabilities matter most here.
5. Record the call and keep the transcript attached to the brief.
6. Require the draft to carry at least three specifics that could only have come from that call.
7. Have the interviewee review the product section for accuracy before publication.
8. Reuse the same internal expert across the whole keyword cluster so the point of view stays consistent.

**Pitfall:** An industry-expert byline produces confident, credentialed content that still says what everyone else in the industry says. The signal is that nothing in the article would have to change if a competitor published it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not solve thin clinical content by commissioning aesthetics practitioners to write it. Interview Pabau's own product, sales and support people, and use outside writers only to structure what those interviews produce.

**Apply anywhere:** Hiring writers with industry expertise does not fix generic content; it fills the blog with their views instead of yours. Interview your own founders, product managers and customer-facing staff, and use writers to structure what they say.

### 15. Do not invest in meta descriptions Google will rewrite anyway  `74.23`
*useful · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid reaches the starter guide's section on controlling your snippet and notes it has not been updated in a long time, because Google overwrites your meta description. He points to a whole video and post on the mechanics of snippet display: the letter W takes up about three times the space of a capital I or a lowercase L, so character counts are unreliable, and Google has a way of working out the user's screen and the font in use. That makes writing to a fixed character limit a false precision. His practical framing is that the parts of the snippet you can genuinely influence are the title, the breadcrumb path and anchor-based sublinks, which he demonstrates elsewhere in the walkthrough.

> "Google overwrites your meta description"

**Evidence:** David: the letter W takes roughly three times the space of a capital I or lowercase L, and Google works out the user's screen and font.

**How to do it**

1. Write one clear meta description per page and move on; do not iterate on it repeatedly.
2. Stop tuning to an exact character count, since pixel width varies by letter, font and screen.
3. Put your effort into the title tag, which Google rewrites less often and which carries the query match.
4. Set breadcrumbs so the snippet path tells the reader what type of page they are getting.
5. Add a linkable table of contents so Google can pull anchor sublinks into the result.
6. Check the live SERP for your important pages to see what Google is actually showing, rather than assuming your description ran.

**Pitfall:** Auditing thousands of meta descriptions against a character limit. Google rewrites most of them, and the pixel width of the text varies enough that the count was never the right measure.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep writing one meta description per article and stop treating length warnings from SEO plugins as findings. David should shift that review time to the SERP title, which is where the guides already put the emphasis.

**Apply anywhere:** Write one meta description per page, ignore character-count warnings, and spend the review time on the title tag and breadcrumb path instead.

### 16. Do not publish news rewrites without a first-hand source or audience-specific impact  `66.26`
*useful · best practices · source 66*

Content that repeats an announcement or another publisher's reporting without adding a first-hand source, new evidence, analysis or audience-specific impact. It becomes stale quickly and gives search or AI systems no reason to cite it or send users to the rewrite. It covers press-release rewrites, recaps copied from another source and summaries of third-party announcements with no original reporting, evidence or analysis. It scores Low on all five value dimensions at Medium effort. The type is worth naming separately from rehashed explainers because it has a distinct trap: it feels urgent and productive, so it consumes the newsroom capacity that first-hand reporting needs, and it decays to zero value within days. The four listed additions are the exit route, and the lightest of them — audience-specific impact — is often within reach: what this announcement actually changes for your specific readers, which the original announcement did not say and a general publisher would not know.

> "repeats an announcement or another publisher's reporting without adding a first-hand source"

**How to do it**

1. Before covering an announcement, decide which of the four additions you can make: a first-hand source, new evidence, original analysis, or audience-specific impact.
2. If the answer is none, link to the original from a newsletter or social post and do not publish a page.
3. Where the addition is audience-specific impact, lead with what changes for your readers — the cost, the deadline, the workflow, the compliance obligation — and treat the announcement itself as background.
4. Where you can get a first-hand source, get a comment from someone directly affected or involved before publishing, which moves the piece into the prioritized original-reporting type.
5. Attribute the original reporting clearly and link to it, rather than restating it as your own.
6. Set a review date on anything news-shaped, since this type's defining weakness is decay, and either update it or retire it.
7. Audit the existing archive for rewrites with no traffic and consolidate or remove them, since a large stale news archive dilutes the site's overall quality signals.
8. Reallocate the freed capacity to the original-reporting type, which shares the same team and scores High where this scores Low.

**Pitfall:** Believing speed is the differentiator. Being first with a rewrite earns a few days of traffic and a permanent stale page, while the syndicated original and the AI summary both satisfy the query — the framework scores the rewrite Low regardless of how fast it went up.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau shouldn't publish a page that just relays an industry or regulatory announcement. Either get a first-hand source, or lead with what it specifically changes for practices — the deadline, the cost, the workflow — and if neither is available, share the original instead of publishing.

**Apply anywhere:** Don't publish a page that just relays an industry or regulatory announcement. Either get a first-hand source, or lead with what it specifically changes for your audience — the deadline, the cost, the workflow — and if neither is available, share the original instead of publishing.

### 17. Drop 'best' from the title when the SERP overlaps without it  `72.21`
*useful · best practices · source 72*

Khanal targets the keyword 'best ai copywriting tools' because that is what the keyword tool returned, then questions whether the title needs the word. His reasoning: if you go after 'ai copywriting tools' you will rank for 'best' too, because the SERP for the term with and without 'best' overlaps significantly. That frees the title to say what is actually inside the post. He rejects the AI's suggestions that were too presumptive, such as questions every roundup skips, while keeping the one word he liked from them.

> "if you just go after AI copywriting tools you'll rank for best"

**Evidence:** Khanal judged the SERPs for 'ai copywriting tools' and 'best ai copywriting tools' to overlap significantly, and deferred the title decision until after the draft existed.

**How to do it**

1. Check whether the SERPs for your keyword with and without the 'best' modifier substantially overlap.
2. If they do, treat 'best' as optional in the title and spend the characters on what the post actually contains.
3. Draft several titles that describe the post's real angle rather than restating the keyword.
4. Reject any title that makes a presumptive claim you have not earned, such as what every competitor skips.
5. Keep the single strongest word from the rejected options and graft it into the winner.
6. Confirm the front of the title still carries the head term for the query you are targeting.
7. Leave the final title decision until after the body is written, since the angle often shifts during editing.

**Pitfall:** Assuming the modifier is required because the keyword tool returned it. Khanal notes the keyword came back as 'best' purely because of how the research tool reported it.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau comparison pages, check whether the modified and unmodified SERPs overlap before spending title characters on 'best'. If they do, use the space to state the article's actual angle.

**Apply anywhere:** Check whether the SERPs with and without the 'best' modifier overlap, and if they do, spend those title characters describing the post's real angle instead.

### 18. Expect a lightly briefed freelance writer to miss every real value prop  `92.16`
*useful · content insights · source 92*

Grow and Convert close by stating plainly what a typical freelance writer given minimal info will not do. > "A typical freelance writer just given minimal info isn't going to stumble"

3. Add a do not emphasize list naming the value props competitors over-use.
4. Add the criteria section outline so the writer inherits the ordering rather than inventing it.
5. If you cannot supply pain points, budget writer time for two customer-facing interviews instead.
6. Review the first draft against the brief's pain point list before reviewing the prose.

**Pitfall:** Blaming the writer for shallow copy when the brief supplied nothing to be deep about. The tell is a draft that names real features in the wrong order and pads the gaps.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's brief template should carry a ranked pain point list and a do-not-emphasize list before any writer starts. Without them the draft will lead on feature counts and integrations, which is what every competitor already does.

### 21. Expect mirage content whenever a writer has no expert input  `167.19`
*useful · general insights · source 167*

Grow and Convert name the output pattern that all five of their hiring mistakes converge on: mirage content, meaning fluff produced by a writer googling a topic and more or less regurgitating what they read into their own article. ### 22. Expect the blog to lag and bridge the gap with borrowed audiences  `173.13`
