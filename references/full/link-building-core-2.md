### 1. In ultra-niche industries, perfect relevance becomes the bottleneck  `32.4`
*core · general insights · source 32*

The standard link-building rule that relevance matters above all can become counterproductive in genuinely ultra-niche industries, because insisting on perfect topical relevance means spending excessive time searching for a small handful of qualifying websites that may not exist in sufficient number. The reframed lesson is that relevance still matters, but in these specific cases perfect relevance itself becomes the constraint holding back the campaign, so the fix is to widen the target site pool to adjacent industries while keeping the actual content angle tight and believable, rather than abandoning relevance altogether.

> "perfect relevance can become a bottleneck in these ultra niche industries"

**Evidence:** The petrochemical-startup case study: widening from chemical-manufacturing-only sites to broader engineering and industrial sites (while keeping content tightly focused) is what unblocked the campaign and ultimately produced the ranking win.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau's link-building or digital PR efforts stall in a narrow sub-vertical (e.g., a specific procedure or niche clinic specialty), recognize the bottleneck may be an over-strict relevance filter rather than a lack of link opportunities — consider widening to adjacent healthcare-business, wellness, or practice-management-adjacent sites while keeping the actual contributed content tightly on-topic and credible.

**Apply anywhere:** When your link-building or digital PR efforts stall in a narrow sub-vertical, recognize the bottleneck may be an over-strict relevance filter rather than a lack of link opportunities — consider widening to adjacent industry, professional, or lifestyle sites while keeping the actual contributed content tightly on-topic and credible.

### 2. Judge a link by relevancy, consensus, and positioning, not DR alone  `10.2`
*core · best practices · source 10*

When judging whether a prospective backlink is worth pursuing, first check what it's pointing at — the same link can be toxic to a brand-new site or a rocket booster on an authoritative page (e.g., a parasite page on Forbes), so root-domain trust matters as much as the individual placement. Then check niche relevancy at the page level only (the specific linking page should be topically close to yours) — domain-level relevancy isn't required for the page-level check but does count heavily toward "link-profile consensus," i.e., whether the linking domain belongs to Google's trusted cluster of domains that already power your known competitors in that niche. Finally weigh the link's ability to pass trust/authority signal and its "positioning and clickworthiness" — Google's algorithms increasingly evaluate where on the page a link sits, the surrounding context, and whether it looks editorially placed versus stuffed in. Build a simple three-check framework for every prospective link: what it's pointing at and that root's trust level, whether the linking page/domain is niche-relevant and part of your niche's consensus set, and whether it's positioned naturally.

> "niche relevancy only needs to exist at a page level"

**How to do it**

1. Before pursuing any link, identify the exact page and domain it will point to, not just the domain's overall DR/DA metric.
2. Check the root domain's existing trust level — a link from a brand-new or low-trust domain behaves differently than the same link on a page on a high-authority site like Forbes.
3. Confirm the specific linking page is niche-relevant to your target page's topic (page-level relevancy, not domain-level).
4. Pull your top 3-5 competitors' backlink profiles (e.g., in Ahrefs) and check whether the prospective linking domain already appears there — domains that already power multiple competitors count as trusted "consensus" domains for the niche.
5. Evaluate where the link sits on the page (above the fold vs. buried footer, editorial context vs. sidebar list) by viewing the live page manually, since positioning and clickworthiness are algorithmically weighted (inferred manual verification method).
6. Set your own quality threshold based on your specific campaign's goals rather than applying one universal DR/DA cutoff, since a parasite-page campaign and a brand root-domain campaign need different thresholds.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** A single link can be toxic on a brand-new site but rocket an identical page on Forbes to number one — the same link is not equally good everywhere, so root-domain trust must be checked link by link.

### 3. Judge a link program by the referring domain, not links per month  `107.11`
*core · best practices · source 107*

Grow and Convert name link building as where most B2B companies waste the majority of their SEO budget. The product most agencies sell is volume-driven outreach for guest posts and niche edits on whatever sites will accept them, which yields dozens of low-relevance backlinks from sites with no audience overlap with the client. They say those links do not move rankings and Google has gotten much better at discounting them. What works is high-relevance placement on sites real buyers in the industry already read. That takes longer and costs more per link, but the links compound. Their evaluation question for any program, in-house or agency, is deliberately not how many links per month. It is what the typical referring domain looks like and whether your buyers would recognize the publication.

> "whether your buyers would recognize the publication"

**How to do it**

1. Ask any prospective agency for the last 20 live placements they built for a similar client, with URLs.
2. Show that domain list to two of your own salespeople and ask which publications their buyers read.
3. Reject the program if fewer than half are recognized, regardless of the DR figures attached.
4. Rewrite the contract to specify referring-domain criteria rather than a monthly link count.
5. For in-house work, build a target list of the publications, newsletters and communities your buyers actually read, and pitch only those.
6. Accept a lower monthly link count and a higher cost per link as the correct trade.

**Pitfall:** Volume-based contracts create the incentive to buy whatever will accept a placement, so the reporting looks healthy while rankings do not move and the spend is unrecoverable.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's link targets should be aesthetics and healthcare practice publications, industry associations and clinic-owner communities, not general business blogs. David should apply the recognition test to any agency list before signing.

**Apply anywhere:** Evaluate link building by asking what the typical referring domain is and whether your buyers would recognize it, not by links per month. Accept fewer, more expensive, more relevant placements.

### 4. Keep building real backlinks so ranking-page growth doesn't outpace them  `51.5`
*core · best practices · source 51*

Edward's caveat to the whole PAA/self-authority strategy is that it cannot fully replace real backlink building — running this tactic alone will eventually hit a ceiling on how much self-authority a site can generate internally, and backlinks, especially ones that drive actual referral traffic, make the overall SEO profile look more natural to Google. He reframes the common industry concern about a 'natural backlink profile' as really being about a 'natural authority profile': since both your own ranking pages (via satisfied clicks) and your external backlinks generate authority, having too many internally-generated ranking pages appear too quickly without your backlink count scaling alongside them creates an imbalance that can get the site penalized by Google.

> "a lot of people talk about having a natural backlink profile"

**How to do it**

1. Continue running a parallel, ongoing link-building program (outreach, digital PR, guest content) even while actively publishing and internally linking PAA/FAQ pages.
2. Track two metrics side by side over time: the count/rate of new internally-ranking pages you're generating via this strategy, and the count/rate of new referring domains/backlinks you're earning.
3. If the rate of new ranking pages is significantly outpacing your backlink growth rate, slow down publishing new PAA pages and/or increase backlink-building effort to rebalance the two (inferred threshold-based rule).
4. Prioritize backlinks that also generate real referral traffic over purely link-equity placements, since Edward states these look most natural to Google.
5. Periodically review your overall authority profile, both self-generated page authority and backlink-based authority together, rather than only a traditional backlink-profile audit, when assessing spam risk (inferred).

**Pitfall:** Relying on this PAA/self-authority trick alone and letting ranking-page growth badly outpace backlink growth — Edward warns this imbalance, too many ranking pages appearing way too fast without backlinks scaling alongside them, is specifically what can get a site hit by Google.

### 5. Launch an affiliate program to raise your listicle inclusion hit rate  `108.16`
*core · concrete actions · source 108*

Grow and Convert give a mechanism for why listicle inclusion outreach usually fails and how to fix the incentive. Many of the listicles and resource pages ranking for 'best project management software' style terms are monetized through affiliate commissions. The site owner has no reason to add your product if there is nothing in it for them, and every reason to keep the products that already pay. Grow and Convert say having an affiliate marketing program significantly increases the success rate of inclusion requests, because you can offer the owner revenue when their readers click through and convert. It also improves retention on the listing, since the owner now has a reason to keep your entry accurate and current. The payoff is a link from a page that already ranks and already sends buyers, which is why they rate this alongside guest posting rather than below it.

> "Having an affiliate marketing program significantly increases your success rate"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's observation that most ranking software listicles are affiliate-monetized, and that offering commission materially raises inclusion success rates.

**How to do it**

1. Search your main category keywords and list every listicle and resource page in the top 20.
2. Check each page for affiliate disclosure or tracked outbound links to identify the monetized ones.
3. Note which competitors are already listed and in what order.
4. Stand up an affiliate program with a commission rate competitive with those competitors before any outreach.
5. Contact the author or site owner with the affiliate offer named in the first email, alongside accurate product details.
6. Supply a ready-to-paste entry: positioning line, key features, pricing and the use case you fit best.
7. Follow up when you ship a relevant feature, giving the owner a reason to refresh the listing.
8. Track referral traffic and conversions per listing so weak placements can be renegotiated or dropped.

**Pitfall:** Sending inclusion requests with no incentive to affiliate-monetized listicles. Response rates stay near zero and the team concludes outreach does not work, when the real problem is that the page earns money from the incumbents.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's inclusion in 'best clinic software' and 'best med spa software' roundups would improve if there were a commission on offer. Before the next outreach round, check whether Pabau can pay a referral commission, since most of those roundups are affiliate sites.

**Apply anywhere:** Most ranking software listicles run on affiliate revenue. Set up an affiliate program before you run inclusion outreach, name the commission in the first email, and supply a ready-to-paste entry so the owner only has to accept.

### 6. Let clear 'we do this' pages earn links passively over time  `17.4`
*core · best practices · source 17*

Rather than actively pursuing links, this tactic is to publish very clear, confidently-worded bottom-of-funnel landing pages that simply and plainly state "we do this" for exactly what people are searching for, and let links accrue passively. The mechanism: when someone else is later writing content that recommends a solution for that need, for example "this company can do this thing if you're looking for it," they do a Google search, find the page that confidently and unambiguously states it does the thing, and link to it as the citation, meaning the clarity and confidence of the existing page is what gets it chosen and linked, over vaguer competitor pages. The host connects this to a broader principle from another episode of his podcast, "You Don't Need 1,000 Backlinks to Rank on Google": if there's real search volume behind a keyword, people genuinely care about that topic, and content that directly and clearly addresses that search intent will earn backlinks over time simply by existing and being found, without an active outreach campaign for every link.

> "People are linking to us because these are common things that people"

**How to do it**

1. Identify the specific, concrete things your business does that people search for directly, bottom-of-funnel queries like "who does X" or "does anyone offer Y."
2. Write a dedicated landing page for each one stating plainly and confidently that you do this thing, avoiding vague or overly broad service descriptions.
3. Apply the same exact-match on-page placement, title, URL, H1, opening sentence, used elsewhere so the page is easy to find via search for that specific need.
4. Publish the page and leave it live and maintained rather than treating it as a one-off; do not expect to need to actively pitch it for links.
5. Periodically search for who is writing roundup or recommendation content in your space, such as "best tools for X" or "who offers Y," and confirm your page surfaces when they search for the exact thing you do.
6. Track referral traffic and new backlinks landing on these bottom-of-funnel pages over time as a passive-link-earning signal, separate from your active outreach link building (inferred measurement step).

### 7. Link building eats most new-site time: podcast guesting, parasite platforms  `23.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 23*

On a new site, Edward states link building will consume more of an SEO's time than any other activity, and the tactics go beyond simple outreach: he recommends starting a podcast specifically to invite small bloggers and niche figures as guests, since that relationship-building naturally produces backlinks and connections afterward. He pairs this with more conventional tactics (directory submissions, HARO-style journalist pitching) and, separately, developing 'parasite' properties on platforms like Medium or X, where ranking video content is currently the strongest parasite-SEO format — once these accounts build a following, their posts can rank for target keywords independently of the main domain.

> "is going to be doing link building"

**How to do it**

1. Identify 10-20 small blogs, podcasts, or niche creators in your industry who would be plausible podcast guests or collaborators.
2. Launch or use an existing podcast/content series specifically to invite these smaller niche voices as guests, prioritizing relationship-building over one-off transactional link requests.
3. Follow up with guests after their appearance to request a backlink or mention from their own site/newsletter, leveraging the relationship built during the interview.
4. In parallel, submit your site to relevant industry directories as a baseline link-building activity.
5. Monitor journalist request platforms daily and pitch relevant expert commentary to earn press mentions and links.
6. Set up secondary 'parasite' properties on platforms such as Medium and X, and post content targeting your money keywords on those accounts as well as your main site.
7. Prioritize video content specifically for parasite SEO, since Edward states ranking with video is currently the strongest parasite-SEO format.
8. Track which parasite properties/posts begin ranking for target keywords independently, and continue investing in whichever platform is producing results (inferred verification step).

**Tools:** Medium, X, HARO-style journalist request platforms

### 8. Manually vet a site's traffic, backlinks, and niche before buying a link  `10.6`
*core · best practices · source 10*

Before buying a link or guest-post placement from a marketplace or vendor, manually vet the individual site rather than trusting bulk DR/DA filters alone: check whether its traffic looks faked (suspicious traffic-graph spikes), whether its backlink profile looks manipulated, whether it's using redirect tricks to inflate its Domain Rating, and whether it's actually ranking in the SERPs for the keywords it claims relevance for. Critically, also check what else the site ranks for — Charles gives the real example of a marketplace listing that looked "niche-relevant, 4,000 to 20,000 DR" but was actually ranking for adult content, a bad-neighborhood association you don't want your brand linked from. At scale (enterprise budgets), replace manual vetting with a formal multi-stage pipeline combining AI, an MCP-connected Ahrefs, manual review, and VAs across every prospective domain, and use a tool like Link Research Tools to filter out sites above your toxicity threshold before they even reach human review.

> "see if it's faking traffic, see if it's faking backlink profiles"

**How to do it**

1. Pull the prospective linking site's traffic graph (via Ahrefs or similar) and check for unnatural spikes or patterns suggesting bought or bot traffic.
2. Check the site's own backlink profile for signs of manipulation or a purchased-link footprint.
3. Verify whether the site's Domain Rating is inflated via redirect tricks such as 301-ing old, high-authority expired domains into the site.
4. Search the site in a SERP tool to confirm it actually ranks for the keywords and niche it claims to be relevant for.
5. Check what other keywords and content categories the site ranks for, to rule out bad-neighborhood associations like adult, gambling, or spam content hiding behind a decent DR number.
6. For high-volume or enterprise link buying, set a toxicity threshold in Link Research Tools or an equivalent tool and auto-filter out domains above it before manual review.
7. For enterprise-scale programs, build a formal multi-stage due-diligence pipeline (Charles describes 10-12 stages) combining AI screening, Ahrefs/MCP data pulls, manual review, and VA execution for every domain before purchase.
8. Reject any site that fails traffic, backlink-profile, DR-integrity, or neighborhood checks regardless of how attractive its raw DR or niche label looks.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Link Research Tools, Ahrefs MCP

**Pitfall:** A marketplace listing can look perfect on paper (good DR, seemingly niche-relevant) while actually ranking for content like adult material — always check what else a prospective linking site ranks for; skipping this due diligence is how clients have wasted a million dollars on backlinks.

### 9. Mine Ahrefs Content Explorer + AI to find outlier link-worthy content  `10.5`
*core · ai workflows · source 10*

To find content and digital-PR ideas worth reverse-engineering, search Ahrefs' Content Explorer for niche-specific queries (Charles gives examples: SaaS tools, e-commerce niches, calculator tools, mortgage content, alphabet/glossary pages) and pull the full list of matching published content. Export data including each piece's publish date, referring-domain and link count, and social signal count, then feed that export to an AI model and ask it to identify the outliers — pieces that gained a disproportionate number of links or social shares very quickly after publishing relative to the rest of the set. Those AI-flagged outliers are the specific content formats and angles to reverse-engineer and rebuild for your own site, because Charles's core link-building principle is "copying what works": you measure the ROI competitors already proved rather than guessing at original content ideas.

> "ask the AI: which ones overperformed, which are the outliers"

**How to do it**

1. Open Ahrefs Content Explorer and search a niche-specific query relevant to your business, such as a SaaS sub-topic or tool category (inferred exact tool location: Ahrefs > Content Explorer search bar).
2. Apply Content Explorer's filters for referring domains and organic traffic or social shares to surface pieces that clearly earned links or social signal.
3. Export the result list including each page's publish date and its link/social metrics.
4. Paste or upload the exported data into an AI tool such as ChatGPT or Claude with a request to identify outliers among the pieces.
5. Review the AI's flagged outliers manually to confirm they are genuinely reproducible content types and not one-off news events.
6. Reverse-engineer the format, angle, and structure of each outlier and build an equivalent piece or campaign for your own site.
7. Track your new piece's own link and social velocity after publishing and compare it back against the original outlier's performance curve to validate the approach (inferred verification step).

**Tools:** Ahrefs Content Explorer, ChatGPT or Claude

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Which of these pieces overperformed? Which are the outliers — which got a large number of links very quickly after publishing, and which are recently doing well?
```

**Pitfall:** Creativity here does not mean asking the AI to invent generic content ideas from scratch — the value comes from finding pieces that already proved they earn links and reverse-engineering those, not blind brainstorming.

### 10. Only chase backlinks from pages that rank and refer traffic  `52.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 52*

The filter given for link-building is to stop chasing backlinks purely on Domain Authority if the linking page itself isn't actually ranking or driving clicks — a backlink from a "high DA site that isn't going to rank and get you clicks" is treated as low value. Instead, the target should be relevant backlinks placed on pages that are themselves indexed, ranking, and capable of sending real referral traffic. Edward extends this into a second authority source that doesn't require any backlink at all: a page that ranks well in its niche and earns satisfied, non-pogo-sticking, clicks generates its own authority simply through that ranking performance and click behavior.

> "backlinks, relevant backlinks, that are actually passing you referral traffic"

**How to do it**

1. Before pursuing a link opportunity, check the prospective linking page's own ranking status and estimated traffic, e.g., via Search Console if you have access, or a third-party tool like Semrush or Ahrefs, rather than relying on its Domain Authority score alone.
2. Deprioritize or reject link opportunities on high-DA pages that show no evidence of ranking or organic traffic themselves.
3. Prioritize relevant sites and pages in your niche that are demonstrably ranking and receiving clicks, since those are the ones capable of sending real referral traffic through your backlink.
4. Track referral traffic from acquired backlinks, e.g., in Google Analytics, to confirm they're actually sending visitors, not just existing as a link.
5. In parallel, monitor your own pages' organic click behavior and pogo-sticking rate, since ranking well with satisfied clicks is treated as generating its own authority independent of new backlinks.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics, Semrush, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Chasing backlinks by Domain Authority alone, without checking whether the specific linking page itself ranks or gets any traffic, wastes effort on links that don't pass real referral value — the DA number of the overall domain doesn't guarantee the individual linking page is worth anything.

### 11. Pair anchor text with informational content, not exact-match alone  `30.6`
*core · best practices · source 30*

Ellen's rule for anchor text on any acquired link is to only use business-name or service-keyword anchor text when it's paired with a genuinely informational description that answers a real question a searcher would have, rather than optimizing the anchor text in isolation — this matters because Google's tolerance for exact-match anchor ratios has tightened (per guest Joy Hawkins, cited in the same conversation), so anchors that used to look normal can now read as manipulative. ZipSprout's placements are also standard follow links rather than no-follow, which she treats as an advantage worth protecting by keeping anchor/context pairing natural.

> "really matters, aside from local relevance, is how informational the business is"

**How to do it**

1. Before requesting anchor text on a placement, draft a 1-2 sentence description of the business that answers a real question a searcher would have (e.g., 'frustrated with X — here's how we solve that') rather than a bare service keyword.
2. Only request exact-match or keyword-rich anchor text when it can be paired with that informational description on the same page or placement.
3. If the business name alone doesn't make clear what the business does, pair it with a descriptor rather than using the bare name as anchor text.
4. Confirm with each site owner whether the resulting link is follow or no-follow before counting the placement toward your link target.
5. Vary anchor/description phrasing across a multi-link campaign rather than repeating an identical exact-match anchor on every placement.

**Pitfall:** Requesting exact-match anchor text without pairing it with informational context — or repeating the same exact-match anchor across many placements — is now more likely to read as manipulative, since the threshold for what counts as a 'normal' anchor-text ratio has dropped.

### 12. Parasite SEO: choose the property by its barrier to entry  `57.16`
*core · concrete actions · source 57*

Jackie's working definition is that parasite SEO is posting your own article onto another website to rank for your target keyword, piggybacking on that site's existing SEO strength - he might not rank 'best SEO company' nationally with his own weak site, but with LinkedIn Pulse he might. His property ranking: Reddit for consumer, LinkedIn for B2B, though he isn't bullish on LinkedIn because it's being pummelled with articles right now. The strategic principle he draws out is counterintuitive and worth more than the property list: the best targets are the private ones that don't publicly advertise their articles, and the highest-priced placements perform best because not everyone can jump in. The price is a barrier to entry, which keeps content quality up and the host domain's authority intact - so a more expensive placement is usually the better long-run investment because that limitation is what preserves its ranking ability.

> "post your own article onto another website"

**How to do it**

1. List the candidate host properties for your category and sort them by how open they are - free and open (Reddit, Medium, LinkedIn Pulse), semi-open (industry publications with contributor programmes), and private (unadvertised paid inventory).
2. Check each host's current ranking ability directly: search a term you know it should own and see whether its user pages actually hold page one.
3. Discount the saturated ones. His specific read is that LinkedIn is getting pummelled with articles, so its per-article ranking power is falling.
4. For each shortlisted property, establish whether outbound links are permitted, tolerated, or a ban risk - this is what determines whether you get a link or only a mention.
5. Write genuinely unique content per property. Duplicating one article across hosts is what gets it filtered, and Cody's historical version of this wrote a distinct article for every placement.
6. Target the query with the article's title and first paragraph, since on most user-generated properties those are what become the indexable title and description.
7. Push the page through the indexing routine - indexing services plus social links from profiles you control.
8. Weight budget toward the more expensive private inventory rather than volume on free properties, on his reasoning that the barrier to entry is what protects the placement's long-term performance.
9. Track each property's yield separately - rankings achieved per placement - and cut the properties that stop working, since he notes this shifts constantly.

**Pitfall:** Every property in this list is being used against its own terms of service to some degree, and the host can de-index the whole subfolder at once - Jesper's Claude artifacts case in source 58 went from ranking in five hours to patched within weeks. Never build anything load-bearing on a property you don't own.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau the practical read is defensive: competitor visibility on long-tail practice-management terms may be coming from user pages on third-party platforms rather than from their own domain, so a gap analysis that only crawls competitor sites will understate them.

**Apply anywhere:** The practical read is defensive: competitor visibility on long-tail terms may be coming from user pages on third-party platforms rather than from their own domain, so a gap analysis that only crawls competitor sites will understate them.

### 13. Pay creative contributors in promotion instead of cash  `123.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 123*

ThinkApps did not have in-house designers to produce the Apple Watch concept artwork, so Hyam enlisted the design agencies the company already worked with and asked them to submit concept art for their favorite apps. The offer was not money. It was that ThinkApps would promote the content and get their artwork seen by a large audience, which would in turn promote their agency. That works because the contributors are businesses that need exposure themselves, so the incentive is aligned and the cost of the asset is close to zero. It also multiplies the promotion reach, since every contributing agency has a reason to share the finished piece. Hyam notes even they underestimated how well the piece would do.

> "would promote the content for them"

**Evidence:** Concept artwork from partner design agencies, sourced at no cash cost, became the post that produced 200+ linking domains and 20,000 visitors in a week.

**How to do it**

1. List the partners, vendors or contractors you already work with who own a skill you need for the asset.
2. Define one small, self-contained contribution each can make, such as one design concept or one data point.
3. Offer promotion and attribution as the payment, and say specifically how you will promote it: press pitching, named credit, a link to their site.
4. Let contributors pick the subject inside your frame, as ThinkApps let agencies choose their favorite apps, which raises submission quality.
5. Set a submission deadline that sits before the press-pitch window opens.
6. Assemble the submissions into one post with a credit and a link for each contributor.
7. Send every contributor the live link and ask them to share it on launch day.

**Pitfall:** Offering promotion you cannot actually deliver. If the piece gets no pickup the contributors got nothing, and they will decline the next request, which kills the channel after one use.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can source expert contributions from the aesthetic practitioners and clinic owners already on the platform, paying in named credit and a link to their clinic rather than a fee, and package them into one original piece for the blog.

**Apply anywhere:** Source the creative or expert parts of a flagship piece from partners you already work with, paying in named credit, a link and real promotion rather than cash, and give each one a small self-contained contribution to make.

### 14. Point links only at articles already ranking page 1 to 3 with zero built links  `128.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 128*

Grow and Convert's link prioritization is half data, half judgment, and the data half has one dominant filter: they prioritize articles already sitting on page 1 to page 3 for their target keyword without any backlinks having been built to them. Their reasoning is that an unlinked page in that range is proof Google already finds the article valuable, so authority is the missing input and links are the right lever. The second data indicator is whether the article has shown conversion potential through another channel, typically Facebook. An article that has both, an unlinked page 1 to 3 position and recorded conversions from paid, is the highest-priority link target they have, because ranking it should reproduce conversions they have already seen.

> "We typically prioritize articles that are sitting on page 1 to page 3 without us having built any backlinks."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert prioritize articles at page 1 to 3 with no built backlinks, on the basis that the unlinked position proves Google already values the article.

**How to do it**

1. List every published article with its target keyword, current position and count of manually built links.
2. Drop anything where you have already built links, since the unlinked position is the signal.
3. Keep only articles ranking within the top 30, which is page 1 to page 3.
4. Cross-reference that list against per-article conversion data from paid promotion or email.
5. Rank the survivors: unlinked page 1 to 3 plus recorded conversions first, then unlinked page 1 to 3 alone.
6. Assign the month's link allocation top-down from that list rather than spreading it across all new posts.
7. Leave articles outside the top 30 in the re-optimization queue instead of the link queue.
8. Re-run the list monthly, since positions move articles in and out of the range.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Spending the link budget on a page ranking beyond page three. Nothing in its unlinked position says Google rates the page, so links are usually the wrong diagnosis and the money buys no movement.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should build a monthly list of Pabau pages ranking in the top 30 with no outreach behind them, and aim all link and digital PR effort at that list, starting with pages that also convert.

**Apply anywhere:** Prioritize link building for pages already ranking in the top 30 without any links having been built to them. That unlinked position is evidence the content is rated and authority is the gap, so links are the correct lever. Push anything below page three into re-optimization instead.

### 15. Pre-arrange instant backlinks to beat an embargo the moment you publish  `17.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 17*

Facing a strict pre-launch content embargo, Apple would revoke a reseller's ability to sell iPhones if they published before the keynote, a telco SEO manager pre-arranged partnerships with media sites that were already publishing iPhone rumors and speculation, without publishing anything on the telco's own site yet. The moment Apple's keynote ended and the specifics became official, the team wrote and published their own iPhone page on the fly, and immediately had the pre-briefed media partners add links from their existing rumor coverage straight to the brand-new page. Within about an hour of publishing, the telco's page ranked number one in Google for both the specific new iPhone model and the bare word "iPhone," outranking Apple's own site, though Apple reclaimed the top spot within three days.

> "within literally an hour, we were ranking number one in Google"

**How to do it**

1. Identify an upcoming, highly-anticipated announcement in your industry where you cannot publish content in advance due to an embargo or similar restriction.
2. Before the announcement, build relationships with media sites or partners who are already publishing rumors, speculation, or lead-up coverage on the topic.
3. Ask those partners to commit in advance to adding a link to your page immediately once you publish, timed to the announcement.
4. Prepare your page template, structure, and draft copy ahead of time so only the specific just-announced details need to be filled in (inferred prep step, since the source says the content was made "on the fly" during the keynote itself).
5. During or immediately after the announcement event, fill in the confirmed details and publish the page the moment the embargo lifts.
6. Immediately notify your pre-briefed media partners that the page is live so they add the agreed links right away.
7. Monitor your rankings for the target term within the first hour after publishing to see how quickly the combination of speed and instant links moves you up.
8. Expect the ranking boost to be temporary once larger, more authoritative competitors publish and gain their own links, and plan follow-up content or promotion accordingly rather than assuming the position is permanent.

**Pitfall:** The ranking boost was temporary — the telco lost the number-one iPhone ranking back to Apple after three days, so this tactic wins a short-lived visibility spike around a launch moment rather than a durable ranking.

### 16. Publish unlinked first, read the natural position, then link only the converters  `141.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 141*

Grow and Convert run link building as a second pass, not a launch activity. The sequence they describe: optimize the post around its target keyword, publish it, wait to see where it lands in the SERPs organically with no links pointing at it, and only then choose which articles get links. The selection filter is two-sided. The article has to have high conversion potential, and it has to be close enough that a couple of links could push it to page 1 or 2. Articles that already sit well, or that sit so far back that links will not close the gap, do not get budget. They apply the same logic to internal links on the concussion client, holding back the competitive head terms until enough long-tail high-intent pages exist to point at them. This turns link building from a fixed cost per article into a triage decision made with real ranking data.

> "wait to see where it ends up in the SERPs organically without link building"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert used this sequence on both the subscription client and the concussion treatment center, and say they were targeting the harder pages for link building over the following months to push them to page 1.

**How to do it**

1. Optimize each new post around one target keyword and publish it with no external links pointed at it.
2. Wait until the page has settled in the SERPs, then record its unlinked organic position for the target term.
3. Score each published post on conversion potential using your own first-click, last-click and any-click conversion data, not on traffic.
4. Keep only posts that score high on conversion and currently sit close enough that a couple of links would reach page 1 or 2.
5. Drop from the link plan any post ranking far outside that range; fix intent or content instead.
6. Build links to the shortlist and re-check position after they are indexed.
7. Once you have exhausted the long-tail high-intent terms, point internal links from those ranking pages at the more competitive head terms you want next.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Buying or earning links for every published article spends budget on pages that were never going to convert; the signal is a rising referring-domain count with a flat signup count.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, publish new template and blog pages with no outreach, wait for the natural position, then spend outreach only on pages already producing demo requests and sitting on page 2. Point internal links from ranking template pages at the competitive head terms like practice management software.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish first with no links, let the page find its natural position, then link-build only the pages that both convert and sit within reach of page 1 or 2. Use internal links from your ranking long-tail pages to support the harder head terms once the long tail is exhausted.

### 18. Recruit link partners from the thread of people saying they have no one to link to  `74.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 74*

David Quaid gives a direct method for the most common link-building complaint. He sees threads on X full of people saying they have nobody to link to, and points out that everyone in that thread has the same problem and is likely in a similar area, which makes them each other's answer. His worked example: if one person builds compliance software for financial advisors, another builds cybersecurity for them, another email marketing, another a CRM, you are all talking to the same customer. So review each other's products, give advice if a product is not ready for market, and host each other on your blogs. He frames promotion explicitly as sanctioned, quoting the starter guide's line that Google wants you to promote your site, and adding that promoting is not black hat or gray hat. He notes the starter guide names community engagement, advertising online and offline, and word of mouth as legitimate promotion.

> "The answer is staring you in the face"

**Evidence:** David's financial-advisor software example: compliance, cybersecurity, email marketing and CRM vendors all selling to the same buyer and able to review each other.

**How to do it**

1. Search X for threads where people in your vertical say they cannot find link prospects.
2. List everyone in the thread who sells to the same customer as you but does not compete on the same product.
3. Pick five and write a genuine review of their product on your blog, linking to them without asking for anything.
4. Follow up with a short note saying the review is live and offering them a guest slot or a reciprocal review.
5. For products not yet market-ready, offer specific product advice first and let the relationship produce the link later.
6. Keep a list of these partners and repeat the exchange when either side ships something new.
7. Track which partners actually send referral visitors, and prioritize repeat exchanges with those.

**Tools:** X

**Pitfall:** Turning this into a mechanical reciprocal-link scheme. David's version works because the products genuinely serve the same customer and the reviews are real; a swap list of unrelated sites is a different, riskier thing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sells to aesthetic and healthcare practices. David should list the non-competing vendors selling to the same practices, such as payment processors, device manufacturers and marketing agencies, and run genuine product reviews on the Pabau blog to open link relationships.

**Apply anywhere:** List the non-competing vendors selling to your exact customer, publish genuine reviews of their products first, and let the reciprocal coverage follow.

### 19. Relevance beats volume: 3 great links can outrank 1,000  `24.12`
*core · content insights · source 24*

Responding directly to a poster whose relative's competitors have 500-1,000 backlinks versus their own three, the host argues backlinks do provide a baseline of authority, but keyword relevance matters far more than most SEOs and marketers credit. The most commercially valuable (high-intent, bottom-of-funnel) keywords are frequently under-targeted because fewer people search that deep into the funnel, meaning less competing content exists — so when too few genuinely relevant pages exist for a query, Google falls back to the closest relevant match, which can let a page with much less authority win simply by being the most relevant thing indexed. The stated conclusion: three very high-quality, relevant backlinks can be enough to compete against a rival's 500-1,000, provided the page also places the high-intent keyword in the title, URL slug, H1, and opening sentence, and answers search intent as directly and fast as possible.

> "if you have three very high-quality backlinks, that could be enough"

**Evidence:** Framed as the host's general operating belief across the podcast's many guest case studies (e.g. the Jake Hundley agency ranking #1 with 1/100th competitors' backlinks), rather than a single controlled experiment.

**Apply at Pabau:** Don't benchmark Pabau's link-building targets against competitors' raw backlink counts — audit which high-intent, bottom-of-funnel keywords in the practice-management/clinic-software space are under-targeted and under-linked, and prioritize a small number of highly relevant backlinks plus precise on-page keyword placement (title, slug, H1, opening sentence) over chasing volume.

**Apply anywhere:** Don't benchmark your link-building targets against competitors' raw backlink counts — audit which high-intent, bottom-of-funnel keywords in your own category are under-targeted and under-linked, and prioritize a small number of highly relevant backlinks plus precise on-page keyword placement (title, slug, H1, opening sentence) over chasing volume.

### 20. Run a link-gap analysis before building any links  `30.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 30*

Before starting any outreach or spend, ZipSprout insists on first establishing what they call the 'link gap' — how many more (or fewer) referring links a target page has compared to the pages currently ranking above it for the goal keyword — and sizes the whole campaign around closing that specific gap rather than building links with no numeric target. Garrett states he'd rather not build a single link until the client knows what that link is supposed to accomplish, because without this groundwork, a campaign three months in produces the vague complaint 'nothing's happening, nothing's working' with no way to diagnose why.

> "with an understanding of what we call the link gap"

**How to do it**

1. Identify the target page and its primary goal keyword.
2. Identify the top 3-5 organically ranking competitor pages for that same keyword.
3. Pull each competitor page's referring domain count using a backlink analysis tool. (inferred)
4. Pull your own target page's current referring domain count the same way.
5. Subtract to calculate the gap (e.g., competitors average 300-400 more referring domains, or the gap is as small as 10 links).
6. Set the campaign's link target based on the size of that specific gap rather than an arbitrary round number.
7. Re-run this same gap calculation for every new target page before starting a new mini-campaign, since the gap varies page by page.

**Pitfall:** Building links without first quantifying the link gap means that if rankings don't move after a few months, there's no way to tell whether the campaign under-delivered or was simply never sized to close the actual gap.
