### 1. Qualify a long-tail term by counting genuinely tailored pages in the top 10  `81.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 81*

Grow and Convert give a concrete SERP test for whether a qualified keyword is winnable. For 'best hard money lenders for first-time investors' they observed that only one article on the first page was specifically tailored to first-time investors. Everything else covered hard money lenders broadly and ranked for the qualified phrase only because competition was thin. That count is the buy signal: it means a page written entirely for that segment can outrank pages with far more authority, because relevance to the qualifier is doing the work rather than domain strength. The same reading applies in reverse. If several results are already purpose-built for the qualifier, the gap has been filled and you are back to competing on authority.

> "Only one article on the first page is specifically tailored to first-time investors"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite the 'hard money lenders for first-time investors' SERP, where one of ten results was tailored to the segment.

**How to do it**

1. Search the qualified keyword in an incognito window and open the top 10 organic results.
2. For each result, decide one thing: is this page written for the qualifier, or is it a broad page that happens to contain the words?
3. Count the genuinely tailored pages. Zero or one is a go, two or more is a pass.
4. For a go, check the AI Overview on the same query to see which of those results it is summarizing.
5. Write the page so the qualifier appears in the title, URL, H1 and first sentence, and so every section addresses that segment.
6. Cover the sub-questions specific to the segment that the broad pages skip entirely.
7. Recheck the SERP six to eight weeks after publishing and record whether the tailored page displaced a broad one.
8. If two or more tailored pages already exist, move down to a narrower qualifier and repeat the count.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Judging the term by a difficulty score instead of reading the pages. A keyword tool will report high difficulty because the ranking domains are strong, and hide the fact that none of them is actually about your qualifier.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau commissions a vertical page, the brief should include the count of tailored pages in the top 10 for that term. If it is two or more, the writer should be redirected to a narrower qualifier rather than writing the page.

**Apply anywhere:** Before committing to a long-tail keyword, open the top 10 and count how many pages are actually written for the qualifier rather than merely containing it. Zero or one means a purpose-built page can win on relevance alone; two or more means you are competing on authority again.

### 2. Qualify middle-funnel keywords with two yes-or-no questions  `103.4`
*core · best practices · source 103*

For middle-of-funnel keywords, Grow and Convert apply two tests and require a yes to both. Does the keyword indicate the searcher is in the target audience? And does it indicate they have a pain point the product solves? A yes to the first means ranking reaches the right people. A yes to the second means there is a natural place in the article to introduce and sell the product, rather than bolting a CTA onto unrelated advice. Three keyword shapes reliably pass: use case keywords, template or checklist keywords, and pain point phrases. The test is what separates a middle-funnel keyword from a top-funnel one wearing a commercial coat, and it is quick enough to run on every row of a keyword sheet.

> "there are two qualities that we look for in middle of funnel keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use the two questions as their standing filter for middle-funnel keyword selection across SaaS clients.

**How to do it**

1. Add two columns to the keyword sheet: 'right audience?' and 'pain we solve?'.
2. For each candidate, write which specific job title or buyer would type it, not a segment name.
3. Write the exact pain the searcher has, in their words, and mark whether the product addresses it.
4. Delete any row that fails either column, however good the volume looks.
5. For surviving rows, write one sentence describing where the product enters the article naturally.
6. If that sentence needs the reader to change topic, the row fails the second test after all.
7. Group survivors into use case, template or checklist, and pain point phrase, and brief them as those formats.

**Pitfall:** A keyword can name your audience precisely and still describe a problem your product has nothing to do with. Those articles rank, get the right readers, and convert nobody, because the product mention is a non sequitur.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every middle-funnel topic on pabau.com should pass both tests before it goes into the calendar. 'How to reduce no-shows' passes on both counts; a general article on aesthetics industry trends passes the first and fails the second.

**Apply anywhere:** Score every middle-funnel keyword on two questions: does it prove the searcher is your buyer, and does it name a pain your product fixes? Both must be yes or the article will not convert.

### 3. Rank for how to do the job inside a tool that is not yours  `109.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 109*

The jobs-to-be-done version of a deviant keyword is what Grow and Convert call inefficient ways of doing a job. The searcher asks how to complete a task using a named tool that has nothing to do with your product. Their example is an accounting product targeting 'how to create an invoice in word'. On the surface it is irrelevant, since the product has no connection to Microsoft Word. But the query tells you the reader is doing something arduous with substandard results, and you can offer a better route, possibly free if the feature sits in your free plan. This is the shape that combines a jobs-to-be-done query with an alternative tool: how can I do this task using that tool. The searcher looks committed to a different solution, which is exactly why the page has to help before it argues.

> "the person searching for this is doing something arduous"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's accounting example targeting 'how to create an invoice in word', and the Circuit article on planning multi-stop routes in Google Maps.

**How to do it**

1. List the jobs your product does, then list the generic tools people use to do them badly: Word, Excel, Google Maps, paper, email.
2. Build queries of the shape 'how to do JOB in TOOL' for each pairing.
3. Check volume and read the ranking pages to see whether anyone has covered the workaround well.
4. Write the actual step-by-step for doing it in the other tool, accurately and completely.
5. Surface the friction as you go rather than in a closing pitch: the manual steps, the limits, the error risk.
6. Name one hard limit of the other tool if there is one, since a concrete ceiling is the strongest switch argument.
7. Add a short section showing the same job in your product, with the step count difference.
8. Measure the page on trial signups over months, not on traffic.

**Pitfall:** Writing a thin version of the workaround so the reader has to use your product instead fails the query and loses the ranking. The help has to be genuinely usable.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should target how-to queries for the tools clinics currently misuse, such as building an appointment schedule in Google Calendar or tracking client records in Excel. Deliver the real steps, then show the limits.

**Apply anywhere:** Target 'how to do X in TOOL' queries where the tool is a generic workaround for what your product does. Write the workaround properly, surface its friction as you go, and close with the same job done in your product.

### 4. Rank keyword difficulty last because topical authority outweighs it  `106.11`
*core · best practices · source 106*

Grow and Convert put keyword difficulty last in their process and explain why. KD is a vendor metric that summarizes the backlink strength of pages currently ranking. In their experience trying to rank for hundreds of keywords, topical authority has a bigger effect on how easily you rank than KD does. The mechanism is that high buying-intent keywords are by definition about your own product category, so they are the keywords you already have topical authority for. A time-tracking SaaS will rank more easily for 'time tracking app' than for higher-volume adjacent terms like 'productivity tips', 'payroll tools' or 'invoicing clients', even though those look easier on paper. Prioritizing buying-intent and prioritizing your authority turn out to be the same act.

> "topical authority has a bigger impact on"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's illustration: a time-tracking SaaS ranks more easily for 'time tracking app' than for 'productivity tips', 'payroll tools' or 'invoicing clients' despite the latter looking more attainable.

**How to do it**

1. Build and intent-verify the keyword list before opening any KD column.
2. Group the verified list by how close each term sits to your core product category.
3. Publish the terms inside your category first, regardless of their KD score.
4. Treat a high KD on an in-category term as a timeline estimate, not a reason to skip it.
5. Treat a low KD on an adjacent, out-of-category term as a warning, since you lack authority there.
6. Check what your existing content and inbound links are actually about, and confirm the target sits inside that topic.
7. Once the in-category list is published, use volume and KD together to sequence what remains.

**Tools:** Semrush, Google Keyword Planner, Moz Keyword Explorer

**Pitfall:** Filtering the list by a KD ceiling before checking intent. It removes exactly the in-category commercial terms you are best placed to win and leaves easy adjacent topics that convert nobody.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should stop discarding practice-management keywords on a KD threshold. Pabau's authority is in clinic and aesthetics software, so a high-KD term like 'aesthetic clinic software' is a better bet than a low-KD term like 'skincare marketing ideas'.

**Apply anywhere:** Consider keyword difficulty last. You have topical authority in your own product category, so an in-category term with a scary KD is usually easier to win than an easy-looking adjacent topic where you have none.

### 5. Rank long-tail buyer-intent variants before the head term  `10.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 10*

Instead of targeting a site's biggest head term from day one, map out and publish all the long-tail and variant queries around that core topic first — Charles gives the example of "link building" (huge, resource-intensive, low buyer intent) versus "link building agency," "link building service," "buy backlinks," "how to buy backlinks," and "link building marketplace" (far less competitive, much higher buyer intent and ROI). Publishing the long-tail set first lets those pages index and rank quickly, which signals topical authority to Google; only once those variant pages are indexing and ranking do you build the big pillar or head-term page, because by then you've already unlocked the topical authority needed to compete for it. This inverts the common practice of writing the flagship page first and treats topical-authority-building as a prerequisite unlocked bottom-up rather than top-down.

> "Often I'll build out all the long-tails and all the variants first"

**How to do it**

1. Pick your core topic or head term (e.g., a product category relevant to your business).
2. Brainstorm and list every long-tail variant and buyer-intent query around that core topic before writing the head-term page itself.
3. Rank the long-tail list by estimated buyer intent and ROI, not just search volume, favoring modifiers like "agency," "service," "buy," "vs," or "for [use case]."
4. Publish the long-tail pages first and monitor rank tracking or GSC for how quickly each indexes and starts ranking.
5. Treat consistent fast indexing and ranking across the long-tail set as the signal that topical authority has been unlocked for the head term.
6. Only then write and publish the big pillar or head-term page targeting the core, high-competition query.
7. Avoid allocating your best content resources to the head-term page while the site is still new and topically unproven (inferred prioritization guardrail).

**Pitfall:** Trying to rank a massive head term like "link building" or "hospital" straight away is resource-intensive with comparatively low buyer intent; the long-tail variants have vastly less competition and often better ROI.

### 6. Read a JTBD searcher as an advanced user seeking validation, not a beginner  `131.3`
*core · content insights · source 131*

Grow and Convert define jobs-to-be-done keywords as descriptions of a feature's use case that contain neither the feature name nor the product category. Their mechanism for why these convert is specific. Someone who describes the problem rather than naming the category is often an advanced user looking for validation that a product solves that exact problem. In many cases they have already tried a tool in the category that did not meet their needs, so they are rewording their search to surface products that fit the precise use case. That makes JTBD terms carry product buying intent even though they sit higher in the funnel than category or comparison terms. The practical bonus they call out is that these keywords usually need very few backlinks to rank.

> "often advanced users looking for validation"

**Evidence:** For Smartlook, JTBD terms like 'tracking user activity on website' attracted users hunting specific advanced features, and the piece became a top 10 converter.

**Pitfall:** Writing a JTBD post as a beginner explainer wastes the intent. The searcher already knows the problem; if the article spends 800 words defining it before naming any tool, they leave.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's JTBD articles should assume the reader already runs a practice and has probably tried another system. Skip the definition of practice management software and go straight to whether the specific job, such as automated recall for injectable top-ups, is handled and how.

**Apply anywhere:** Write jobs-to-be-done articles for a reader who has already tried a tool in your category and found it lacking. Show, quickly and specifically, that your product does the exact job they described, rather than explaining the category to them.

### 7. Read the SERP to test buying intent before bidding on a keyword  `158.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 158*

Grow and Convert say the most common paid search mistake is picking keywords by low CPC and high search volume rather than by buying intent. Their client's previous agency bid on 'magento ecommerce platforms', which looks relevant to a Magento development agency. Searching it shows Google serving explainer pages about what Magento is and who it suits, not agency listings. That means the searcher is not looking to hire anyone, so conversion rate will be near zero however cheap the click is. They reject the counter-argument that these keywords nurture people toward buying, because conversion from nurture to closed deal is very low. If people ready to buy now exist, spend there first. The same SERP test they use for organic keyword selection applies unchanged to paid.

> "if you carefully analyze the SERP for this keyword"

**Evidence:** 'magento ecommerce platforms' returned platform explainer results, and the client spent on it with almost no qualified lead potential.

**How to do it**

1. List every keyword currently active in the account with its spend for the last 90 days.
2. Search each one in an incognito window and read the top 10 result titles.
3. Classify the SERP as informational if the results explain what something is, or commercial if they are vendor, agency or comparison pages.
4. Pause every keyword whose SERP is informational, regardless of its CPC or volume.
5. Keep the commercial-SERP keywords and rank them by how directly they name the service you sell.
6. Move any informational term you still want presence on to an organic content target instead of a bid.
7. Re-check the SERP for surviving keywords quarterly, since Google shifts intent classification over time.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Keywords that contain your product name feel relevant, so they survive audits. The tell is spend concentrated on a term with clicks but no qualified conversations over a full quarter.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau bids on or writes for terms like 'aesthetic clinic software', check what Google serves. If the page one is definitions and listicles, the term belongs to a blog article, and the ad budget belongs on 'practice management software for medspas' style terms.

**Apply anywhere:** Before bidding on a keyword, search it and read the top 10 titles. If Google serves explainers rather than vendors, the searcher is not buying, and the term belongs in your content plan rather than your ad account.

### 8. Read the top ten titles and domains to verify commercial intent  `106.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 106*

Grow and Convert call SERP verification the most crucial step in their process, and the check is deliberately quick. Scan the top ten results and read three things: the title of each post, the headers inside the ranking articles, and sometimes the domains themselves. For 'mental health journaling' the results are encyclopedias and scholarly articles, which they name as a giveaway that the searcher wants information rather than a transaction. For 'mental health journaling app' the same scan shows a Google summary of apps and titles promising lists of journaling apps, which means there is room to pitch a product. The check exists because you can convince yourself a keyword has buying-intent while Google has already decided otherwise, and Google's reading is the one that determines what ranks.

> "we briefly scan through the top ten Google results"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's worked example: 'mental health journaling' returns encyclopedias and scholarly articles, 'mental health journaling app' returns app listings.

**How to do it**

1. Search the candidate keyword in an incognito window in your target country.
2. Read all ten organic titles and note whether each promises a product list, a comparison, or a definition.
3. Open two or three of the ranking pages and scan their H2s for buying language such as pricing, features, best for.
4. Note the domain types: encyclopedias, .edu, journals and health authorities signal informational intent.
5. Check whether Google shows a product or app summary module, which signals commercial intent.
6. Keep the keyword only if the majority of the top ten are commercial pages you could credibly replace.
7. If intent is informational, add 'software', 'tool', 'app' or 'platform' to the query and re-run the scan.
8. Park rejected keywords in a separate tab for after the buying-intent list is exhausted, rather than deleting them.

**Tools:** Google

**Pitfall:** Judging intent from the keyword's wording alone. A term can look commercial to you and return ten definition posts, and a page written to sell will not rank against them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO flow should require a screenshot or list of the top ten titles for the chosen keyword before an article is commissioned. Terms like 'aesthetic clinic marketing' will fail this check while 'clinic booking software' passes.

**Apply anywhere:** Before committing to a keyword, scan the top ten titles, the headers inside those pages and the domain types. Encyclopedias and journals mean informational intent; product lists and comparison titles mean you can sell there.

### 9. Recover lost secondary keywords from a post's best-converting month  `116.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 116*

Grow and Convert found that posts often earn more traffic from keywords they were never optimized for than from the target keyword. They call these secondary keywords. Their recovery process starts from conversions, not traffic: find the month a post converted best across the whole engagement, pull the organic keywords it ranked for on that date, then pull the same list for today and diff them. The keywords that disappeared are the recovery list. They filter that list by buying intent rather than volume, so a low-volume comparison term beats a high-volume informational one. Their illustration: 'accounting app for small business' gets 390 searches a month, but pages ranking for it also rank for 'small business accounting software' at 9,900 a month, and both signal someone buying software.

> "what organic keywords this post was ranking for in June 2022"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert re-optimized 22 posts for 30 lost secondary keywords; 20 of the 30 saw significant ranking improvement, and monthly conversions rose from an average of 171 to 258 across the four months either side of February 2023.

**How to do it**

1. Open your conversion-by-post report and find the single month each declining post converted best, not its highest-traffic month.
2. Enter the URL in Ahrefs Site Explorer, Organic keywords, and set the date to that best month.
3. Export that keyword list, then export the same list for today's date.
4. Use the compare-dates view to isolate keywords gained and lost between the two dates.
5. Discard lost keywords with no buying intent; keep category, comparison, alternatives and jobs-to-be-done phrasings.
6. Sort the survivors by the traffic they were sending in the good month, not by tool volume.
7. Check whether each lost keyword's current SERP is served better by re-optimizing the post or by a new article.
8. Log the chosen action per keyword and re-check positions after 8 weeks.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, SEMrush, GA4

**Pitfall:** Starting from the traffic report instead of the conversion report. The highest-traffic month is often not the highest-converting one, and you end up chasing informational keywords that never produced revenue.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this on Pabau's oldest converting blog posts. Pull each post's Ahrefs keyword list for its best demo-request month against today, and treat the lost commercial phrasings, such as software and system variants of aesthetic clinic terms, as the refresh brief.

**Apply anywhere:** Run this on your oldest converting posts. Pull each post's ranked-keyword list for its best conversion month against today, and treat the lost commercial phrasings as the refresh brief.

### 10. Refuse a topic list produced by sorting a keyword tool by volume  `182.2`
*core · best practices · source 182*

Grow and Convert name the exact mechanism behind bad B2B content plans: the agency looks up terms related to the client's category in an SEO tool, sorts by search volume, and uses that order as the roadmap. That process guarantees top-of-funnel topics, because the highest-volume terms in any category are the broad informational ones. They describe those topics as things that could presumably interest your audience but have little or nothing to do with the product you sell, with no indication the reader intends to buy or even research, and conversion rates that are miniscule as a result. Their counter-position is intent over volume: they will happily take keywords with minimal reported volume when intent is high, and say those often become the highest-converting pieces for clients.

> "sort by search volume, and that's how they prioritize"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report conversion rates roughly 10 times higher on bottom-of-funnel terms than on top-of-funnel content, the finding that produced their Pain Point SEO process.

**How to do it**

1. Ask for the raw keyword export behind any proposed roadmap and check what column it is sorted by.
2. Re-sort the same export by purchase intent: does the searcher want to buy, compare, or just learn.
3. Move every 'best <category> software' and every comparison or alternatives term to the top of the list regardless of volume.
4. Keep zero- and low-volume terms in the plan when the phrasing shows a buyer, rather than filtering them out at a volume floor.
5. Push broad high-volume informational terms to a later phase, not out of the plan entirely.
6. Track conversions per article, not sessions per article, so the ordering can be checked against outcomes after a quarter.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** A volume-sorted plan looks impressive because the forecast traffic number is large. The failure shows up months later as traffic growth with flat lead volume, and by then the roadmap is half spent.

**Apply at Pabau:** For pabau.com that means the practice-software comparison and alternatives pages, and terms like best clinic software for a given treatment, get planned before broad aesthetics-industry explainers, even where the explainer shows more volume.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge a keyword plan by what it is sorted on. If the answer is search volume, re-sort it by buying intent before approving any of it.

### 11. Reject a head term by listing who would actually type it  `120.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 120*

Grow and Convert test a tempting head term by writing out the real population behind it. Their example is an IT company eyeing 'cybersecurity', 119,000 monthly searches and squarely on-topic. They list the likely searchers: a student researching the topic, someone considering a career change, an elderly person worried about being scammed. Then they ask who really buys IT services, and answer that it is a senior manager who already knows what cybersecurity is and just needs someone to manage it. That person would never sit down and Google the bare category word. The term is an introductory label for a broad topic, so most of the traffic wants overview information. The test costs five minutes and kills high-volume terms before you spend an article on them.

> "an introductory term for a broad topic"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite 'cybersecurity' at 119k monthly searches as a term an IT security firm should not target for leads.

**How to do it**

1. Take the high-volume term you are tempted by and pull its volume in Ahrefs to confirm the scale of the temptation.
2. Write a list of every distinct person who would plausibly type that exact phrase, including students, job seekers and the merely curious.
3. Separately write who in a buying organization signs off on your product, and what they already know.
4. Ask whether that buyer would type this phrase, given what they already know.
5. If the buyer would not, move the term to a traffic-only bucket and do not schedule an article for it.
6. Rewrite the term with a qualifier that only the buyer would add, such as a service, vertical or capability word.
7. Check the SERP for the qualified version to confirm commercial pages rank there rather than encyclopedic ones.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google

**Pitfall:** Teams skip the exercise because the term is genuinely topically relevant. Relevance to your industry is not relevance to your buyer, and the tell is rankings with high impressions and near-zero form fills.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should apply this to terms like 'aesthetics' or 'medical spa', which draw students and curious consumers. David should keep those in a brand bucket and put article effort behind terms a practice owner buying software would type.

**Apply anywhere:** Before committing an article to a high-volume category term, list every kind of person who would type it, then list who actually buys from you. If the buyer is not on the first list, add a qualifier only a buyer would use.

### 12. Reject a head term by naming everyone who could be searching it  `137.8`
*core · content insights · source 137*

Grow and Convert show an Ahrefs screenshot of 'concussion' at 135,000 monthly searches and then dismiss it. Their test is to name the actual people behind the query: students writing a paper, reporters getting background, and anyone in a conversation about concussions who stopped to learn more. Some fraction could be customers, but measuring conversion rates showed it is a tiny fraction. Their conclusion is that high-volume keywords are inevitably broad, introductory topics, and the effort needed to reach the top five for one is not repaid. They keep the exception open: if a term is pain-point based, has buying intent and also has high volume, take it. The rule is to prioritize on conversion intent first and volume second, not to avoid volume.

> "135,000 monthly searches is really tempting. But who is Googling"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert measured conversion rates and found only a tiny fraction of 'concussion' searchers were potential Cognitive FX customers, so they skipped a 135,000-a-month term.

**How to do it**

1. For each high-volume candidate keyword, write a list of every type of person who could plausibly type it.
2. Include the non-buyers explicitly: students, journalists, researchers, competitors, curious bystanders.
3. Estimate what share of that list is a potential customer; if it is a small minority, deprioritize the term.
4. Check the same term's live SERP for informational and encyclopedic results, which confirms non-buyer intent.
5. Keep the term on the list only if it is also pain-point based or has buying intent.
6. Verify the judgement later with real conversion rates per post rather than relying on the estimate.
7. Spend the freed budget on lower-volume terms whose searcher list contains mostly buyers.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** The volume number is emotionally persuasive and teams commit before doing the searcher audit. The signal you got it wrong is a top-five ranking with high sessions and near-zero conversions.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this searcher audit on broad aesthetic terms before commissioning: a term like 'botox' pulls students, curious consumers and clinicians, while 'botox consent form' pulls practice owners who buy software.

**Apply anywhere:** Before committing to a high-volume keyword, list every type of person who might type it. If buyers are a small minority of that list, deprioritize the term regardless of volume.

### 13. Reject a keyword when the product cannot enter the article naturally  `138.2`
*core · best practices · source 138*

Cup & Leaf rank number one for 'green tea shot'. Nat Eliason points out that the drink in question contains no green tea, so there is no smooth way to put a tea product into the article. The post gets a few thousand visitors a month and has sent $0 in sales. He uses it as the clearest possible illustration that ranking is not the goal. His test is whether the product ties into the topic, and it should be applied before the article is commissioned, not discovered afterwards from the conversion report. The rule catches a whole class of keyword that looks on-topic by category but is off-topic by intent: the searcher shares your vocabulary without sharing your problem.

> "there's really no smooth way to slip our product into an article"

**Evidence:** Cup & Leaf's number-one ranking for 'green tea shot' produced $0 in sales despite a few thousand visitors a month, because the recipe contains no green tea.

**How to do it**

1. Take each candidate keyword and write the one sentence that would introduce your product inside that article.
2. Read the sentence out loud and ask whether a reader who wanted only the answer to that query would find it relevant or intrusive.
3. Search the keyword and read the top three ranking pages; check whether any of them sell a product at all, or whether the SERP is purely informational.
4. Ask what the searcher intends to do in the next ten minutes; if the answer never involves buying anything in your catalogue, cut the keyword.
5. Keep a short list of these rejected terms with the reason, so nobody re-proposes them next quarter.
6. Only escalate a rejected term back onto the list if you launch a product that genuinely serves it.

**Pitfall:** The keyword passes a category-relevance eyeball test, so it survives planning. You only learn it earns nothing after it ranks and you have paid to write and maintain it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau commissions a blog topic, write the sentence that introduces practice management software into it. If the sentence reads as an interruption, the topic is a 'green tea shot' and should not be funded.

**Apply anywhere:** Before commissioning a topic, write the sentence that would introduce your product inside it. If that sentence reads as an interruption, drop the keyword no matter how well you could rank for it.

### 14. Reject the 135,000-search head term and price the searcher instead  `117.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 117*

Grow and Convert use 'concussion' at 135,000 monthly searches as the worked example of why volume is the wrong sort order. They ask who is actually typing it: students writing a paper, reporters getting background, anyone in a conversation who stopped to look something up. Some fraction could be a patient, but in their measured conversion rates it is a tiny fraction. The effort to rank top five for a term that broad is enormous and the payoff is a low-converting audience. Their rule is to sort candidate topics by conversion intent first and search volume second. If a keyword is pain-point based, shows buying intent and happens to have high volume, take it. This source adds the healthcare-specific version: the broad condition name is almost always the trap.

> "135,000 monthly searches is really tempting. But who is Googling"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX's 'concussion' head term shows 135,000 monthly searches; the pain-point term they chose instead produced 32 leads in its first year.

**How to do it**

1. List the broadest condition or category term in your space and note its volume, then set it aside.
2. For that term, write out three types of person who would search it and mark which ones can buy from you.
3. Estimate the buyer share honestly; if it is a small minority, drop the term as a primary target.
4. Rebuild the list sorted by conversion intent: pain point, then direct service, then everything else.
5. Within each intent tier, sort by search volume as the tie-breaker only.
6. Keep a broad term on the list only when it is both pain-point based and high volume.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Chasing the head term costs months of effort and, even when it ranks, converts a tiny percentage, which makes the whole content program look like it does not work.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should stop treating volume as the primary sort for blog topics. A term like 'botox' is the aesthetics equivalent of 'concussion'; the money sits in practice-owner pain points and direct software-intent terms.

### 16. Run a four-question SERP check on every shortlisted gap keyword  `118.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 118*

Grow and Convert treat the gap report as a shortlist, not a plan. Before a keyword enters the content calendar they open the live SERP and look at the top ten organic results, asking four questions: what type of sites rank, who exactly you would be competing with, what type of content they have produced on the topic, and whether it makes sense to introduce your business into that conversation. The last question is the one that kills keywords. A term can look commercial in Ahrefs and still have a SERP made entirely of directories, marketplaces or news, where a vendor page has no natural place. Doing this check after the buying-intent filter and before briefing costs a few minutes per keyword and prevents commissioning articles that cannot rank or cannot sell.

> "perform brief SERP research to finalize keyword ideas"

**How to do it**

1. Take the buying-intent-filtered shortlist and open each keyword's live SERP in an incognito window.
2. Record the page type of each of the top ten results: vendor page, listicle, directory, forum, marketplace, news or documentation.
3. Note which named companies you would be competing against and whether any of them are your size.
4. Read the top two results properly and note the format, the depth and what the page is actually for.
5. Ask whether a page from your company belongs in that result set at all; if a vendor page would look out of place, cut the keyword.
6. Cut keywords where every result is a directory or marketplace you cannot displace.
7. Keep keywords where the ranking pages are thin, off-intent, or written by companies with no product in the space.
8. Record the SERP profile in the brief so the writer matches the format rather than guessing it.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Skipping the check because the keyword already passed a buying-intent filter. Commercial intent and a winnable, vendor-appropriate SERP are different tests, and a term can pass one and fail the other.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every keyword on a Pabau brief should carry a one-line SERP profile: the page types ranking, the named competitors, and whether a Pabau page belongs there. Terms whose SERP is entirely aesthetic directories should be cut, not written.

**Apply anywhere:** Open the live SERP for every shortlisted keyword and check the page types, the competitors, the content format, and whether your business belongs in that conversation at all.

### 19. Run a half-day cross-department customer research session at kickoff  `114.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 114*

Grow and Convert open every client engagement with a half-day customer research session instead of trying to book customer interviews they cannot get. They bring in sales, customer success and support, the CEO or a founder, and whoever sits on the delivery or product side. Each group gets its own question set, because each one hears a different slice of the customer. Notes go into one document, then the team hunts for commonalities and for disconnects. Disconnects are treated as the interesting part: when support describes a different ideal customer than sales does, they push the two groups to explain why they see it differently. The output is a list of common use cases, questions and problems, which they translate directly into content ideas and then into keyword targets. This is the workaround for agencies with no access to the client's customer base, companies protective about customer contact, and teams with too few customers to interview.

> "one way around this is that we hold a half-day"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use this at the start of every new client engagement because they rarely have direct access to the client's customer base.

**How to do it**

1. Block half a day and invite sales, customer success or support, the CEO or a founder, and someone from delivery or product.
2. Ask sales: tell me about a recent prospect conversation and the challenge they were solving; what was the last easy close and why; what alternatives do prospects compare you to; what is your competitive advantage; which industries or customer types fit best.
3. Ask support and customer success: who are your best customers and why; what are the most common use cases; what is your advantage over the alternatives; what are the top questions on customer calls.
4. Ask the CEO or founder: what is your background and the story behind the company, and what led to the idea.
5. Ask product or delivery: which feature set or part of the service do customers use most, and what are the most common use cases.
6. Write every answer down verbatim rather than summarizing in the room.
7. Group the notes into common use cases, common questions and common problems.
8. Flag every disconnect between departments and push the two sides to explain the difference before you resolve it.
9. Turn each surviving problem statement into one content idea, then find the keyword that matches it.
10. Rank the ideas by how directly the problem maps to something your product does.

**Pitfall:** Teams run the session as a single group brainstorm, so the loudest department sets the narrative and the disconnects that carry the real information never surface. The signal is a note file where every answer sounds like the marketing deck.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this session inside Pabau with sales, onboarding, support and a founder, using these exact questions. The output becomes the topic backlog for /blog/ and the qualified pages for medical spas, dermatology and multi-location groups.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a half-day session with sales, support, a founder and product before writing a keyword list. Use a separate question set per department, record answers verbatim, and treat contradictions between departments as the most useful material in the room.

*core · concrete actions · source 102*

### 22. Run keyword research as brainstorm, then SERP check, then volume  `106.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 106*

Grow and Convert give a three-step order for SaaS keyword research and the order is the point. First brainstorm candidates inside the three Pain Point SEO frameworks: category keywords, competitors and alternatives, and jobs to be done. Second, open the SERP for each candidate and read the top ten to confirm Google reads the query as commercial. Third, and only third, put the survivors into a keyword tool for volume and keyword difficulty. Most teams invert this, starting in Ahrefs and sorting by volume, which fills the list with informational terms that never convert. Grow and Convert base the ordering on five years of work with dozens of B2B and B2C SaaS companies, and say it holds for self-serve and sales-led models alike.

> "Our keyword research process has three basic steps"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite 5+ years working with dozens of B2C and B2B SaaS companies across self-serve, sales-led, enterprise and SMB.

**How to do it**

1. Open a sheet with three tabs named Category, Comparisons and Alternatives, and Jobs to be Done.
2. Brainstorm candidates into each tab from product copy, sales calls and customer language, not from a tool export.
3. For each candidate, run the query in Google and read the top ten titles, the headers inside them and the domains ranking.
4. Mark the candidate as buying-intent only if the ranking pages are product lists, comparisons or solution pages.
5. Discard or park any candidate whose SERP is encyclopedias, scholarly articles or definition posts.
6. Only then paste the surviving keywords into Ahrefs or Semrush to pull volume and keyword difficulty.
7. Sort the survivors by volume and KD to sequence publishing, never to decide inclusion.
8. Assign one page per surviving keyword and write it only about that query.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Google Keyword Planner, Moz Keyword Explorer

**Pitfall:** Starting in the tool means the list is shaped by what the tool has volume data for. The signal you have hit this is a keyword sheet where the top rows are all category definitions like 'human resource management'.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO keyword step should reorder: brainstorm from the three frameworks, verify each candidate on the live SERP, and only then pull DataForSEO volume. Today the flow leads with tool data, which is why generic aesthetics explainers keep getting commissioned.

**Apply anywhere:** Reorder your keyword process so the tool comes last. Brainstorm from customer language, verify intent on the live SERP, and use volume and difficulty only to sequence what you already decided to target.

### 24. Run the seven-question PMF survey before planning any content  `161.1`

### 25. Run these five open-ended customer questions to source high-intent topics  `90.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 90*

Grow and Convert invert keyword-first research: they start from the customer's pain point, then look for keywords that describe solutions to it. The raw material comes from five open-ended questions asked in interviews, calls or surveys. Each question maps to one of their three content frameworks. Question one surfaces the problem state, which feeds jobs-to-be-done topics. Question two surfaces who customers see as alternatives, which feeds comparison and alternatives pages. Question three surfaces the words customers use for the product, which catches category synonyms the team never considered. Question four surfaces the top three benefits, which become use-case articles. Question five asks outright what they would search for. Every answer must be open-ended so respondents are not led, and the topics you act on are the answers that come back repeatedly across respondents, not one-off replies.

> "What was the problem you were looking to solve before stumbling across our product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say this is the process they run with all of their clients, and cite it as the source of comparison topics such as posts weighing their client against Hubspot and Marketo.

**How to do it**

1. Ask question 1 verbatim: 'What was the problem you were looking to solve before stumbling across our product or service?' and file answers as jobs-to-be-done topics.
2. Ask question 2: 'If our product/service were no longer to exist, what product/service would you use as an alternative?' and file every named tool as a comparison or alternatives page.
3. Ask question 3: 'How would you describe our product/service to a friend who knew nothing about us?' and mine the wording for category synonyms you do not currently target.
4. Ask question 4: 'What are the top 3 benefits that you receive from our product/service?' and turn each recurring benefit into an in-depth how-to article.
5. Ask question 5: 'If you were to research our product or service, what would you search for?' and run those exact phrases through your keyword tool.
6. Keep every response field open-ended; do not offer multiple-choice options that lead the answer.
7. Tally the responses and only build topics from answers given by multiple respondents.
8. Map each surviving topic to one of the three frameworks: category, comparison and alternatives, or jobs-to-be-done.
9. Where a benefit answer becomes an article, cover several genuine solutions to the problem and position your product as one of them, not the only one.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What was the problem you were looking to solve before stumbling across our product or service?
If our product/service were no longer to exist, what product/service would you use as an alternative?
How would you describe our product/service to a friend who knew nothing about us?
What are the top 3 benefits that you receive from our product/service?
If you were to research our product or service, what would you search for?
```

**Pitfall:** Closed or leading questions produce the answers you already expected, which sends you straight back to the keyword list you would have written anyway. If the responses all sound like your own marketing copy, the questions were leading.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run these five questions with Pabau's sales and onboarding staff once a quarter, then feed the answers into the blog backlog. Question two will produce the competitor alternatives pages, and question three will show whether prospects say 'clinic software', 'practice management software' or 'medspa software'.

**Apply anywhere:** Run these five open-ended questions with customers or your own sales team every quarter, then build topics only from the answers that repeat. Question two produces your comparison pages and question three produces the category synonyms you are not yet targeting.
