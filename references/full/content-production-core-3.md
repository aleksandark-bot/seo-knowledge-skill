### 1. Give the direct answer within the first sentence or two  `36.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 36*

Visitors do not come to a blog post to read a novel, so the core rule is to give exactly what the searcher searched for within the first 100 words of the page, and ideally within the first sentence or two rather than waiting even that long. The reasoning is trust-based: solving the visitor's problem fast earns enough trust that they keep reading past the immediate answer, whereas burying the answer under preamble, story, or SEO padding risks losing the visitor before they ever reach the value.

> "Give them exactly what they searched for in the first 100 words"

**How to do it**

1. Open any existing article and check how many words appear before the direct answer to its target query is stated.
2. If the direct answer appears later than roughly the first 100 words, move it up, ideally restating the core answer within the first one or two sentences of the page.
3. Keep any necessary context, story, or caveats after the direct answer rather than before it.
4. Apply this check as a standing rule for every new article before publishing, not just as a one-time retrofit to old content. (inferred)
5. For bottom-of-funnel commercial pages, pair this fast-answer principle with above-the-fold media and CTA placement so the answer, visual proof, and action are all visible without scrolling. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Burying the direct answer beneath introductory paragraphs, backstory, or SEO-driven preamble — visitors bounce before reaching the value, regardless of how good the eventual answer is.

### 2. Give the template the searcher asked for, then argue against it  `111.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 111*

Grow and Convert target template and checklist keywords, then handle them in a way most brands avoid. For Geekbot they targeted 'daily standup Excel template'. The searcher wants an asynchronous, text-based way to run standups, which is exactly what Geekbot does. So the post includes a downloadable Excel template to satisfy the intent, and also discusses Excel's downsides and argues why Geekbot is the better option. The two moves are deliberate and both are required. Withholding the template fails the intent and the reader bounces. Providing the template without naming its limits gives the reader a free workaround and no reason to buy. They flag template and checklist keywords as particularly relevant in SaaS, because a spreadsheet is usually the product's real competitor.

> "we include a downloadable Excel template to address the searcher's intent"

**Evidence:** Geekbot's 'daily standup Excel template' post: downloadable template supplied, Excel's downsides discussed, Geekbot argued as the better option.

**How to do it**

1. Find the spreadsheet or checklist your buyers build before they buy software, and target that as a keyword.
2. Build the actual template and make it downloadable without gating it behind a form.
3. Open the article by delivering the template, since that is the intent.
4. Add a section on where the template breaks down: manual updating, no reminders, version conflicts, no audit trail.
5. Name each limit as a concrete scenario the reader has lived, not as a generic drawback.
6. Show the same job done in your product, screenshot by screenshot, against those exact limits.
7. Close with the choice framed honestly: the template is fine at low volume, the product wins past it.
8. Track downloads and demo requests separately so you can see the article converting both ways.

**Pitfall:** Gating the template behind a form or omitting it entirely. The searcher wanted a file, does not get one, bounces, and the page loses the ranking that made the argument possible.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /templates/ pages already do the first half. David should add a section to each that names where the spreadsheet or paper form breaks in a busy practice and shows the same job in Pabau, rather than ending at the download box.

**Apply anywhere:** On template keywords, ship the real downloadable template ungated, then add a section naming exactly where it breaks and show your product doing the same job.

### 3. Hand the writer four inputs so they never do strategy or research  `167.10`
*core · best practices · source 167*

Grow and Convert list exactly what a writer receives, and it defines what the writer is not responsible for. First the keyword, so writers do no content strategy; their content strategists, who all started as writers, pick it. Second an interview, typically with a subject-matter expert, because they think asking writers to research and become experts themselves is nonsensical, and pieces should be based on what an actual expert says. Third a kick-off from the content strategist, so writers are not left to get on with it. Fourth ongoing coaching and editing from that same strategist. This is their answer to the complaint that external writers never get the product: product expertise sits in your sales, founder, marketing and product teams, and no amount of general industry experience in a freelancer replaces it. The fix is a process for extracting it, not a better hire.

> "typically with the subject matter expert"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert supply keyword, expert interview, kick-off and coaching with every $500 article, and staff strategists exclusively from former writers.

**How to do it**

1. Assign the target keyword internally before the writer is briefed, and never ask the writer to choose it.
2. Book a recorded interview with the internal subject-matter expert for each piece and give the writer the transcript.
3. Run a kick-off call where the strategist explains the angle, the audience and the product positioning.
4. Name the person who will edit and coach the piece, and keep that the same person across the writer's first several articles.
5. Do not screen for industry domain expertise in the writer, since it will not include your product positioning anyway.
6. If you cannot supply these four inputs, raise the rate significantly rather than expecting the writer to absorb the work.
7. Reuse interview transcripts across related pieces so the expert's time compounds.

**Pitfall:** Hiring for domain expertise and hoping the writer just gets it. Grow and Convert say that expectation was never fair, and the symptom is a client rewriting most of every draft while blaming writer quality.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles should ship with the keyword picked, a recorded interview with an internal aesthetics or product expert, and a kick-off note, so outside writers never invent clinical or product claims.

**Apply anywhere:** Give every writer four things: the keyword, a recorded expert interview, a kick-off call and a named coach. Product expertise lives in your team and cannot be hired in.

### 4. Hand writers four inputs: keyword, expert interview, kick-off, coaching  `164.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 164*

Grow and Convert define exactly what a $500 article buys and what the writer is not expected to supply. The writer gets the keyword, chosen by a content strategist, so the writer does no content strategy. The writer gets an interview, typically with the subject matter expert, because they consider it nonsensical to ask writers to research and become experts themselves; the piece is based on what an actual expert says about the topic. The writer gets a kick-off from the content strategist for guidance rather than being left to get on with it. And the writer gets ongoing coaching and editing from that strategist. This is the structural answer to their earlier point that most writers cannot do strategy and writing well: they remove strategy from the writer's job rather than paying more and hoping.

> "writers aren't asked to research and become experts themselves"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert supply keyword, subject-matter interview, strategist kick-off and ongoing coaching with every $500 article assignment.

**How to do it**

1. Assign the target keyword to the article before a writer is briefed, and never ask the writer to pick it.
2. Book a recorded interview with the internal or client subject matter expert and send the writer the recording and transcript.
3. Hold a kick-off call where the strategist explains the angle, the audience and the intended conclusion.
4. State explicitly in the brief that the article is built from the interview, not from web research.
5. Assign a named content strategist to each writer for editing and coaching through the piece.
6. Keep the strategist accountable for keyword choice, final quality pass and measurement, not the writer.
7. Raise the fee if you ever ask a writer to take on any of these four inputs themselves.

**Pitfall:** Sending a keyword and a word count and expecting expertise back. A writer researching from the SERP reproduces the SERP, which is exactly the generic content the article warns against.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau writers should receive the keyword, a recorded interview with a clinic-facing team member or a customer, and a kick-off call. That interview is what makes a Pabau article say something the competing articles cannot.

**Apply anywhere:** Give every writer four things: the chosen keyword, a recorded interview with a subject matter expert, a kick-off call on the angle, and a named editor for coaching. Writers who must research their own expertise reproduce the existing SERP.

### 5. Have freelancers interview internal experts instead of writing solo  `97.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 97*

Grow and Convert give two fixes for mirage content: hire writers with real hands-on experience in the subject, or hire writers who can interview a subject matter expert and turn that interview into a post. They say the second is best for most companies. The reason is a scheduling problem, not a knowledge problem: the most qualified people, usually the CEO, head of marketing, head of sales or best salesperson, are busy and will not write an 800-word post to help out marketing. The content manager's job in this model is topic selection, expert selection and question design. The writer's job is the interview and the draft. They report running this at three companies, growing blogs past 20,000 unique monthly visitors in as little as a few months, and producing 7-plus quality pieces a week at peak.

> "turn their knowledge into great pieces of content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report running this process at three companies, growing every blog past 20,000 unique monthly visitors in a few months to a year, and hitting 7-plus pieces a week at peak.

**How to do it**

1. Decide the topic yourself as content manager, drawing on customer pain points rather than the writer's suggestions.
2. Name the single person in the company with the deepest hands-on knowledge of that topic, such as the head of engineering for a development post.
3. Work with the writer to draft the interview questions before the call, aimed at the detail that makes the post valuable.
4. Sit on the first few interviews yourself to check the right questions are actually being asked.
5. Hand over interviewing once you are comfortable with that writer's technique, keeping topic and expert selection in-house.
6. Have the writer draft from the transcript and keep the expert's specific examples and numbers intact.
7. Send the draft back to the interviewed expert for a factual check before publishing.
8. Repeat with a bench of several writers so production scales without you writing anything.

**Pitfall:** Handing over question design along with the interview too early produces a transcript of generic answers, and no amount of good writing rescues it. Keep supervising the first few calls.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's strongest detail sits with the support and onboarding teams who see how clinics actually run. David should commission writers to interview them for template and workflow articles rather than briefing from competitor pages.

**Apply anywhere:** For most companies, hire writers to interview your internal experts rather than to write solo, and keep topic selection, expert selection and question design in-house.

### 6. Have the brief say whether and how to introduce the product  `71.22`
*core · ai workflows · source 71*

Devesh treats product guidance as a required section of a content brief and calls its absence one of the main weaknesses of the free generators. His test questions are direct: does the tool understand your business rather than just your topic, and does it tell the writer whether and how to introduce your product. He states the mechanism plainly. If the tool has no knowledge of your products, its brief cannot tell a writer how to represent you, so the writer has no idea which features to introduce, where and how. In the demo the keyword classification decided the answer, a bottom-funnel category keyword earning a pitch of every feature. He also notes most AI content writers do not even think to pitch the product, and cannot, because they know nothing about it.

> "No guidance on how to pitch your product"

**Evidence:** Devesh identified 'no guidance on how to pitch your product' as a weakness shared by every ranking free brief generator, and used it as a selling point for his own tool.

**How to do it**

1. Add a product section to the brief template with three fields: whether to pitch, which features, and where in the piece.
2. Set the whether from the keyword classification, with bottom-funnel category keywords earning a full walkthrough.
3. Name the specific features to introduce and tie each to the pain point the brief identified.
4. Specify the position, for example a dedicated product section after the teardown rather than scattered mentions.
5. Include one or two written product-pitch examples in the brief so the writer sees the intended register.
6. Reject briefs that name a keyword and a word count but leave the writer to guess about the product.

**Tools:** Wave Writer

**Pitfall:** A brief with no product guidance produces a draft that either never mentions your product or mentions it in the wrong place, and the fix costs a full revision round because the argument structure has to change.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should state explicitly which Pabau features an article introduces and where, so writers place them in the Pabau section before the Conclusion rather than seeding the body with product mentions.

**Apply anywhere:** Give every brief a product section stating whether to pitch, which features, and where in the piece. Without it the draft either omits your product or places it wrong, and fixing that takes a full revision round.

### 7. Have the writer interview a product expert before any bottom-funnel draft  `105.9`
*core · best practices · source 105*

Grow and Convert make a hard staffing claim about buying-intent content. A keyword with high buying intent requires the article to give a genuine sales pitch, because that is what the reader arrived for. Producing that pitch requires intimate product knowledge: how the product solves this specific pain point, which capability matters, what the trade-offs are. They state directly that you cannot get this by handing a writer a content brief and expecting them to research the arguments themselves. The writer either knows the product deeply or interviews someone who does. They pair this with a depth rule: a reader searching 'enterprise accounting software' has held that job for years, so beginner explanations of why accounting software matters will not land. You have to show you know more than they do.

> "the writer has to be intimately familiar with your product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state you cannot produce a post that smartly sells your product by handing a writer a content brief and expecting them to research the arguments on their own.

**How to do it**

1. Before commissioning any bottom-funnel piece, book the writer a 30-minute call with a product specialist or a senior salesperson.
2. Ask the specialist which capability wins this specific use case and what a competitor does instead.
3. Ask what objection buyers raise at this stage and what answer closes it.
4. Record the call and give the writer the recording, not a summary.
5. Write into the brief the reader's assumed knowledge level, drawn from the keyword: buyer of enterprise software, or beginner learning the category.
6. Ban beginner definitions from bottom-funnel drafts and require the piece to say something the reader does not already know.
7. Require the draft to name the exact product capability that solves the pain point, not a generic benefit line.
8. Reject any draft where the product section could be swapped for a competitor's without changing a word.

**Pitfall:** Handing a freelance writer only a brief and a keyword produces a competent article with a generic product paragraph. It ranks and does not convert, because the reader came for a pitch and got a summary of the category.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's comparison and 'software for [vertical]' articles need a call with someone who runs Pabau demos before drafting. David should keep the recording attached to the brief so the product section names the specific feature that solves the practice's problem.

**Apply anywhere:** Book every bottom-funnel writer a 30-minute call with a product specialist or senior salesperson before drafting, and pass on the recording rather than a summary. Reject any draft whose product section would read the same with a competitor's name in it.

### 8. Have writers interview your own experts, not outside ones  `107.10`
*core · best practices · source 107*

Grow and Convert argue most companies and most SEO agencies outsource writing badly. The two standard arrangements are asking a writer to research a topic and write a post, or to write from a content brief. Both put the burden of producing detailed arguments and supporting evidence on someone who is not a subject-matter expert, so they cannot do it well. Some companies try to patch this by having writers interview third-party experts. Grow and Convert say that fails differently: the post then reflects the outside expert's opinions rather than your company's. Their answer is that the writer interviews subject-matter experts inside your company. Whether the arrangement is in-house or outsourced matters less than that condition holding. They also rule out using an AI writing tool to produce the content in place of the interview.

> "interview subject matter experts within your company"

**How to do it**

1. Name two or three internal subject-matter experts per topic area and get them to commit interview time.
2. Give writers a brief that includes the interview slot, not just the outline and keyword.
3. Have the writer run a recorded 30 to 45 minute interview before drafting.
4. Require the draft to quote or paraphrase at least three specific points that only came from the interview.
5. Reject drafts that could have been written from the SERP alone, regardless of who wrote them.
6. Do not substitute a third-party expert interview, because the piece then carries their views rather than your company's.

**Pitfall:** Brief-only outsourcing produces posts that are competent and interchangeable, because the writer can only recombine what already ranks. Interviewing outside experts fixes the depth but moves the point of view off your company.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has clinical and product staff who know how practices actually run. Writers on aesthetic workflow articles should interview them rather than working from competitor pages, and the resulting specifics are what carry E-E-A-T on healthcare topics.

**Apply anywhere:** Make an interview with your own internal experts a required step in every brief, and reject drafts that could have been assembled from the search results alone.

### 9. Hold publishing at one post a week rather than scaling with AI  `107.5`
*core · best practices · source 107*

Grow and Convert's first tactical step is cadence, and their answer is quality over quantity. They say one ranking for your best, highest buying-intent keyword is worth 500 random top-of-funnel posts. Their recommended volume is one new post per week, and they note good results from clients publishing only one or two posts a month. Volume can rise slowly if quality holds. They warn the pressure to publish more has worsened in the last two years because AI writing tools made volume cheap, and they have seen B2B companies go from four posts a month to forty expecting volume alone to work. What they observe instead is an early hockey-stick traffic spike followed by collapse over six to twelve months, often below the site's original baseline. They attribute this to Google's 2024 scaled content abuse policy, which suppresses previously well-performing pages too, not just the new ones.

> "one new post per week"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report B2B clients going from four to forty posts a month, spiking, then dropping below their original baseline within six to twelve months; Google's scaled content abuse policy launched 2024.

**How to do it**

1. Set the default cadence at one new post per week, or one to two a month if the team is small.
2. Require every post to target a scored high-intent keyword from the pain-point sheet before it is commissioned.
3. Refuse volume increases unless the additional posts pass the same intent and quality gate.
4. If leadership demands more, add one extra post per month at a time and watch rankings for a quarter before adding again.
5. Do not scale to tens of posts a month on AI drafts, because the downside is sitewide suppression not just weak new pages.
6. Track the ranking of previously strong pages as the early warning that a volume push has triggered scaled-content problems.

**Pitfall:** The traffic spike from a volume push looks like proof it worked. The collapse arrives six to twelve months later and takes older, previously well-performing pages down with it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep its blog cadence deliberate and put the effort into fewer, higher-intent pieces, since a scaled-content flag would also suppress the existing template and code-reference pages that already rank.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish about one post a week against scored high-intent keywords, and resist AI-driven volume pushes, because scaled low-effort content risks sitewide ranking suppression, not just weak new pages.

### 10. Insert a questionnaire and outline gate before the writer drafts anything  `126.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 126*

Grow and Convert's content writing process has a step most teams skip: a pre-writing questionnaire plus outline that the writer completes and the strategist or editor approves before formal writing begins. The stated purpose is to get the writer and the strategist on the same page about the structure and the details, specifically what arguments or claims the piece makes and how each one is backed up. They are explicit about the payoff: this way you do not end up with first drafts, or final drafts, that are way off the mark. It sits between the expert interview and the draft, so it also forces the writer to actually mine the transcript rather than start typing. Grow and Convert say they teach the questionnaire in detail in their course, but the mechanism is simply claim-plus-support agreed in advance.

> "a questionnaire and outline step before the writer starts"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert credit this step with preventing first and final drafts that are way off the mark.

**How to do it**

1. Create a standing questionnaire the writer completes after reading the interview transcript.
2. Require one line per section naming the claim that section makes.
3. Require, next to each claim, the transcript quote or data point that backs it.
4. Require the writer to name the intent the piece serves and how the product ties in.
5. Attach the H2 outline to the same document.
6. Have the strategist or editor approve or reject the questionnaire before drafting is authorized.
7. Reject any section whose support field is blank or points only to a competing article.

**Pitfall:** Treating the outline alone as the gate. Grow and Convert note a basic outline is usually obvious; the difference between a good and a bad piece is what goes inside each section, which is what the questionnaire captures.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should add a claim-and-support table to every article brief before writing starts, with the interview transcript quote in the support column. That is also the artifact the fact-check pass can audit against.

**Apply anywhere:** Put a questionnaire and outline gate between research and drafting. One line per section naming the claim, one line naming the evidence, approved by the editor before a word of the draft is written.

### 11. Interrogate each how-to step with six missing-detail questions  `99.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 99*

Khanal demonstrates the gap between adequate and useful instructions using surfing. His mid-beginner version of step one is 'once you see a wave coming, you should paddle as fast as possible', and step two is 'once you feel the wave pull you a bit, try to stand up quickly.' Neither is wrong, and a surf lesson would tell you both, but he says there is so much information lacking. He then lists the questions a semi-qualified reader brings to step one: when is the wave close enough, which direction should the board face, when do you turn the board, where on the board should your body be, what if you do not catch the wave since that will happen to everyone learning, and what common mistakes people make at this step. He exempts only dictionary-level knowledge retrieval content, such as how many calories are in an apple, from needing this depth.

> "What common mistakes do people make at this step?"

**Evidence:** Khanal's surfing walkthrough, contrasting his own two-step version against the six questions a learning surfer would have on step one alone.

**How to do it**

1. Take each step in the how-to and write the six questions under it: when, which direction or which option, at what moment, where exactly, what if it fails, and what mistakes are common.
2. Answer the timing question with an observable trigger, not a feeling.
3. Answer the positioning question with a measurable placement or setting value.
4. Answer the failure question by describing the most likely failure and the correction for next time.
5. Answer the mistakes question with the specific error a beginner makes and the symptom it produces.
6. If you cannot answer three or more of the six for a step, escalate the step to a subject matter expert.
7. Exempt only knowledge-retrieval facts from this pass, not workflow steps.

**Pitfall:** A step that is technically correct passes editorial review because nothing in it is false. The failure mode is a checklist a reader cannot execute, and the tell is that every step is one sentence long.

**Apply at Pabau:** Apply the six questions to every numbered step in Pabau how-to and template articles: which screen, which setting, at what point in the appointment flow, what breaks, and the mistake practices make. Single-sentence steps are the flag.

**Apply anywhere:** Apply the six questions to every numbered step in your how-to articles: which screen, which setting, at what moment, what breaks, and the common mistake. Single-sentence steps are the flag.

### 12. Interrogate the process for extracting expertise, per article  `101.3`
*core · best practices · source 101*

Step three: ask exactly how the writer or agency gets domain and product expertise out of your experts and into the copy. Grow and Convert warn that most outside writers do 'Google research papers' — they Google the topic and regurgitate the consensus, which yields undifferentiated content. The distinguishing question is the mechanics of expert access: is it a single onboarding interview, a few one-hour calls, or an interview with a subject matter expert for every individual piece? They say the per-piece interview is extremely rare among outside agencies and is the most effective approach, and it is what they do themselves. For B2B, where the audience is advanced industry experts, they add that failing to speak at that level risks reputational damage.

> "doing interviews on a piece-by-piece basis"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say the per-piece interview approach is 'extremely rare' among outside agencies despite being the most effective.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the candidate to describe their expertise extraction process without prompting them with options.
2. Classify the answer: no process, one-off onboarding interview, periodic calls, or per-article SME interview.
3. Rank per-article interviews highest and no process as an automatic reject.
4. Ask who at your company they need, for how long, and at what cadence.
5. Ask for one published sample and check for detail that could only come from an interview.
6. Confirm they can cover value props, differentiators and pain points in your brand's language.

**Pitfall:** Agencies that skip expert access produce content that reads like every competitor's; for expert B2B audiences that reads as ignorance and costs credibility, not just conversions.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any external writer working on pabau.com should get a scheduled interview with a Pabau product or clinical specialist for each article, not a single onboarding call, since aesthetic practice workflows cannot be researched off the SERP.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask candidates precisely how they get expertise out of your team and into each article, and prefer a per-article expert interview to a single onboarding call. Without it you get a summary of what already ranks.

### 13. Interview an operator when your team has no original ideas  `100.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 100*

Grow and Convert anticipate the objection 'what if we don't have any original ideas' and answer with a tactic rather than a scolding: find someone who has original ideas and interview them. They call it a bread and butter technique for client work. Two examples. For ReportGarden they interviewed the founder of a multi-million dollar agency about scaling a digital marketing agency, and the post brought over 6,000 visitors in the first few months after publishing. For Patreon they interviewed someone inside the company to collect specific creator stories for a post on business models. They contrast this with a freelance writer who has never scaled an agency writing something long on the topic. They classify interview-based pieces as medium originality: more work than a contrarian angle, less than a research study.

> "you have to find someone with original ideas and interview them"

**Evidence:** The ReportGarden agency-scaling interview post drove over 6,000 visitors in its first few months. The Patreon business models post, interview-sourced, ranks top 5 for 'best patreon models' and gets 6,000 to 7,000 pageviews a month.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the topic first, then identify who has actually done the thing at scale.
2. Approach operators one tier above your reader, such as a founder who has already solved the problem.
3. Ask for specific stories with numbers and names rather than general advice.
4. Attach a distinct story to each item in the piece rather than one story overall.
5. Credit and tag the interviewee in the post and on social so they share it.
6. Keep the writer's job to structuring and clarifying, not to inventing the substance.
7. Reuse the same interview across several posts rather than one.

**Pitfall:** Interviewing someone at the same level as your reader produces generic quotes. The signal is that the transcript has no numbers, no names and no moment where the interviewee says something you did not expect.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau has no internal expert on a clinical or business topic, David should book a 30-minute interview with a clinic owner or practice manager rather than commission a freelancer to research it. One interview can feed several articles.

**Apply anywhere:** When your team has nothing original on a topic, interview an operator who does and build the piece from their specific stories. Reuse one interview across several posts.

### 14. Interview customer-facing staff before doing any keyword research  `111.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 111*

Grow and Convert put customer research ahead of the keyword tool. Their process starts by interviewing multiple customer-facing employees inside the client company, typically sales, product and founders. They are looking for who the ideal customers are by vertical, size and job title, what pain points those customers are trying to solve, how the product solves those pain points differently or better than competitors, and what competing tools or processes buyers tried before. Only then do they use that knowledge to inform keyword research and to select specific terms per article. The order is the point. Keyword tools return the vocabulary of the market in aggregate; the interviews return the vocabulary of the buyer and the list of competitors and workarounds worth building comparison and alternatives pages against.

> "we start with interviewing multiple customer-facing employees"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run this interview stage at the start of every client engagement before selecting keywords for their Pain Point SEO strategy.

**How to do it**

1. Book 45-minute interviews with at least one salesperson, one product person and a founder.
2. Ask each for the verticals, company sizes and job titles of the last ten closed deals.
3. Ask what pain point the buyer named in the first call, in the buyer's own words, not the marketing phrasing.
4. Ask what tool, spreadsheet or manual process the buyer was using before, and write down every product name.
5. Ask what the buyer said the product did better than the alternative they evaluated.
6. Turn the named competitors and workarounds directly into alternatives and versus keyword candidates.
7. Turn the buyer's pain-point wording into how-to and direct-pain-point keyword candidates.
8. Only now open Ahrefs, and use it to size and validate that list rather than to generate it.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Skipping straight to the keyword tool produces industry jargon your buyers do not search, and misses the competitor and workaround names that make the highest-converting pages possible.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run these interviews with Pabau's sales team and founders once a year and keep the output in a shared doc. The named competitors and the pre-Pabau workflows drive the alternatives pages and the how-to briefs.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview your sales, product and founder team before touching a keyword tool. The competitors and workarounds they name become your comparison pages; the buyer's own wording becomes your pain-point keywords.

### 15. Interview sales and support to source GEO content details  `78.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 78*

Grow and Convert's second step is interviewing every customer-facing role inside the company: sales, support, product and the founders. They ask what users actually say their pain points and use cases are, then extract the positioning, features and differentiators that make the product the right choice in each specific scenario. Their claim is blunt: these details are not obtainable from Google research, and AI-generated copy will not produce them by default. The detail required for LLM recommendation only exists in the heads of people who talk to customers daily. This makes the interview the input to the content, not a nice-to-have research step bolted on afterwards.

> "We interview the people who know your customers best"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert built Constitution Lending's topic map by interviewing their lending experts who talk to borrowers every day.

**How to do it**

1. Book 45-minute calls with one rep from sales, support, product and a founder.
2. Ask each: what are the three situations where a prospect says yes fastest, and what did they say their problem was.

### 16. Interview sales, support and account managers before writing product copy  `92.4`

### 17. Interview the client before keyword research, then match keywords to topics  `124.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert deliberately invert the usual order. Most agencies run keyword research first, prioritize the list and assign briefs. They interview the client company first to learn what pain points it solves, who its best customers are and who it thinks its competitors are. Topics come out of that interview. Only then do they find keywords that correspond to each topic, focusing on terms tied to the customer's pain points. Then, at writing time, they run a second interview with a team member about that specific keyword, asking how the product addresses the pain point in relation to that keyword. The two-interview structure is what is distinctive here: one at strategy level to generate topics, one per article to supply the argument.

> "We interview our clients before we look for keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report eight years of using this order to rank for bottom-of-funnel keywords and drive client leads.

**How to do it**

1. Book a kickoff interview with the client covering pain points solved, best-fit customers and self-named competitors.
2. Write the topic list from that interview before opening any keyword tool.
3. For each topic, find the keyword that corresponds to it rather than picking keywords by volume.
4. Filter candidates to terms tied to a customer pain point, and lean on competitor and bottom-of-funnel terms.
5. Book a second, per-article interview with a client team member once a keyword is assigned.
6. In that interview ask only about the pain points behind this specific keyword and how the product addresses them.
7. Have the writer fill the questionnaire from the interview before proposing an outline.
8. Repeat the per-article interview for every new topic rather than reusing the kickoff notes.

**Pitfall:** Running only the kickoff interview and writing every later article from it. The articles drift back to generic because nobody has fresh, keyword-specific detail to work with.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should hold one strategy interview with product and support to generate the topic list, then a short per-article interview before each blog or template piece. The keyword should be chosen to fit the practice pain point that interview surfaced, not picked from a volume-sorted export.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview the business first to generate topics, then find the keyword that matches each topic, then run a second short interview per article about that specific keyword. Two interviews, one strategic and one per piece.

### 19. Interview the client or internal expert once per article, not once per engagement  `125.13`
*core · best practices · source 125*

Grow and Convert state plainly that they interview their clients for each article they create, in order to find bits of originality that differentiate the piece from what everyone else writes on the topic. > "We interview our clients for each article we create"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe per-article client interviews as a key component of their content strategy, run to find bits of originality that differentiate each piece from competing coverage.

**How to do it**

3. Book 20 to 30 minutes and record it; this does not need to be an hour.
4. Ask specifically what they do differently from the standard advice on this topic.
5. Ask for one example with numbers or a sequence attached.
6. Pull the two or three specifics into the brief as required inclusions before the writer starts.
7. Refuse to schedule the draft if the interview has not happened.
8. Keep transcripts filed by topic so recurring topics build a source library.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's article process should name a per-article interviewee, matched to the topic, rather than relying on one product briefing across a whole batch. This is what supplies the originality nugget each brief requires.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a short recorded interview for every article, with the expert matched to that topic, rather than one kickoff interview reused across a batch. Pull two or three specifics into the brief before drafting.

### 25. Make the agent scrape page one before it writes a word  `77.3`
*core · ai workflows · source 77*

Schneider's second stage has the agent scrape page one of Google for the target keyword using a SERP scraping API, and only then write. The article is written from that scraped corpus rather than from the model's training data. This is the guardrail that stops the pipeline producing generic output: the agent's context contains what actually ranks for the query right now, so the draft inherits the current intent shape, the subtopics competitors cover and the facts they cite. It also gives the writing stage something to be original against, because the agent can see what every ranking page already says. > "Researches the article by scraping page 1 with seper"

**Evidence:** Schneider names Serper as the scraping layer and places it between keyword selection and writing.

**How to do it**

1. Call a SERP API for the target keyword and pull the top 10 organic URLs.
2. Fetch the full text of each of those pages, not just the SERP snippets.
3. Extract each page's headings into a single list so the agent can see the subtopic coverage across the SERP.
4. Pass the extracted text and heading list into the writing stage as the research context.
5. Instruct the writing stage to source every factual claim from that corpus and to name where it came from.
6. Have the agent list the subtopics that appear on fewer than three of the ten pages, and require the draft to cover at least one of them as its original angle.

**Tools:** Serper
