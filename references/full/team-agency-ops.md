### 1. First three SEO hires: a generalist, a digital-PR/content strategist, then an assistant  `46.e4`
*core · best practices · source 46 · universal-edition only*

Asked to build a world-class SEO team from scratch, Tallent's agency-side sequence is deliberate. Hire one: a generalist with 10-plus years who can do the work, talk to clients, and hire and lead — he calls this the biggest available business gain, because that person thinks in 80/20 terms and will not burn weeks arguing over a comma. Hire two: a digital-PR-and-content strategist, on the argument that getting the brand's name out matters more as website traffic trends down and zero-click traffic trends up, and that this person can also drive omni-channel work on YouTube and TikTok. Hire three: an assistant to the first two, which both multiplies their capacity and creates a training pipeline — that person can grow into one of them within two or three years.

> "my first hire would be a generalist ... That person views the world in terms of 80/20"

**How to do it**

1. Hire a genuine generalist first: someone who can execute, communicate with clients, and build a team.
2. Screen that person explicitly for 80/20 prioritization rather than depth in one discipline.
3. Hire a digital-PR-and-content strategist second, scoped to brand visibility and off-site mentions, not just blog production.
4. Include omni-channel and short-form video in that second role's remit from the start.
5. Make the third hire an assistant supporting both, rather than a third specialist.
6. Treat the assistant seat as a deliberate training pipeline and plan to promote from it in two to three years.

**Tools:** None (process)

**Pitfall:** Hiring a deep specialist first — the detail-oriented profile that argues over a comma is what you want in hire two or three, not in the person setting priorities.

### 2. Hire for communication first — it is the one skill AI cannot take  `46.e1`
*core · best practices · source 46 · universal-edition only*

Travis Tallent, who grew an agency SEO org from 15 to 90 people, names communication as the single most underrated skill in SEO hiring, 'bar none'. His reasoning is that no tool and no AI removes it, and that influencing clients and executives is the actual mechanism by which an SEO's career advances. He treats it as a trainable muscle rather than a fixed trait: the team ran deliberate mock client calls and mock pitch sessions, starting with a literal 'ring ring' to put people in the right mindset, on the theory that reps accumulate until presenting under pressure feels calm.

> "The biggest skill that I would say is underrated is communication, bar none."

**How to do it**

1. Add a communication-weighted stage to your interview loop, scored separately from technical SEO knowledge.
2. Book recurring mock-call sessions where each person presents a real finding to a role-played client or executive.
3. Have the manager give specific feedback on framing — what outcome the message was trying to produce, not just whether the facts were right.
4. In one-on-ones, coach the framing of a specific upcoming client or stakeholder conversation rather than reviewing communication in the abstract.
5. Reduce reporting overhead only once trust is established; until then ask new hires to over-communicate status deliberately.

**Tools:** None (process), Video call software

**Pitfall:** Treating communication as a personality trait you screen for once, instead of a muscle that needs scheduled reps — the mock calls feel tedious and slightly silly, which is exactly why teams quietly drop them.

### 3. Open every SEO interview with 'audit this new site' and judge the walkthrough  `46.e2`
*core · concrete actions · source 46 · universal-edition only*

Tallent's standard opening interview question is to hand the candidate a hypothetical new website and ask them to walk through how they would audit it and arrive at a strategy. He uses the answer as a single instrument reading three things at once: whether the person actually understands SEO, whether they can communicate a process clearly, and whether they can tell which details matter from which do not. He reports usually knowing by the end of that first question whether the candidate is a fit, and uses it to frame the rest of the interview. He screens for two further traits: communication, and 'executive presence' — judged by whether the call left him energized or drained.

> "say you get a new website, walk me through your process for auditing that website"

**How to do it**

1. Script one opening prompt: 'You've just been handed this website — walk me through your audit process and how you'd arrive at a strategy.'
2. Let the candidate talk without rescuing them; the structure (or absence of it) is the signal.
3. Score three axes separately: SEO understanding, clarity of communication, and prioritization judgement.
4. Probe where they spent their time — a candidate who opens on comma-level detail rather than traffic-moving levers is telling you their prioritization instinct.
5. Note your own energy at the end of the call as an explicit data point on executive presence.
6. Use the remainder of the interview to test the specific weak axis the walkthrough exposed.

**Tools:** None (process)

**Pitfall:** Grading the walkthrough only on technical correctness — the prioritization signal (what they chose to do first, and what they ignored) is the part that predicts on-the-job value.

### 4. Publish decision rights and information flows before headcount grows  `46.e5`
*core · best practices · source 46 · universal-edition only*

Tallent's answer to how 90 SEOs avoid colliding is not more coordination meetings but explicit clarity about who decides what and where information travels. He argues that at that size hierarchy genuinely matters — people need to know who to go to for which decision, and what goes to their manager versus elsewhere — and that making those decision rights and information flows crystal clear removes most of the chaos, because people know immediately who to tell when something happens. He also notes a side effect: naming a decision as someone's to make shifts them from 'here is the work I have to do' toward owning outcomes. The instrument he names is RACI — for each initiative, identify who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed — worked line by line, client by client, which he estimates gets you about 85% of the way there.

> "you identify who's responsible (the R), who's accountable (the A), who's consulted (the C), and informed (the I)"

**How to do it**

1. List your initiatives or client engagements as rows.
2. For each, name the single accountable person, then who is responsible for the work, who must be consulted, and who is merely informed.
3. Write down where information flows: what escalates to a manager, and what routes elsewhere.
4. Publish the grid where the team actually works, not in a buried document.
5. Re-run the exercise per new client or initiative rather than maintaining one static chart.
6. Frame each row as an owned outcome, not a task list, to convert the clarity into ownership.
7. Foster the norm that nobody hoards knowledge or method — competitiveness is fine, information hoarding is not.

**Tools:** Notion, Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Assuming a flat structure scales — the claim here is that past a certain size, ambiguity about who decides is the thing that generates the mayhem, not the hierarchy itself.

### 5. Hire product copywriters, not writers, for conversion content  `113.9`
*useful · general insights · source 113*

Grow and Convert are direct that writing conversion content is a distinct skill set that is not easy to find or teach. Even great writers often lack the ability to understand value propositions, key features and unique differentiators and express them clearly, because that ability is closer to product copywriting than to content writing. They say most content marketing and SEO agencies, many of whom outsource writing to freelancers, lack good processes for gaining and expressing product and domain expertise. Their own answer is structural rather than editorial: base articles on extensive interviews with the client's product, sales and customer support teams, and run an extensive writer training process that has built a tight-knit team of product copywriters over years. Their conclusion is that involving your own company's experts in content creation is essential for content that converts advanced B2B readers.

> "outsource content writing to freelancers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert have run a writer training process over years to build a team of product copywriters, and base articles on interviews with client product, sales and support teams.

**Pitfall:** Judging a writer on portfolio prose quality. A strong content writer with no product copywriting instinct produces clean articles that never explain why anyone should buy.

**Apply at Pabau:** If David outsources Pabau articles, the test in the trial brief should be whether the writer can explain a Pabau feature against a named competitor, not whether the prose reads well. Otherwise the internal team has to supply that section.

**Apply anywhere:** Test writers on whether they can explain your product against a named competitor, not on prose quality. Budget for internal expert interviews, because outsourced writing rarely supplies product expertise on its own.

### 6. Keep the content manager out of writing every post  `97.9`
*useful · best practices · source 97*

Grow and Convert argue that a content marketing manager who writes every piece cannot do the more important parts of the job, which they list as user research, promotion, conversion and measuring success. Their conclusion follows from that: you cannot have a writer run content marketing, because you are setting yourself up for failure. The model they run instead is a manager who owns research, topics, expert selection, promotion and measurement, with a bench of freelancers doing interviews and drafting. That split is what let them push production to 7-plus quality pieces a week without the quality falling, and it is why they see the writing bottleneck as an org design problem rather than a capacity problem.

> "you can't have a writer run content marketing"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert ran this split at three companies and reached 7-plus pieces a week at peak while growing blogs past 20,000 monthly uniques.

**How to do it**

1. List the content manager's non-writing duties explicitly: user research, promotion, conversion work and measurement.
2. Cap the manager's own writing at a fixed share of output, or remove it entirely once a writer bench exists.
3. Move drafting to freelancers or interviewed experts and keep editing with the manager.
4. Protect calendar time each week for listening to sales calls and for promotion outreach, before drafting time.
5. Judge the manager on leads and conversions from content, not on pieces published.
6. Recruit a second writer before increasing the publishing target, rather than absorbing it into the manager's week.

**Pitfall:** When the manager writes everything, promotion and measurement quietly stop happening and the blog looks productive while doing nothing commercially. The signal is a full publishing calendar with no distribution plan attached to any post.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should keep ownership of research, topic selection and measurement for Pabau content, and push drafting to agents and writers so those higher-value tasks do not get squeezed out.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not let whoever runs content marketing write every post. Keep research, topics, promotion and measurement with them and push drafting to a writer bench.

### 7. Report content ROI as the differentiator that retains clients  `150.15`
*useful · best practices · source 150*

Grow and Convert say solving ROI reporting was one of the first problems they tackled when the agency started, and they were explicit about why. Proving the value of their content would give them a large advantage over other agencies, who typically do not report on ROI at all, and it would let them keep clients long term. That second reason is the operational one. In their experience, once a business can see it is making money from the channel it is content; the hard part is holding buy-in through the months before breakeven, and a chart showing the pace of approach is what does that. The lesson generalizes to in-house teams, where the executive asking about ROI plays the same role a client does.

> "we would a) have a massive advantage over other agencies"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert built ROI reporting as a founding priority because most agencies do not report on it, giving them both a sales advantage and longer client retention.

**How to do it**

1. Set up conversion tracking and the breakeven target during onboarding, not after the first complaint.
2. Put the breakeven number and expected crossing month into the engagement agreement or the internal plan.
3. Send the same conversions-versus-breakeven chart monthly from month one, including the months it looks bad.
4. Name the trough explicitly in the first report so it is a prediction rather than an excuse later.
5. Pair every report with the pace figure: at the current slope, breakeven lands in month N.
6. When a page starts converting, say which keyword and which intent it was, so the strategy is visible.
7. Use the same reporting with internal executives; the retention dynamic is identical.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Google Sheets

**Pitfall:** Introducing ROI reporting only when a client or executive starts questioning results makes it look defensive, and the early months of data will be missing. Set it up before publishing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content function should set the breakeven demo target and the reporting chart before a new cluster starts, so the pre-breakeven months are budgeted rather than defended.

**Apply anywhere:** Set up conversion tracking, the breakeven target and the monthly chart before the first article ships, not after someone questions results. Most teams never report ROI at all, so doing it is both a differentiator and the thing that holds buy-in through the trough.

### 8. Run onboarding as a bootcamp: theory each morning, real client work each afternoon  `46.e3`
*useful · concrete actions · source 46 · universal-edition only*

When Tallent joined, there was no training program at any level. He built a three-month onboarding program for entry-level hires, including flying the cohort into one room, and a lighter check-in-based program for senior hires involving their manager and their manager's manager. The structure of the intensive phase was a split day: mornings ran like a university course on theory, afternoons were workshops where each person had a hands-on assignment on a real site with senior oversight. Trainees also picked a passion-project brand to act as their own mock client. He claims that in four weeks people with no prior SEO background reached the point of running audits better than many practitioners with two to three years' experience, and that the durable skill acquired was interpreting data — including telling a seasonal blip or one-off sale from a real trend.

> "In four weeks' time, you go from knowing very little about SEO ... to being able to run audits better than most other people"

**How to do it**

1. Split the training day: theory instruction in the morning, supervised hands-on assignment in the afternoon.
2. Assign real client work with senior-manager oversight rather than sandbox exercises only.
3. Let each trainee also choose a brand or passion project to run as their own mock client.
4. Expect a morale dip when the technical-SEO block lands in week two, and staff extra support there.
5. Make data interpretation an explicit graded outcome: can they distinguish seasonality and one-off events from a real trend?
6. For senior hires, replace the cohort format with scheduled check-ins involving both their manager and the level above.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Screaming Frog, Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Front-loading theory and deferring hands-on work — the claim here is explicit that reading guides does not produce capability until someone has their hands on a real site.

### 9. Standardize on one project and knowledge platform, then actually invest in it  `46.e8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 46 · universal-edition only*

Tallent describes going two years with no project-management tool at all and fighting to get one adopted, because every sub-team operated differently — so when someone was randomly out sick, nobody on another team could pick up their work and keep going. The fix was aligning the whole organization on a common project-management process plus a shared approach to information gathering and knowledge storage. The team started on Monday and had shifted to Notion by the time he left. He frames platform choice and sustained investment in it as one of the most important things a team can do, and explicitly as 'the not-sexy part of the job' that prevents large amounts of downstream pain.

> "having a platform, and investing in that platform, is one of the most important things that any team can do"

**How to do it**

1. Audit how each sub-team currently tracks work and stores knowledge; expect divergence.
2. Pick one platform for project management and one convention for knowledge storage.
3. Run the 'hit by a bus' test: could someone from another team resume this work cold from what is written down?
4. Migrate teams onto the shared convention rather than allowing per-team variants to persist.
5. Budget ongoing time for maintaining the platform, not just the initial rollout.
6. Revisit the choice deliberately if it stops fitting, rather than letting shadow processes reappear.

**Tools:** Notion, Monday.com

**Pitfall:** Choosing a platform and then under-investing in it — the value comes from everyone genuinely operating inside it, which is unglamorous maintenance work rather than a one-off decision.
