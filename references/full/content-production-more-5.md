### 1. Put promotion spend inside the retainer, not on the client invoice  `182.7`
*useful · best practices · source 182*

Grow and Convert say they run both promotion prongs from their own budget with no extra spend for clients, and call that what makes them a full-service agency rather than a writing supplier. The reason it matters commercially is incentive alignment. When the agency pays for the ads and the link outreach out of the same fee it earns, it stops proposing distribution it does not believe will work, and the client cannot end up funding promotion that only produces impressions. It also removes the negotiation that usually kills promotion, where the agency proposes a media budget, the client refuses it, and everyone reverts to publish-and-wait. The practical version for a buyer is to ask whose budget pays and get the answer in the contract.

> "from our own budget, with no extra spend"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert fund both paid ads and manual link building for clients from their own budget as a stated differentiator.

**How to do it**

1. Ask every shortlisted agency whether promotion spend is inside the fee or billed on top.
2. If it is billed on top, ask for the historical average per article so the real cost of the engagement is visible.
3. Write the answer into the statement of work as a fixed inclusion, not a best-efforts clause.
4. For in-house teams, allocate the promotion budget to the content team's own line so the choice to promote is not a separate approval each time.
5. Review quarterly whether the promotion spend produced leads, and shift it between channels rather than cutting it.

**Pitfall:** An agency that bills promotion separately has no reason to keep it efficient, and a client who approves it case by case will approve it less and less as the novelty fades.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should hold a standing promotion budget inside the content function for pabau.com rather than requesting approval per article, so distribution happens by default on every new piece.

**Apply anywhere:** Fund promotion from the same budget line as production, whether you buy it from an agency or run it in-house, so promoting a piece never needs a separate approval.

### 2. Put the ease promise in the title with a specific step count  `135.17`
*useful · best practices · source 135*

Grow and Convert titled their interview-editing post 'How to Edit an Interview Video in 5 Simple Steps' because ease of use was the differentiator they were selling. The searcher is assumed to be a marketer or researcher without editing experience, so the number in the title is doing intent work: it promises the task is finishable, which is what that reader is uncertain about. The intro then addressed the editors the searcher might already know, explained why those are wrong for someone without experience, and introduced the platform instead. The rule generalizes. When the product's differentiator is speed or simplicity, the title should demonstrate it as a countable number rather than assert it, and the body then has to actually be that many steps.

> "How to Edit an Interview Video in 5 Simple Steps"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert used this title inside a mid-funnel set averaging 1.06% conversion, in a program where 22 of 31 posts reached page one.

**How to do it**

1. Identify the one differentiator the post is selling: speed, simplicity, cost or coverage.
2. If it is speed or simplicity, express it in the title as a countable number of steps or minutes.
3. Count the real steps in your product's workflow for the task before choosing the number.
4. Structure the body as exactly that many H2 sections, one per step, with no hidden sub-steps.
5. Open the intro by naming the tools this searcher already knows and why they are wrong for someone at their experience level.
6. Introduce your product as the route that makes the count possible.
7. Include a screenshot per step so the count is verifiable at a glance.
8. Check the title against the SERP for competitors making the same promise, and go lower on the count only if it is honest.

**Pitfall:** Choosing the number for the headline and then padding the body with sub-steps. The reader counts, sees the gap, and the ease claim that brought them in is the first thing they disbelieve.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau how-to articles about clinic tasks should carry honest step counts in the title where Pabau genuinely shortens the task, with one H2 and one screenshot per step, each screenshot captioned to say how the feature helps.

**Apply anywhere:** When simplicity or speed is your differentiator, put a real step count in the title, structure the body as exactly that many sections, and show one screenshot per step so the promise is verifiable.

### 3. Put your keyword in title, URL, H1, first sentence  `47.5`
*useful · best practices · source 47*

A Reddit comment describing hidden white-text keyword stuffing, repeating target keywords in a large text box colored to match the background, as a supposedly great, long-working SEO tip is a joke circulating in the thread, and Edward explicitly warns newer SEOs not to attempt it. The genuine, non-joke practice he confirms in its place is exact keyword placement: put your target keyword in four specific locations on every page you want to rank, the page title, the URL slug, the H1 heading, and the beginning of the first sentence of body copy, with the meta description as an optional fifth location. This is presented as basic, foundational on-page SEO that still reliably works, in contrast to the fake hidden-text trick.

> "put it in your page title, your URL slug, your H1"

**How to do it**

1. For every page or article you want to rank for a specific target keyword, write the exact keyword, or a very close natural variant, into the page title tag.
2. Include the same target keyword in the page's URL slug.
3. Include the target keyword in the page's H1 heading.
4. Open the first sentence of the page's body copy with the target keyword, rather than easing into the topic indirectly.
5. Where practical, also include the target keyword in the meta description, treating this as optional but recommended.
6. Never use hidden or background-matched-color text to stuff extra keywords onto a page - this is explicitly a joke or scam tactic, not a real technique, and risks a manipulative-content penalty rather than helping rankings.
7. Spot-check a sample of your existing pages against this four-location checklist, title, URL, H1, first sentence, and fix any page missing the keyword in one of them (inferred).

**Pitfall:** Hiding repeated keywords in background-colored text is a joke or scam tactic being passed around as real advice - it is textbook keyword-stuffing cloaking that search engines are built to detect and penalize, not a genuine ranking technique, regardless of how it's framed.

### 4. Rank for template keywords by giving the template away, then displacing it  `86.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 86*

Grow & Convert treat use case and template keywords as a distinct bottom-of-funnel type, narrower than jobs-to-be-done. Searches like 'daily standup meeting template' or 'delivery driver training checklist' show someone trying to solve a specific problem right now. Their recipe is two-part: rank by providing the free template, then use the page to explain how your product makes that template unnecessary or more effective. The template earns the ranking and the link-worthiness; the explanation is what gives an LLM reading the page a reason to name your product when the same task comes up as a prompt. It is the same pattern as their jobs-to-be-done advice, where the ranking content explains how to do the thing manually and then introduces the product as the better route.

> "providing a free template and then explaining how your product makes the template unnecessary"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert's named examples of use case and template keywords: 'daily standup meeting template' and 'delivery driver training checklist'.

**How to do it**

1. List the recurring documents and checklists your buyers build by hand for the workflows your product covers.
2. Check each as a keyword, in both 'template' and 'checklist' forms.
3. Build the actual asset and put it on the page as a free download, not behind a form.
4. Write the section explaining the manual process the template supports.
5. Add a section stating specifically how the product removes or automates that manual step, naming the feature.
6. Link the template page to the matching comparison and category pages so the product claim is reinforced elsewhere.
7. Check the prompt in ChatGPT afterward to see whether the page or the product gets cited for that task.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Template pages that only host the download rank but say nothing about the product, so an LLM reading them learns a format and never learns who you are.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /templates/ pages already carry a download box. Each one needs an explicit section naming the Pabau feature that removes the manual step, so the page earns a product mention and not just a download.

**Apply anywhere:** Template pages need an explicit section naming the product feature that removes the manual step, so the page earns a product mention rather than just a download.

### 5. Read all ten ranking pages plus PAA before outlining anything  `107.8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 107*

Grow and Convert's research and outline step is deliberately manual. For an SEO post you read through each of the top ten Google results properly, not just the titles, and you also scan People Also Ask and Related Searches. You are looking for two things: what all ten results have in common, which is what you must include to rank, and how your post will be more unique or more helpful. Notes taken during that pass become the outline, which you then arrange logically and fill in with missing gaps such as examples backing your arguments and needed background. For originality-led content they follow the same shape but cast wider, looking beyond Google to whatever platform they intend to promote on, naming LinkedIn, Twitter and industry-specific forums. Note the contrast with the title-only scan they recommend at the earlier one-post-or-two decision: that step is cheap on purpose, this one is not.

> "carefully read through each of the top ten results"

**How to do it**

1. Open all ten ranking results for the target keyword and read them, not just skim the headings.
2. List every subtopic that appears in most or all ten; that list is the mandatory coverage.
3. Write down, before drafting, one sentence on how your post will be more useful than each of the top three.
4. Scan People Also Ask and Related Searches and add any question the ten results answer poorly.
5. Arrange the mandatory coverage into a logical order rather than mirroring a competitor's structure.
6. Fill gaps in the outline with the specific examples, data and background you will need to source.
7. For originality-led pieces, also review the target promotion platform, such as LinkedIn or a niche forum, for what gets shared there.

**Pitfall:** Skimming headings gives you the shared subtopics but not the depth level, so the draft matches the outline of the top results while being noticeably thinner than them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should carry both outputs: the shared coverage list from the top ten, and the named originality nugget that makes the piece different. That second line is already a house requirement and this is the process that produces it.

**Apply anywhere:** Read all ten ranking pages in full, note what they share and what they miss, scan People Also Ask and Related Searches, and build the outline from both before writing a word.

### 6. Read result titles and sources first to infer intent before opening any page  `96.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 96*

Grow and Convert point out how much you learn from the SERP without a single click. For 'paid search dashboard' the top results were several product pages from dashboard SaaS competitors, a blog list of dashboard visualization examples from an industry content site, a 'how to create' post from a competitor, and several template product pages. From titles and sources alone they concluded searchers wanted dashboard software products, meaning purchase intent, that let them visualize data, and that they were also interested in how to create dashboards and what templates existed. Noting the sources also tells you who you compete against: direct competitors, news sites, or adjacent products and services. Only after this did they open the pages to study structure, recurring topics and subheadings.

> "without clicking into individual results, we can learn a lot about search intent"

**Evidence:** The 'paid search dashboard' SERP read gave commercial intent plus a secondary how-to and template interest, which shaped a post now at position 2.

**How to do it**

1. Screenshot page one and transcribe every title and domain into a table.
2. Tag each result by page type: product page, template page, how-to, list, guide, news.
3. Tag each domain as direct competitor, adjacent product, industry publisher or news site.
4. Write down what the mix implies about intent, including whether it is commercial or informational.
5. Note any featured snippet and what question it answers, since that is the intent Google surfaced.
6. Only then open the pages to study structure and recurring subheadings.

**Pitfall:** Skipping straight into reading the pages. You end up absorbing the competitors' framing before you have formed an independent read of what the SERP mix says about intent.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's SERP-analysis step should log domain type as well as page type. If aesthetic-industry publishers dominate a term rather than software vendors, that term probably wants an editorial piece, not a product page.

**Apply anywhere:** Read titles and domains first, tag page types and competitor types, and write down the intent that mix implies before opening a single result.

### 7. Read the SERP for a recurring hook, then decide to cover or counter it  `124.15`
*useful · best practices · source 124*

Grow and Convert make SERP analysis one of two mandatory questionnaire sections, and they define it more narrowly than a competitor content audit. The writer analyzes the content ranking on Google and works out why those pieces may be ranking, then says what that implies about search intent. Their example is concrete: if multiple pieces in the SERP highlight free software, it might be worth talking about free software in your post. What is distinctive is the next move. Whether you cover the limits of free software or recommend it for certain customers is decided by the client interview, not by the SERP. So the SERP supplies the topic that must be addressed and the interview supplies the position taken on it. Only after both sections does the writer propose the outline.

> "if multiple pieces in the SERP highlight"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's questionnaire requires a SERP section alongside the audience section, with the free-software example given as the pattern to look for.

**How to do it**

1. Open the top results for the target keyword and read what each one leads with.
2. Write down any angle or qualifier that appears in more than two results, for example free, cheapest or for small teams.
3. Write one sentence on what that repetition implies about intent.
4. Take that recurring hook back to the client interview and ask how the product relates to it.
5. Decide from the interview whether to endorse the hook, qualify it, or argue against it.
6. Give the hook its own section so the piece is not missing what the SERP says readers want.
7. Do not copy the competitors' structure; only their recurring subject matter is the signal.
8. Write the proposed outline only after both the audience and SERP sections are complete.

**Pitfall:** Treating the SERP as the brief and reproducing the consensus article. The SERP tells you which subjects you cannot omit, not what position to take, and taking the consensus position removes the reason to link to you.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau keywords, David should log the recurring SERP hook, often pricing or free options, and then set the Pabau position from the internal interview. Pabau has no free trial, so the position on free tools has to be stated deliberately rather than copied from competitors.

**Apply anywhere:** Note any angle that repeats across the top results and treat it as a subject you must address. Then decide your position on it from your expert interview, not from what the competitors concluded.

### 8. Record expert interviews on Zoom and transcribe them in Otter.ai  `126.16`
*useful · concrete actions · source 126*

Grow and Convert name their exact interview stack in step three of the process: they interview the internal expert, ask the questions prepared from the SERP analysis, record on Zoom and transcribe with Otter.ai. The article shows the Zoom recording screen with Cameron Brown waiting for April, the TapClicks customer success manager, to join. The reason the transcript matters is downstream: the writer works from the expert's own phrasing rather than from a summary, which is how details like the difference between how in-house teams and agencies segment marketing reports survive into the draft. A summarized set of interview notes loses exactly the oddly specific phrasing that makes a passage impossible for a competitor to have written. The recording also settles later disputes about what the expert actually claimed.

> "record (Zoom), and transcribe (Otter.ai)"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show the Zoom recording of Cam's interview with April at TapClicks, the source of the page-one enterprise reporting piece.

**How to do it**

1. Book the interview as a Zoom call and turn on cloud recording at the start.
2. Get verbal confirmation on the recording that the expert agrees to be recorded.
3. Run Otter.ai on the call so you have a searchable transcript within minutes.
4. Store the recording and the transcript with the brief, not in the interviewer's inbox.
5. Have the writer quote the transcript verbatim in the questionnaire's support column.
6. Keep the transcript available for the fact-check pass so claims can be traced to the expert.

**Tools:** Zoom, Otter.ai

**Pitfall:** Taking notes instead of transcribing. Summarized notes lose the specific phrasing that makes a passage unrepeatable, which is the entire point of the interview.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should standardize on recorded and transcribed internal interviews, stored with the article brief. The transcript then doubles as the evidence trail the /fact pass can check claims against.

**Apply anywhere:** Record every expert interview and transcribe it automatically, then store the transcript with the brief. Writers should quote the transcript directly, because summarized notes strip out the specific phrasing that makes a passage original.

### 9. Recruit collaborators from people whose work you already read  `171.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 171*

Sol Orwell needed a research partner and did not go looking for one. He already knew Kurtis Frank as silverhydra, a moderator of r/Fitness who spent 30 to 60 minutes per answer digging through papers. Sol says there was no founderdating needed: Kurtis had seen him posting, knew who he was, and knew he had found some level of success online. The vetting was already done on both sides, in public, over years. That is why Sol could message him an hour after deciding, and why he could tell someone about to start a PhD to drop it. The transferable point is that public work in a community is a continuous, mutual audition, and the best collaborators come out of it pre-qualified.

> "There was no founderdating needed"

**Evidence:** Kurtis Frank, moderator of r/Fitness as silverhydra, became Examine's co-founder after Sol messaged him on Reddit an hour after deciding on the idea.

**How to do it**

1. Keep a running list of the people in your community who answer hard questions well and cite sources.
2. Note how long their answers take and how often they are right, since that is the qualification signal.
3. Contribute publicly yourself so those people can vet you in return; the audition runs both ways.
4. When you need a collaborator, approach the person on the list directly rather than posting a role.
5. Reference the specific work of theirs you have read, which is what makes the approach credible.
6. Be willing to make a large ask, since the shared history is what supports it.
7. Do not substitute a cold hire; the working relationship starts from years of observed output, not an interview.

**Tools:** Reddit

**Pitfall:** Trying to shortcut this by cold-recruiting an expert with credentials but no shared history. Sol could make an outsized ask precisely because Kurtis had watched him work for years.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's expert contributors and case-study partners should come from the practitioners already visible in clinic communities and comment threads, not from a freelance marketplace. David should keep a running shortlist of the people who answer well in public.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep a running shortlist of the people in your community who answer hard questions well, and recruit collaborators from it. Public work is a continuous mutual audition, and it produces better matches than any cold hire or job post.

### 10. Recruit stalled freelance marketers out of marketing Slack communities  `165.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 165*

Hyam names a specific, under-used candidate pool: freelancers in marketing Slack groups who have the skills to execute content marketing but cannot grow their own client base. They join those groups to network and to find clients, and once they have exhausted their own marketing efforts they become receptive to a full-time offer. The pitch is that you are hiring someone with proven delivery ability whose weakness is business development, which is a weakness that does not matter inside your company. He points to a list of Slack communities for SEOs and digital marketers as the starting point. The timing element is the operational part: reach out when someone has been visibly hunting for clients for a while, not the week they announce they have gone independent.

> "struggle growing their own business"

**Evidence:** Hyam identifies Slack communities as a source of skilled marketers who cannot grow their own clientele.

**How to do it**

1. Join three to five marketing and SEO Slack communities and read the client-hunting and introductions channels.
2. Note freelancers who post repeatedly looking for work over several weeks, rather than one-off posts.
3. Check their public work: a blog they grew, case studies, or client results.
4. Message them privately with the role framed around delivery, not business development.
5. Confirm they have built or managed a writer bench, since that is the duty most freelancers have skipped.
6. Time the approach to when their pipeline is clearly thin, and be direct about salary rather than asking them to guess.

**Tools:** Slack

**Pitfall:** Hiring a freelancer whose real problem was delivery, not sales. Ask for two client results they can name before assuming the gap is only business development.

**Apply at Pabau:** David can source Pabau content help from SEO and marketing Slack communities, prioritizing freelancers with healthcare or B2B SaaS clients who are visibly between engagements.

**Apply anywhere:** Watch marketing and SEO Slack communities for capable freelancers who keep hunting for clients. Their weakness is business development, which stops mattering once they are on your payroll.

### 11. Reject drafts that read like a Google research paper  `81.8`
*useful · best practices · source 81*

Grow and Convert name the failure mode that follows from hiring freelance writers with little or no domain expertise: generic content that simply regurgitates what has already been said online. They call it a Google Research Paper. It is not a quality problem in the usual sense, since the writing can be clean and the structure correct. The problem is informational. A page assembled from the other page-one results contains nothing an LLM cannot already get from those results, so it gives the model no reason to cite you and no material with which to describe you as different. Their alternative is to source the substance from internal experts first and treat the writer as the person who shapes it.

> "generic content that simply regurgitates what's already been said online"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert identify this as the common mistake at the drafting stage and tie it directly to LLMs having nothing distinctive to say about the brand.

**How to do it**

1. Require every brief to carry at least three facts that cannot be found on the current page-one results.
2. Source those facts from internal experts, proprietary data or customer conversations before assigning the draft.
3. Give the writer the interview transcript, not a list of competitor URLs, as the research input.
4. On review, highlight every sentence and ask where it came from. If the answer is 'another article', cut it.
5. Reject the draft if a competitor could publish it unchanged by swapping the brand name.
6. Keep the writer if they can interview well, and pair them with an expert rather than replacing them.
7. Track which pages carry proprietary facts and compare their citation rate against the ones that do not.

**Pitfall:** Measuring writers on word count and turnaround. Those metrics select for exactly the summarize-the-SERP behavior that produces uncitable pages.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should require facts from the product and support teams that no competitor can restate, and reviewers should cut any paragraph that only summarizes what already ranks.

**Apply anywhere:** Refuse drafts that only restate what already ranks. Require three facts per brief that do not appear on the current page-one results, sourced from internal experts or customer conversations, and cut any paragraph whose only source is another article.

### 12. Require taste rather than writing ability in a content marketing hire  `165.13`
*useful · best practices · source 165*

Hyam is explicit that writing is not on the skill list for a content marketer. What is required is taste: knowing good content from bad. He goes further and argues it may be better that the person is not a writer, because then they obsess over and excel at marketing rather than writing. The gap is closed by hiring writers and managing them, which is why hiring freelance writers appears as a duty in his job description. This is a sharper version of the general advice to separate the writer role from the marketer role. It gives you an interview instrument: instead of asking for writing samples, ask the candidate to judge content, because taste is observable in how they explain why a piece is good and what they would cut.

> "you need to know good content from bad"

**Evidence:** Hyam names hiring a writer to run content marketing as the number one mistake he sees companies make.

**How to do it**

1. Remove writing samples from the mandatory application requirements for a marketer role.
2. Replace them with a taste exercise: send two articles, one strong and one weak, and ask which is better and why.
3. Score the answer on the reasons given, such as intent match, structure and specificity, rather than the verdict.
4. Ask which three companies do content marketing best and why, as a second read on taste.
5. Confirm the candidate can hire and manage writers, since that is how the writing gets done.
6. Keep an editor in the process if the manager is genuinely not a writer, rather than assuming they will edit well.

**Pitfall:** Hiring the best writer in the pile for a marketing role. Hyam's stated number one mistake is hiring a writer to run content marketing, and it happens because writing samples are the easiest thing to compare.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, test a content hire on judging two live pabau.com articles against a competitor's, not on a writing sample. Keep a named editor in the workflow if the hire is not a writer.

**Apply anywhere:** Test a content marketing candidate on taste, not writing. Send a strong and a weak article and score the reasoning behind their verdict, then confirm they can hire and manage writers.

### 13. Reserve outside-expert interviews for top-funnel guides only  `126.8`
*useful · best practices · source 126*

Grow and Convert are not absolutist about outside experts. They say interviewing a single outside person for a whole piece, or emailing questions to several and sprinkling quotes through, is certainly far better than the writer doing a Google research paper alone. Their view is that it works best for top-of-funnel pieces such as an ultimate guide or a how-to, because those cover the fundamentals of a topic where a range of practitioner views genuinely adds something. Their example: for an ultimate guide to ecommerce supply chain management they would be curious what various supply chain experts say. But they immediately add the caveat that if that guide sits on a supply chain software company's blog and is meant to convert demo requests, it will convert far worse than a bottom-funnel piece like best ecommerce supply chain management software.

> "best for top of the funnel pieces like"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contrast an ultimate guide to ecommerce supply chain management with 'best ecommerce supply chain management software' as the far better converting target.

**How to do it**

1. Classify the piece as top or bottom funnel before deciding who to interview.
2. For top-funnel fundamentals, source three to five outside practitioners by email with the same question set.
3. Attribute each quote and link to the source's site or LinkedIn.
4. Do not use the piece to carry product positioning; keep the tie-in light.
5. Set the conversion expectation low for the piece and measure it on assisted reach, not demos.
6. Spend the majority of your budget on bottom-funnel pieces with internal interviews instead.

**Pitfall:** Judging a top-funnel expert round-up by demo conversions. It will lose to a bottom-funnel piece every time, which is an argument against the topic choice, not against the method.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau runs an expert round-up, it should be on genuinely top-funnel clinical or business-of-aesthetics topics, and it should not be expected to drive demos. Keep the demo pages on bottom-funnel software comparisons with internal input.

**Apply anywhere:** Use outside expert round-ups only for top-funnel fundamentals, email the same question set to several practitioners, attribute each, and do not expect the piece to convert. Keep bottom-funnel product-adjacent topics on internal interviews.

### 14. Run a JTBD worksheet with the team before any keyword is briefed  `75.10`
*useful · concrete actions · source 75*

The author publishes a JTBD worksheet and tells readers to steal it and use it with their team, then shows a filled-out example built from the Qualtrics NPS content. The worksheet is the mechanism that connects the theory to the calendar: it forces the team to answer what the searcher's job is, what content would help them achieve it, and what experience would serve them most, before anyone writes. He also uses it as the input to the underserved-keyword check, filling it in first and then going to the SERP to see which keywords present a gap. His three framing questions are the columns to use: circumstances rather than demographics, problems with inadequate solutions, and jobs that are social and emotional as well as functional.

> "Steal this worksheet to use with your team"

**Evidence:** The author's published worksheet plus a filled-out example built from the Qualtrics NPS calculator content; he also names a Figma board worksheet for identifying jobs to be done.

**How to do it**

1. Book a 45-minute session with the writer, an SEO and someone who talks to customers, before the brief is written.
2. Write the target keyword at the top and the solution-agnostic problem statement under it.
3. Fill a column on the searcher's circumstances, what is happening around them when they search, not who they are.
4. Fill a column on what content would help them achieve the outcome, listing formats not headings.
5. Fill a column on what experience would serve them most, including reading level and how much they already know.
6. Note the social and emotional part of the job as well as the functional one, for example not wanting to look uninformed.
7. Take the completed sheet to the SERP and mark which of the top 10 delivers what the sheet describes.
8. Attach the sheet to the brief so the writer and the reviewer judge the draft against it.

**Tools:** Figma

**Pitfall:** Filling the sheet after the outline is written, which turns it into justification rather than input. The tell is that the sheet never changes the chosen format.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a short JTBD worksheet stage to the Pabau brief process, between keyword selection and outline approval, with one line each on circumstances, helpful content, desired experience and the emotional part of the job.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a short JTBD worksheet with the writer, an SEO and someone who talks to customers before the outline exists. Cover circumstances, helpful content formats, the desired experience and the emotional side of the job, then check the SERP against it.

### 15. Run a two-way lunch and learn where each team explains its month  `177.3`
*useful · concrete actions · source 177*

The author's first fix for the mutual-ignorance problem is a lunch and learn, but he specifies both directions. Marketing walks through what it will be doing over the coming month: the strategy, the reasoning behind each campaign, and the systems involved if that helps. Sales then presents from their side, covering which channels are performing best, what actually happens inside their phone conversations, and what is working and not working. The point is not team bonding. He had just heard a salesperson answer the question of where their leads come from with the name of a marketing automation tool, which he treats as evidence that sales often has no model of how demand is created. A team that does not know where leads come from cannot give useful feedback on them.

> "Hold a lunch and learn that walks through"

**Evidence:** A salesperson asked where most of his leads came from answered with the name of his marketing automation system rather than a channel.

**How to do it**

1. Schedule a monthly lunch and learn with both teams in the room, one hour.
2. Have marketing present the month's plan: the strategy, the reasoning per campaign, and the systems in use.
3. Include the actual acquisition channels by name, so sales can answer where a lead came from.
4. Have sales present second: best-performing channels, what happens on calls, what is and is not working.
5. Close with a written list of the disagreements surfaced, and who owns each.
6. Test comprehension the following month by asking a rep where their last three leads came from.
7. Repeat monthly rather than running it once at onboarding.

**Pitfall:** Running the session one-way, with marketing presenting to sales, turns it into a status update nobody retains. The signal is that no disagreement surfaces in the meeting.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should walk Pabau's sales team through which articles and which queries produce demo requests, using names of pages rather than channel labels. Reps who can name the article a prospect read can pick the conversation up where the content left off.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a monthly two-way lunch and learn: marketing presents the plan and the actual acquisition channels, then sales presents call detail and channel performance. Test afterward whether a rep can name where their last three leads came from.

### 16. Run cold and lookalike paid audiences on a per-client channel test  `182.8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 182*

Grow and Convert's short-term promotion prong is paid ads with two targeting methods. The first is cold audiences built from interest and demographic targeting. The second is lookalike audiences built from the client's existing customer list or website visitors. They do not commit to a network in advance: they test Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn and Google Ads per client, choosing by where that client's audience is most likely reachable. The role of the paid prong is explicitly a short-term traffic boost while the piece waits for search to work, paired with the link-building prong that produces the long-term line. The combination is what gives a piece traffic in month one instead of month six.

> "lookalike audiences (based on the client's existing customer list"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run this on client work and describe the paid prong as producing the short-term traffic that precedes sustainable organic traffic.

**How to do it**

1. Upload the customer list and the website visitor audience to each network you intend to test and build lookalikes from both.
2. Build a parallel cold audience from interest and demographic targeting for the same piece.
3. Run the same article to both audience types on two networks first, with equal budget, for a fixed window.
4. Compare cost per engaged visitor and cost per conversion between the four cells, not clicks.
5. Kill the losing network and reallocate to the winner rather than spreading budget across all four.
6. Keep the paid push running only until organic traffic to the piece exceeds paid traffic, then move the budget to the next article.

**Tools:** Facebook Ads, LinkedIn Ads, Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Choosing the network by reputation rather than by test. LinkedIn is the default assumption for B2B and is frequently the most expensive cell in the test.

**Apply at Pabau:** For pabau.com the audience is reachable on Facebook and Instagram, where aesthetics practice owners already gather, so test those against LinkedIn per piece rather than assuming a B2B network.

**Apply anywhere:** Promote each new article to both a cold interest audience and a lookalike built from your customer list, test two networks head to head, and keep only the cheaper one per conversion.

### 17. Run the two-step: customer research first, then back into keywords  `110.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 110*

Grow and Convert lay out their pain point SEO process in two steps and refuse to reverse them. Step one is customer research, which answers two questions: who are the best customers, and what are their pain points. Step two is building a bottom-up content strategy off that research, using the pain points to back into which keywords and topics will drive conversions quickly. The ordering matters because keyword tools return volume, not intent, and a list built from volume cannot tell you which terms your best customers actually search when they have a problem. Grow and Convert say they run this live for four real companies in their course, interviewing the founding team with their customer research questions and producing pain point SEO ideas in a spreadsheet.

> "It All Starts With Customer Research"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run this process live for four companies in their course, interviewing founding teams and producing pain point SEO ideas in a shared spreadsheet.

**How to do it**

1. Define best customers by revenue and retention, not by count, before interviewing anyone.
2. Interview the founding team and customer-facing staff with a fixed set of research questions.
3. Record the pain points verbatim in the customer's own words.
4. Group the pain points and rank them by how directly your product solves them.
5. For each pain point, search the phrasing customers used and collect the matching keywords.
6. Put each keyword in a spreadsheet alongside its pain point and best-customer fit.
7. Discard any keyword that cannot be traced back to a recorded pain point.
8. Sequence the spreadsheet by buying intent and publish from the top.

**Tools:** Google Sheets

**Pitfall:** Starting in a keyword tool and retrofitting pain points to the terms that came back. The output looks the same in a spreadsheet but the intent filter is missing, and conversion rates land at blog baseline.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should interview Pabau's sales and onboarding staff for practice-owner pain points before the next keyword pull, and record them verbatim. Every pabau.com keyword in the plan should trace back to one of those recorded pain points.

**Apply anywhere:** Run customer research before keyword research. Interview founders and customer-facing staff, record pain points verbatim, then search the customers' own phrasing and keep only keywords that trace back to a recorded pain point.

### 18. Run three research methods at once rather than a survey alone  `161.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 161*

Khanal describes what he actually did at ThinkApps rather than what he planned. He met past customers in person and by phone to ask open-ended questions, surveyed past customers through Survey Monkey, and installed Qualaroo on the site to catch visitors while they were browsing. Running all three at once is the point. Interviews give depth and follow-up questions, the survey gives enough volume to see patterns, and the on-site tool reaches prospects who never became customers and who a customer list cannot contact. He says the patterns only became visible once answers from all three were read together, and those patterns then informed every decision the company made going forward.

> "I started surveying past customers through Survey Monkey"

**Evidence:** At ThinkApps, Khanal ran in-person and phone interviews, a Survey Monkey survey and Qualaroo on-site simultaneously, and reported finding patterns that informed all subsequent decision making.

**How to do it**

1. Book five to ten in-person or phone conversations with past customers and ask open-ended questions with no script beyond the seven themes.
2. Send the same seven questions as a Survey Monkey or Typeform survey to the full past-customer list.
3. Install an on-site micro-survey tool such as Qualaroo and target it at high-intent pages like pricing and demo.
4. Ask on-site visitors one question only, such as what is stopping you from signing up today.
5. Pool all three sources into one sheet with a column marking the method.
6. Look for themes that appear in all three sources; those are the ones to act on first.
7. Note themes that appear only on-site, since those are prospect objections your customers never had.
8. Rerun the interviews annually and leave the on-site survey running continuously.

**Tools:** Survey Monkey, Qualaroo, Typeform

**Pitfall:** Relying on customer surveys alone means you only hear from people who already bought. The objections that kill deals never appear, because the people holding them are not on your customer list.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should pair the customer survey with an on-site micro-survey on the pricing and book-demo pages asking what is stopping the visitor from booking. Those answers belong in the FAQ blocks of those pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Run interviews, a list survey and an on-site micro-survey at the same time, and act first on the themes that show up in all three.

### 19. Run two different SERP scans: one minute at research, a deep pass before writing  `108.5`
*useful · best practices · source 108*

Grow and Convert separate SERP work into two stages with very different budgets, which most teams collapse into one. At the keyword research stage the scan is a quick check, a minute or two per keyword, just to verify buying intent is present. At the content creation stage the same SERP gets a much deeper analysis before a word is written. They are also blunt that stopping at high-level intent labels like 'commercial' or 'informational' is nowhere near enough. You need the specific layers of intent for that keyword, which only come from reading what ranks and working out why Google chose those particular results. Splitting the two stages keeps research fast enough to cover a large keyword list while still giving every commissioned article a proper analysis.

> "a minute or two per keyword just to verify buying intent"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated split: one to two minutes per keyword at research, a much deeper documented analysis at the content creation stage.

**How to do it**

1. At the research stage, open each candidate's SERP for no more than two minutes.
2. Record only one thing: whether the ranking pages indicate a buyer or a browser.
3. Move on without reading the articles; the deep read comes later.
4. Once a keyword is approved for production, reopen the SERP and budget a full session.
5. Record the dominant page type across the top ten results.
6. Click into the top three to five results and list every subtopic they share.
7. Write down what none of them covers, and what your piece will add.
8. Note the authority profile of the ranking domains so effort and link budget can be set before writing.
9. Refuse to brief the article until those four notes exist in writing.

**Pitfall:** Doing the deep analysis during research burns weeks on keywords you never commission. Doing only the quick scan before writing produces articles that match the intent label but not the format Google wants.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO flow should keep the keyword-selection SERP check short and push the four-part analysis into the brief stage, so a large candidate list can be screened without slowing the writer down.

**Apply anywhere:** Use two SERP passes. A one-to-two minute scan during keyword research to confirm buying intent, and a full analysis of format, subtopics, gaps and ranking domains before any article is briefed.

### 20. Run two or three format tests per client so luck has somewhere to land  `136.17`
*useful · concrete actions · source 136*

Grow and Convert are explicit that the accidental pain point posts existed because they were always testing, and that when you test, sometimes you manufacture your own luck. They were running tests on promotion tactics and content types throughout the engagement, and one of those tests, a comparison post and a category list repeated from an earlier client, became the whole strategy. They also draw a line: testing does not mean random guessing. Every test had a reason behind it, and the reason came from their research process, specifically the question about which other tools a customer was evaluating. So the discipline is a small number of reasoned format tests running under the main program at all times, with per-article conversion tracking so an outlier is visible when it happens.

> "sometimes you manufacture your own luck"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's comparison and category-list tests at Leadfeeder converted around 10x better than the main program and became pain point SEO; two May 2018 marketing automation posts tested and failed.

**How to do it**

1. Reserve one or two slots per month in the calendar for format tests outside the main plan.
2. Derive each test from a specific research finding, not from a hunch about what might work.
3. Repeat a format that worked for another client or another segment rather than inventing a new one.
4. Track conversions per article so an outlier with low traffic is visible in the report.
5. Give each test at least three to six months before judging it, since ranking takes that long.
6. When a test outperforms by 5x or more, stop testing and scale it into the main plan.
7. Kill any format that produces no conversions after four attempts, as they did with the marketing automation stack topics.
8. Keep a written log of every test, its reasoning and its result, so the reasoning survives staff changes.

**Pitfall:** Running tests without per-article conversion tracking. The winner is usually a low-traffic page, so it shows up as a failure in a traffic report and the finding gets thrown away.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep one or two experimental article slots a month outside the main brief queue, each derived from a specific customer or sales finding, and review them against demo bookings per article rather than traffic.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep one or two reasoned format tests running under the main content plan at all times, each derived from a real research finding. Track conversions per article so the winner, usually a low-traffic page, is visible instead of being read as a failure.

### 21. Screen separately for product selling ability when content must sell  `164.14`
*useful · best practices · source 164*

Grow and Convert draw a line between ghostwriting opinion or thought leadership and writing content intended to sell a product or service. The second requires understanding and expressing competitive advantages, value propositions, benefits and features, which they call a unique skill set akin to product copywriting that many freelance writers simply do not possess. Because their own focus is bottom-of-funnel product content ranking for terms like best accounting software or Google Analytics alternatives, their portfolio review asks specifically whether a writer can contrast value propositions clearly and communicate differentiators between two products persuasively. They accept that an applicant will rarely have a piece that is exactly what you would publish; the test is whether the writing shows signs they could write like you, not whether they already have.

> "a unique skill set, akin to product copywriting"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert focus on bottom-of-funnel keywords like 'best accounting software' and screen every portfolio for the ability to contrast value propositions.

**How to do it**

1. Decide first whether the content is meant to sell a product or to build thought leadership.
2. If it sells, add one explicit screening criterion: can this writer contrast two products' value propositions clearly?
3. Look in the portfolio for a passage that names a differentiator and explains why it matters, not for polished prose.
4. Point the mini sample question at a real product page so selling ability is tested directly.
5. Do not require an exact match to your own output; look for signs the writer could write like you after coaching.
6. Reject writers whose product passages restate feature lists without saying what the feature lets the reader do.

**Pitfall:** Hiring on general writing talent for bottom-funnel work. Grow and Convert say this skill is unique and often absent, and the symptom is well-written articles that never explain why one product beats another.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's comparison and alternatives pages live or die on this skill. Screen writers with a paragraph contrasting Pabau against a named competitor before commissioning any listicle.

**Apply anywhere:** If your content has to sell a product, screen writers explicitly on their ability to contrast two products' value propositions. That skill is closer to product copywriting than to content writing, and general writing talent does not imply it.

### 22. Sell the product inside every ranking article, not just on landing pages  `163.15`
*useful · best practices · source 163*

Grow and Convert describe what they actually do on a page built for a high-buying-intent keyword, using examples like 'best accounting software' and 'concussion clinic in utah'. They sell the client's product or service in every piece of content they produce: getting into the details of features, explaining how those features solve customer pain points, weaving in testimonials and case studies, and differentiating the client's product from competitors. This is the practical output of the internal-interview process, and it is why they argue a writer without product expertise cannot serve bottom-funnel topics. The hiring test follows from it. Ask a candidate to show a ranking article that does all four things at once: features, pain points, proof, and named differentiation. Most SEO content samples do none of them.

> "we sell our client's"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert apply this to every piece across dozens of client accounts, with buying-intent examples spanning B2B software and local healthcare services.

**How to do it**

1. For every bottom-funnel article, plan four elements: specific features, the pain point each solves, a testimonial or case study, and a named differentiator versus competitors.
2. Source the feature detail and the pain points from an interview with product and customer success, not from the marketing site.
3. Pull a real customer quote or case study result into the piece rather than a generic claim.
4. State the differentiation against named competitors, not against an unnamed 'other tools'.
5. Keep the article answering the search query first, so the selling sits inside a genuinely useful page.
6. Use the same four-element checklist to grade any writing sample from a candidate or agency.

**Pitfall:** Selling only in a closing CTA block while the body reads like a neutral overview. The page ranks and passes traffic on to a competitor's comparison article.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's bottom-funnel and listicle pages should carry feature detail tied to a practice pain point, a customer proof point, and explicit differentiation, ahead of the standard Pabau section before the Conclusion.

**Apply anywhere:** Bottom-funnel and listicle pages should carry feature detail tied to a real user pain point, a customer proof point, and explicit differentiation, not just a closing CTA.
