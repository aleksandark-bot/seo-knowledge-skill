### 1. Open with a contrarian claim, then promise to fix it  `127.5`
*core · best practices · source 127*

Grow and Convert analyze a good intro on the competitive 'how to lose weight' term and name the two-part structure that makes it work. The first move is a contrarian view stated immediately: most of the advice out there lacks any scientific evidence. The second sentence promises this post will solve that problem. They say the combination shows the author has some know-how on the topic and sets the first impression that this is not going to be the same old regurgitated nonsense. The reader-side mechanism is explicit: a searcher opening several results at once gets an immediate sense of originality, thinking 'Ok this is the one with the strategies backed by science.' That is what grabs attention and convinces the reader a great article follows. Two sentences do the whole job.

> "immediately there is a contrarian view presented"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite a ranking 'how to lose weight' article whose opening claims most existing strategies lack scientific evidence, then promises science-backed ones.

**How to do it**

1. Write one sentence naming what is wrong or missing in the existing advice on this topic, based on what actually ranks.
2. Make the criticism checkable, for example 'most of it cites no evidence', not 'most of it is bad'.
3. Write a second sentence promising that this post fixes exactly that gap.
4. Put those two sentences as the first two lines of the article.
5. Confirm the body delivers the promise, since the opener is now a commitment.
6. Test the pair by imagining the reader has your page plus three competitors open, and ask what one-line label they would give yours.
7. Reuse that same label in the title and the meta description so the SERP snippet and the intro agree.

**Pitfall:** A contrarian opener the body does not deliver on is worse than a generic one, because the reader arrives expecting the evidence and finds the same regurgitated advice. Only make the claim you can substantiate in the piece.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles should open by naming what the existing aesthetics-industry advice gets wrong, then promising the fix. On a no-show article: most published advice stops at reminder texts, and here is what deposit policy data actually shows.

**Apply anywhere:** Open with one checkable sentence about what existing advice on the topic gets wrong, then a second sentence promising your piece fixes that. A searcher with four tabs open needs a one-line reason to pick yours, and this gives them one.

### 2. Optimize for time to value, because instant usability lifts time on page  `75.6`
*core · content insights · source 75*

The author's strongest evidence is webwhiteboard.com, run by A Web Whiteboard. The keyword "web whiteboard" has 51,000 traffic potential and AWW takes all of it, beating Miro. In the author's interview, AWW's founder explains that Miro's SEO team studied the page with them and reached two conclusions. First, the product was built around extreme simplicity, and interviews showed simplicity was the single thing people loved. Second, because time to value is so fast, the whole site is an instantly usable whiteboard, every visitor plays with it, so time on page is naturally high. The founder adds that Google themselves told them not to focus on SEO but on the user. The author's reading is that serving the job faster, measured in time to value, is what wins the SERP.

> "Because the time to value is so fast"

**Evidence:** webwhiteboard.com holds the keyword web whiteboard with 51,000 traffic potential against Miro; the founder attributes it to extreme simplicity and fast time to value producing naturally high time on page.

**How to do it**

1. Measure the seconds between landing on the page and the visitor being able to do the job, with no signup, no tour and no scrolling.
2. Remove every step between arrival and first use, including account creation, cookie walls above the tool and explanatory preamble.
3. Put the usable thing at the top of the page rather than describing it.
4. Cut features until the one job the keyword names is the only thing on the page.
5. Interview users on what they actually like, and expect the answer to be simplicity rather than a feature.
6. Track average engagement time on the page as the proxy for time to value, and treat a rise as the leading signal.
7. Resist adding upsell modules that delay first use, since they reverse the mechanism.
8. Compare your time to value against the incumbent's before assuming you cannot outrank a larger brand.

**Tools:** Miro

**Pitfall:** Adding onboarding, signup or feature tours ahead of first use. The visitor never reaches value, engagement time drops, and the page loses the only advantage a small site had over the category leader.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau cannot make its whole product instantly usable, but individual pages can be. On template pages, put the usable or downloadable asset above the fold instead of after 800 words, and watch average engagement time as the check.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat time to value as a ranking input on tool and template pages. Put the usable asset at the top, strip every step between arrival and first use, and track average engagement time as the confirming signal.

### 3. Overrule the objection to naming competitors in your own list posts  `146.7`
*core · best practices · source 146*

Grow and Convert say many companies are hesitant or unwilling to write list posts because they do not want to draw attention to competitors. Their position is that the ranking advantage outweighs that cost. Done well, list posts rank and convert, and they help the sales process by stating your key value props against competitors up front. Their closing argument is the decisive one: if people are Googling a product keyword and being shown lists of products, and you are not in that conversation, you are leaving those conversions and customers on the table. Someone else's list is deciding your positioning for you. If listing products beyond your own is the price of a top spot on a high-converting keyword, they consider it more than worth paying.

> "you're just leaving those conversions and customers on the table"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report list posts done well are very effective for ranking and producing conversions across their client base.

**How to do it**

1. When a stakeholder blocks a list post over naming competitors, show them the current SERP for the keyword and who controls the list today.
2. Point out that the comparison is already being published by third parties, without your framing in it.
3. Agree the editorial rules up front: real competitors, accurate descriptions, no invented weaknesses.
4. Write your own entry to state the value props you want carried into sales calls.
5. Publish and measure position plus conversions, then bring both numbers back to the objector.
6. Reuse the competitor framing in sales enablement so the post pays back twice.

**Pitfall:** Softening the list until competitors are barely described. The post loses the comparison intent it was written for and stops ranking, which then gets read as proof that list posts do not work.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's listicles already name competitors, and David should treat that as non-negotiable when a stakeholder pushes back, using the SERP as the argument.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat naming competitors in your listicles as non-negotiable when a stakeholder pushes back, and use the current SERP as the argument.

### 4. Position a core feature as a reason to buy, never as an optional perk  `92.6`
*core · best practices · source 92*

Grow and Convert call this the subtlest of the four mistakes, because it only happens once you already have the right value prop. HubSpot's roundup introduced automatic translation with the phrase if you're short on time, you can opt for the Translate Everything mode. The feature is correct and it is one of the customers' biggest pain points, but the framing makes it sound like a heated cupholder in a luxury car: nice if you happen to need it. Nobody buys the car for the cupholder. Automatic translation is a reason a buyer picks one plugin over another, so framing it as a time-saver for busy people undersells it. The same post positioned another tool's human translation as being for people who want to start simple but grow into more complex language implementations. Grow and Convert reject the growth framing: the real value of human translation is accuracy, and a five-page site may need extremely accurate copy for legal reasons. Positioning a feature by company size when it is really about accuracy loses buyers who need accuracy now.

> "like a heated cupholder in a luxury car"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's suggested rewrite for the Lingotek positioning: these three tiers let brands pair the speed and automation of machine translation with the accuracy of human translation, depending on the accuracy levels they need.

**How to do it**

1. Find every sentence introducing a feature you know is a top-three pain point.
2. Delete conditional openers such as if you're short on time, if needed, for those who want, and should you require.
3. Rewrite each so the sentence names the pain the feature removes, in the customer's words.
4. Check whether any feature is framed by company size or maturity; if so, ask what the feature is actually about.
5. Replace maturity framing with the real axis, such as accuracy, speed or compliance.
6. Test the rewritten line against the reaction you want: this solves my exact problem, not let me see what else is out there.
7. Do this pass separately from the fluff pass, because these sentences are grammatical and accurate.

**Pitfall:** These lines survive every review because they are technically accurate. Grow and Convert's phrase is that the copy is so close to being good, which is why nobody flags it and the conversion loss is invisible.

**Apply at Pabau:** Audit Pabau's product sections for perk framing. Lines like if you want to reduce no-shows, you can enable reminders should be rewritten to lead with the no-show cost, since reminders are a reason practices switch systems, not an optional extra.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your product sections for perk framing. Conditional openers turn a core buying reason into an optional extra; rewrite them to lead with the cost of the problem the feature removes.

### 5. Proprietary advantage is the durability test: what would it take to reproduce this?  `66.8`
*core · content insights · source 66*

Proprietary advantage measures how difficult the content would be to reproduce without the organization's data, access, expertise, systems, customer relationships or operating context. It is the framework's durability dimension, and it maps almost perfectly onto the prioritize/deprioritize split: every one of the thirteen prioritized content types scores Medium or High, and every one of the eight deprioritized types scores Low. That correlation is the framework's real thesis in one column — content that anyone with a writer and public sources can reproduce is precisely the content an AI system can also reproduce, and its exposure is structural rather than a matter of execution. The six listed asset classes also double as a checklist of what to attach to a page to raise its score: your data, your access, your expertise, your systems, your customer relationships, or your operating context.

> "how difficult the content would be to reproduce without the organization's data, access, expertise, systems, customer"

**Evidence:** Across the worksheet's twenty-one scored types, proprietary advantage is Medium or High for all thirteen prioritized types and Low for all eight deprioritized ones — the only dimension that separates the two lists without exception.

**Apply at Pabau:** Use the six asset classes as a commissioning checklist: before Pabau publishes, name which one the page draws on — platform data, customer access, clinical or operational expertise, the product itself, practice relationships, or Pabau's operating context. A page that names none is a page a competitor can copy in an afternoon.

**Apply anywhere:** Use the six asset classes as a commissioning checklist: before publishing, name which one the page draws on — your data, your customer access, your expertise, your systems, your customer relationships, or your operating context. A page that names none is a page a competitor can copy in an afternoon.

### 6. Publish A-B test case studies on single UI elements as linkable assets  `125.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Alongside the public-data research post, Grow and Convert note that two of Growth Rock's other top-linked pages are A-B testing case studies built on the company's own client data. One covers the navigation menu, the other a sticky add to cart button. Each earned a nontrivial number of external links, which they treat as the key indicator that a piece has thought leadership in it. The pattern worth copying is the scope: each case study is about one element, not about a whole redesign or a whole engagement. A single element with a measured result is specific enough to cite, and other writers covering that element have nothing else to link to. Grow and Convert use external link counts as their working test of whether a piece qualifies as thought leadership at all.

> "one on the navigation menu and the other on what's called a "sticky add to cart button."

**Evidence:** Two of Growth Rock's top eleven pages by links are single-element A-B testing case studies, on the navigation menu and on a sticky add to cart button, both with nontrivial external link counts.

**How to do it**

1. Pick one interface element you have tested, not a whole page or redesign.
2. Write down the variant, the control, the sample size and the measured lift.
3. Publish the result even when it is small or negative, since the number is what gets cited.
4. Name the element in the title using the phrase practitioners actually use for it.
5. Show the screenshots of both variants so the test is reproducible by a reader.
6. Explain why you expected the result and why the data disagreed.
7. Track external links to the page in Ahrefs and treat that count as the test of whether the piece earned its place.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Bundling several tests into one roundup kills the citation value, because a writer covering one element will not link to a page that is mostly about other elements.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can publish single-change results from its own product analytics, such as what happened to no-shows after a specific reminder setting change, one element per article. Those pages will earn links that generic practice management guides do not.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish A-B test case studies scoped to one interface element, with control, variant, sample size and lift. One element per article, and judge success by external links earned.

### 7. Publish aggregate time-to-rank data across your whole client base  `125.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert's second data example is a post analyzing 20 active and former clients to answer two questions: how long it took to rank articles on page one and in the top three for their intended keyword, and how many leads were generated over the first several months of each engagement. They present findings that surprised them and explain their evolving thinking behind those findings rather than only reporting the numbers. They say the piece is unique in its transparency and in showing data most agencies do not publish, and that they have not seen anything else like it. The mechanism is simple: any agency or vendor with more than a handful of accounts is sitting on benchmark data the whole market wants and nobody publishes, and publishing it is the cheapest defensible originality available.

> "an analysis we've done on 20 active and former clients"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's time-to-rank post analyzing 20 active and former clients on time to page one, time to top three, and leads generated over the first several months.

**How to do it**

1. Pull every account you have run, including former ones, and stop at no fewer than 20 for a stable benchmark.
2. Define two questions the market wants answered, such as time to page one and leads in the first months.
3. Record the answer per account with the start date and the target keyword.
4. Anonymize client names but keep industry and starting authority, since those explain the spread.
5. Report the distribution, not just the average, and call out the outliers.
6. Write up the findings that surprised you and say what you now believe instead.
7. State the methodology and sample size in the piece so it can be cited.
8. Refresh the analysis annually with new accounts so the page stays the current benchmark.

**Pitfall:** Publishing only averages hides the spread and invites disputes from anyone whose experience differs. Also, the data can be unflattering, and the piece dies internally if nobody has agreed in advance to publish the real numbers.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau holds anonymized onboarding and usage data across many practices. David could publish a benchmark on how long a practice takes to reach full booking adoption, or typical no-show rates by treatment type, with the sample size stated.

**Apply anywhere:** Aggregate results across at least 20 accounts into a published benchmark: how long things took, what the outcomes were, the distribution rather than the average, and the findings that surprised you.

### 8. Publish head-to-head vendor comparisons to outrank review aggregators  `128.19`
*core · concrete actions · source 128*

Grow and Convert rank first for "Monetate vs. Certona" for their client Inflow, an agency in the personalization space, against Gartner, TrustRadius and Capterra. The pattern is repeatable because of what aggregators can and cannot do. A review platform generates a comparison page for every pair of vendors in its database from structured fields and user ratings, so the page exists but nobody has actually used both products and written about the difference. A practitioner who has implemented both can say which one fits which situation, and that is the answer the searcher wanted. The keyword is also as bottom-funnel as it gets, since somebody comparing two named vendors is in a live evaluation. The requirement is real hands-on experience with both, which is exactly what the aggregators lack.

> "comparing two personalization platforms"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert rank number one for "Monetate vs. Certona" for Inflow against Gartner, TrustRadius and Capterra.

**How to do it**

1. List the vendor pairs your customers and prospects actually evaluate against each other, from sales calls and support tickets.
2. Check the SERP for each "A vs B" term and note whether the results are aggregator template pages with generic feature grids.
3. Pick pairs where someone in your organization or network has genuinely used both.
4. Interview that person for an hour and build the outline from which product fits which situation.
5. Structure the page around decision scenarios, not a feature matrix, since the matrix is what the aggregators already have.
6. State a clear recommendation per scenario, including when neither is the right answer.
7. Include your own product only where it honestly belongs, and disclose the relationship.
8. Add the term to a rank tracker and expect a few weeks of position testing before it settles.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Answering an "A vs B" query with a feature table. That is the aggregators' own format and they have more structured data than you, so you lose on their terms instead of competing on hands-on judgment they cannot supply.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish head-to-head comparisons of the practice management systems its prospects actually weigh against each other, built from an hour with someone who has migrated practices off both, and structured around scenarios rather than feature grids.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish head-to-head "A vs B" comparisons for the vendor pairs your prospects actually evaluate, built on an hour with someone who has used both. Structure them around decision scenarios with a clear recommendation per scenario, not a feature matrix, since that format is where review aggregators already win.

### 9. Publish official documentation as the current source of record  `66.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 66*

Content that publishes authoritative facts only the organization can provide: product or service requirements, feature behavior, compatibility, pricing rules, policies, warranties and release changes. Its role is to be the current source of record — explicitly not to persuade, compare options or troubleshoot every possible context. It covers release notes, documentation, compatibility matrices, policy and warranty pages, specifications, eligibility rules and official answers within maintained hubs. It scores High on citation potential, brand mention potential, business value and proprietary advantage at Medium effort, with Medium click resilience. The asymmetry is the reason to build it: an AI system will answer 'does it support X?' from your documentation whether or not the user clicks, so the documentation's accuracy determines the accuracy of the answer. Documentation is where the framework's citation and brand-mention returns are cheapest to earn, because the facts already exist internally and only need publishing in a maintained hub.

> "Its role is to be the current source of record"

**How to do it**

1. Inventory the factual questions support and sales answer repeatedly — requirements, compatibility, limits, what a feature actually does, what a policy covers, what changed in the last release.
2. Give each a documentation page inside one maintained hub, rather than scattering the answers into blog posts that will not be updated.
3. Write in the register of a record: state the fact, its scope, its exceptions and its effective date. Cut the persuasion — this type is scored on being current and complete, not convincing.
4. Build compatibility and requirements as tables, so both readers and AI systems can extract a specific row rather than parse prose.
5. Publish release notes on every release with a version, a date and the behavior that changed, since 'what changed in version X' is one of the type's target prompts.
6. Date-stamp every page with a visible last-reviewed date, and make the date real by assigning an owner per page.
7. Wire the hub's internal hierarchy so each documentation page links to the policy, specification and troubleshooting page it depends on.
8. Route the neighbouring jobs elsewhere: send comparison questions to the comparison pages and environment-specific failures to the troubleshooting guides, and link out to them rather than absorbing them.
9. Re-audit against the product on a release cadence, treating a stale documentation page as a defect rather than a content backlog item.

**Tools:** Schema.org, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Letting documentation drift behind the product. This type's entire value is being the current record, so a stale specification is worse than no specification — it becomes the fact an AI system confidently repeats about you, and it is the one error you cannot blame on a third party.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's feature documentation is a citation and brand-mention asset, not just a support cost. Publish what each feature does, its requirements and its limits in a maintained hub with visible review dates — and keep it accurate on the point that every Pabau subscription includes every feature, since that is exactly the kind of fact AI systems get wrong about software pricing.

**Apply anywhere:** Feature documentation is a citation and brand-mention asset, not just a support cost. Publish what each feature does, its requirements and its limits in a maintained hub with visible review dates — and keep it accurate on how plans and inclusions work, since that is exactly the kind of fact AI systems get wrong about software pricing.

### 10. Push the expert to categorize the symptom into named types  `117.4`
*core · content insights · source 117*

Dr. Loewen broke post-concussion headaches into six distinct types, and could sometimes link each type back to its likely cause. None of the existing articles ranking for the term acknowledged that split, including Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic and WebMD. That taxonomy became the layer of information that differentiated the article. Grow and Convert also got mechanisms out of her that a generalist would not have reached: autonomic nervous system dysfunction, neurovascular coupling dysfunction and vestibular issues as causes of post-concussion headaches. The transferable move is to ask the practitioner to sort the thing the article is about into categories from their own casework, then structure the article around those categories. A taxonomy nobody else publishes is both an originality nugget and a large set of long-tail headings.

> "break down and categorize post-concussion headaches into six different types"

**Evidence:** The resulting article ranked #1 for its target keyword for several years and ranks for 8.4K keywords, ahead of pages from Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic that omitted the distinction.

**Pitfall:** If the categories are invented by the writer rather than drawn from the clinician's casework, they read as filler and can be factually wrong in a YMYL topic.

**Apply at Pabau:** In Pabau articles about a procedure or a practice problem, ask the interviewed clinician to sort cases into types from their own book and build the H2s from that list.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask your expert to sort the article's subject into types drawn from their own casework, then build the article's headings around that taxonomy. A classification nobody else publishes is the differentiator.

### 11. Put a TLDR callout above the fold, with the CTA directly beneath it  `56.6`
*core · best practices · source 56*

Cody puts a TLDR block above the fold on his articles, styled as a callout with a different background colour and some text styling so the eye is drawn straight to it, and places the CTA immediately below it. He reports two effects. For the human reader it answers the query instantly - 'okay, what I'm looking for is here' - and the natural next thing they see is the CTA. For AI search it produces lift, because it gives the model a clean written answer it can pull from without having to crawl the whole site and synthesise its own. Edward Sturm's addition is that giving the TLDR away costs you nothing: you still get the small percentage of readers who go deeper, and that self-selected group is the valuable one.

> "putting TLDRs above the fold"

**How to do it**

1. Answer the article's core query in two to four sentences and put that block above the fold, before any preamble.
2. Style it as a visually distinct callout - different background colour, some text styling - so it reads as the answer and not as an intro paragraph.
3. Put the primary CTA immediately below the callout, so the natural eye path is answer then action.
4. Keep the answer genuinely complete; a teaser that withholds the answer loses both the AI citation and the reader.
5. Let the rest of the article serve the minority who want depth, and don't shorten it to protect the TLDR.

**Pitfall:** Withholding the answer to force people to read on defeats both purposes at once: the reader bounces and the LLM has nothing clean to quote.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau article should open with a genuine answer block, not a scene-setting intro - it is the single cheapest change that serves both the reader who wants the answer and the AI engines deciding whether to cite the page.

**Apply anywhere:** Every article should open with a genuine answer block rather than a scene-setting intro - it is the cheapest single change that serves both the reader who wants the answer and the AI engines deciding whether to cite the page.

### 12. Put a one-line TL;DR summary above the fold on every article  `13.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 13*

To stop pogo-sticking, where visitors bounce back to Google because a page did not satisfy intent fast enough, Irwin recommends writing blog content the way top YouTube videos are structured: lead with the best, most valuable point and a summary immediately rather than building up to it. Concretely, after the title and an optional short intro, include a one-line or one-paragraph TL;DR that states the core takeaway, functioning like a mini table of contents summarizing all the subheadings to follow. This must appear above the fold or within the first few paragraphs, because failing to satisfy search intent above the fold sends the searcher straight back to Google, which both speakers treat as the worst possible signal a page can send.

> "If you don't satisfy search intent above the fold nowadays"

**How to do it**

1. Draft the article's core answer or takeaway in one sentence before writing the rest of the body; this becomes the TL;DR seed.
2. Place this one-line or one-short-paragraph TL;DR directly after the H1 and any brief intro, ensuring it renders above the fold on both desktop and mobile.
3. Write the TL;DR to summarize the article's subheadings or sections in miniature, so it reads like a condensed table of contents of the value to follow.
4. Avoid burying the direct answer under scene-setting, storytelling, or SEO preamble before the TL;DR appears.
5. Check the rendered page on a mobile viewport to confirm the TL;DR is visible without scrolling past the fold (inferred: use Chrome DevTools device toolbar to check the fold line at common mobile breakpoints).
6. After publishing, monitor Search Console and Analytics for that URL's pogo-stick behavior, such as very short time-on-page combined with return-to-SERP, as a signal the TL;DR is not satisfying intent fast enough (inferred).

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Making the reader scroll or read multiple paragraphs before reaching the actual answer sends the visitor straight back to Google, which both speakers frame as the single worst conversion and ranking signal.

### 13. Put a product walkthrough and competitive positioning in every commercial article  `181.8`
*core · best practices · source 181*

Grow and Convert require three components in every article on a buying-intent keyword: a product walkthrough, feature explanations, and competitive positioning that gives the reader a reason to choose this product. The reasoning is that these keywords are searched by informed buyers who are comparing options, so an article that explains the category without showing the product fails the intent it was written for. They source the walkthrough detail from the expert interviews, which is what makes it specific rather than a brochure paragraph. This is the operational form of a rule the base already carries, that selling in a bottom-funnel post is intent matching rather than salesiness. What is added here is the three-part checklist you can run against a draft.

> "Every article includes product walkthroughs, feature explanations, and competitive positioning"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report 5-7% conversion on bottom-funnel content built this way against 1-2% on typical top-of-funnel articles.

**How to do it**

1. Before publishing any buying-intent article, check it contains a walkthrough of how the task is actually done in the product, with screenshots.
2. Check it explains the specific features involved, at the level of detail an informed buyer would test in a demo.
3. Check it states competitive positioning: what this product does that the named alternatives do not.
4. Pull the walkthrough detail and the differentiators from the expert interview transcript rather than from marketing copy.
5. Name specific competitor limits (a plan cap, a missing integration) rather than making vague comparative claims.
6. Reject any draft on a commercial keyword that has all three parts missing, and send it back with the interview transcript.

**Pitfall:** Writing a bottom-funnel keyword in a top-funnel style is the common failure: the article explains the category, never shows the product, and converts at the informational rate.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau article on a buying-intent term should show the actual Pabau workflow with captioned screenshots, explain the features involved, and say what Pabau does that the named alternatives do not. Template pages are the obvious first place to apply the check.

**Apply anywhere:** Require three things in every buying-intent article: a screenshot walkthrough of the product doing the task, an explanation of the features involved, and explicit positioning against named alternatives.

### 14. Re-audit every content cluster's structure at least every six months  `25.7`
*core · best practices · source 25*

Koray argues the 'Helpful Content Update' is misnamed — it functioned as a 'functional content update' that erased sites/pages lacking any monetization, service, or product function, regardless of how genuinely helpful their text was. Google evaluates this partly through 'centerpiece annotation,' its patent/API-leak term for identifying the single most important element on a page; if that dominant element doesn't classify as commercial/functional in the right way, the page can be excluded from that query's index entirely. Because Google periodically changes its embedding classifications as part of core updates, his operating rule is to re-evaluate, at minimum every six months, whether each piece of content still deserves to be a standalone page, should be demoted to a heading/section within another page, or should shrink to just a sentence.

> "updating that every six months is always a necessity"

**How to do it**

1. Set a recurring six-month calendar reminder per content cluster or topical-map segment (not just per individual article) to run a structure review.
2. For each page in the cluster, identify its current 'centerpiece' element — the single most prominent content block, tool, or function on the page (inferred).
3. Check whether that centerpiece still matches a functional/commercial classification: does the page let the user do something (book a demo, compare pricing, use a tool) versus purely read text.
4. Check current Google SERPs and AI Overviews for the page's target query cluster to see whether competitors are now ranking dedicated pages, headings, or just sentences for that sub-topic.
5. If a page's sub-topic has been demoted industry-wide to heading-level treatment, merge/fold its content into a heading on a stronger parent page instead of keeping it standalone.
6. If a page's sub-topic has been promoted to deserving its own page (competitors now have dedicated pages for it), split it out into a new standalone page.
7. Confirm every page in the cluster demonstrates a clear function or monetization angle (link to a demo, tool, or product), not just informational text.
8. Log the review date and outcome per page so the next six-month cycle can measure what changed (inferred).

**Pitfall:** Assuming 'helpful' literally means well-written informational text is safe — Koray's examples (allergy sites blocked from monetizing by regulation, content-only affiliate blogs) show Google removed genuinely helpful text-only sites while functionally-monetized sites with the same content survived, so treating this as a writing-quality update rather than a business-model update is the core mistake.

### 15. Re-optimize instead of link-building when the post sits at position 6 or lower  `116.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 116*

Grow and Convert use a second, mutually exclusive rule for the re-optimization track. A post gets rewritten rather than linked when it ranks position 6 or lower for its target keyword, when the SERP has changed significantly since publication, and when its Clearscope score has dropped. The Clearscope drop is a proxy signal: the tool grades against currently ranking pages, so a score that falls without the page changing means competitors have moved. They are explicit that the score is not the deciding metric on its own, only that it helps. They also disclose Clearscope is a client, though they used it beforehand. Any content optimization tool that grades against the live SERP does the same job.

> "The post's Clearscope score has gone down"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use position 6 or lower, a significantly changed SERP and a fallen Clearscope score as the three conditions for choosing re-optimization over links.

**How to do it**

1. Filter your audit to posts ranking position 6 or lower for their target keyword.
2. Re-run each post through Clearscope, or an equivalent grader, without editing it.
3. Flag every post whose grade has fallen since publication, since that means the SERP moved, not the page.
4. Pull the current top 10 for the keyword and note the format, angle and audience they serve.
5. Compare against the SERP as it looked when you published to name exactly what changed.
6. Assign those posts to the rewrite track and take them off the link-building list.
7. Rewrite the angle first, then the on-page basics, then re-score in Clearscope.
8. Recheck position 8 weeks after republishing.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Treating the content grade as the target. Grow and Convert say the score is not the ultimate deciding metric; chasing the number produces keyword-stuffed copy that still misses the new intent.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau blog posts sitting outside the top five, David should re-score the page against the live SERP before editing. A grade that fell without the page changing means competitor coverage moved and the article needs a new angle, not more links.

**Apply anywhere:** For posts outside the top five, re-score the page against the live SERP before editing. A grade that fell without the page changing means the competition moved and the article needs a new angle.

### 16. Read page one for intent layers, then build one page covering all of them  `94.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 94*

Grow and Convert's method for writing a keyword-tailored page is to analyze what already ranks on page one and use it to identify the separate layers of intent behind the query, then write one article aimed at nothing except satisfying all of them. Cam Brown's worked example for their client TapClicks was 'paid search dashboard': the page-one results showed searchers wanted a definition, wanted templates, and wanted to see products that do the job. Three layers, one page. They note this is precisely why the TapClicks homepage could never rank for the term, since there is no realistic way to fold a definition, templates and a product comparison into a homepage. The blog post they wrote instead ranked number one for the term.

> "people want a definition, they want templates, and they want to see products"

**Evidence:** The TapClicks post built from the three intent layers of 'paid search dashboard' was ranking number one for the term at the time of writing.

**How to do it**

1. Search your target keyword and open the top ten results in tabs.
2. For each result, note in a sheet what job it does for the searcher: define, compare, template, tutorial, list vendors.
3. Group those jobs into distinct intent layers and count how many appear across the top ten.
4. Drop any layer that appears only once and is clearly an outlier.
5. Outline one page with a section per surviving layer, ordered by how many results serve that layer.
6. Include the actual asset where a layer demands one, for example a downloadable template, not a description of one.
7. Cut every section that does not serve one of the layers, however useful it seems.
8. Check the finished page against the layer list before publishing, and confirm the keyword sits in the title.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Teams read page one for headings to copy rather than for intent layers, and end up with a longer version of the same article. If your outline matches one competitor's structure, you have copied a page instead of reading the SERP.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO refresh work should start with this layer count. For a term like 'medical spa consent form', the layers are usually a definition, a downloadable form and a software recommendation, and all three belong on the one page.

**Apply anywhere:** Before writing, open the top ten results and record what job each does for the searcher. Group those jobs into intent layers and build a single page with a section for each, including the real asset when a layer calls for one.

### 17. Read page-one consensus as proof the SERP is too basic for buyers  `126.20`
*core · content insights · source 126*

Grow and Convert use SERP uniformity as a diagnostic, not just a complaint. They show three page-one results for enterprise reporting tool all opening with what is enterprise reporting and touching the same few themes, plus another page explaining that self-service reporting means users can create reports themselves. Their explanation for the pattern is that everyone is Googling around, reading and regurgitating each other's work, which is why so many first-page posts make identical points. Then they ask the question that turns it into an opportunity: does an enterprise customer looking for a reporting tool need to be told what enterprise reporting is, do they not already know it involves extracting, processing, organizing, analyzing, displaying and reporting data, and are they even searching the term to learn what it means, or do they just want a list of tools. Their verdict is that all of it is too basic for the customer.

> "They're too basic for the customer."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert document three page-one competitors for 'enterprise reporting tool' all opening with the same 'what is enterprise reporting' framing.

**How to do it**

1. Read the first 200 words of each page-one result and list the claims they share.
2. Mark every shared claim as commodity and forbid it from carrying your intro.
3. Ask whether the searcher, given their seniority, already knows each commodity claim.
4. If they do, treat the whole SERP as a gap and aim your piece at the next question down.
5. Check what the searcher actually wants, for example a list of tools rather than a definition, and lead with that.
6. Fill your differentiated space with interview-sourced detail none of the competitors could have.
7. Re-check after publishing whether you rank on the same generic intent or on the specific one you aimed at.

**Pitfall:** Copying the SERP consensus as a relevance play. You get a page that matches the topic and differentiates on nothing, and Grow and Convert argue that gives the reader no reason to feel you understand their problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau's SERP research shows every competitor opening with the same definition, David should treat that as the opening the Pabau article must not use. Lead with what the practice owner actually wants at that query, usually a comparison or a list, and back it with Pabau's internal detail.

**Apply anywhere:** When every page-one result makes the same points, read it as a signal the SERP is pitched below the buyer, not as a template. Ban the shared claims from your intro, lead with what the searcher actually wants, and fill the space with interview-sourced detail.

### 18. Read the piece by its headings only before submitting it  `124.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert got this from a client who asked that every piece be read by its headlines alone before review. The author had not heard of it and adopted it after trying it. It does two jobs. It tests scannability, since a reader decides from the headings whether to enter a section, so each heading has to describe what is actually discussed. And it tests narrative flow, because reading only the headings exposes gaps or disruptions in the argument. They demonstrate it on their own article, printing the full H1, H2 and H3 outline, then printing it again with one heading inserted, What is Content Writing, to show how obvious a misfit becomes at outline level.

> "we read it only by its headlines"

**Evidence:** The practice came from a Grow and Convert client's standing request, and the author reports seeing its value on first application to his own work.

**How to do it**

1. Extract the H1, H2 and H3 headings into a plain list before submitting the draft.
2. Read the list top to bottom with no body copy visible.
3. For each heading, ask whether it describes what the section actually says, and rewrite any label-style heading into a descriptive one.
4. Check the list reads as a sequence that delivers the promise made in the H1.
5. Mark any point where the argument jumps or a step is missing, and add or reorder headings there.
6. Delete any heading whose question the reader has already answered by clicking the title.
7. Confirm each remaining heading has a section that follows through.
8. Save the heading list with the draft so the reviewer can run the same check.

**Pitfall:** Reading the headings inside the document, where the surrounding copy fills the gaps for you. Extract them into a separate list or the test does not work.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles already carry a fixed block order, so the heading-only read should run right before the Pabau section and Conclusion are added. It is the fastest way to catch a definition H2 that does not belong on a bottom-funnel practice-software piece.

**Apply anywhere:** Extract the H1, H2 and H3 headings into a standalone list and read only that before submitting. It shows whether the headings are descriptive enough to scan and whether the argument has a gap.

### 19. Read the vendor's own help docs before writing a listicle entry  `92.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 92*

Grow and Convert found HubSpot's roundup had completely mis-characterized Loco Translate. The tool does not translate any public-facing content on a WordPress site, no homepage headline, no on-page copy, no blog posts. It only helps people edit translations of WordPress theme files, and its own help documentation says so plainly, including an explicit statement that it does not translate dynamic content. The writer had regurgitated the WordPress plugin listing instead. Grow and Convert's verdict is that the tool did not belong on that list at all, or if included, the entry had to state it is a narrow tool for editing theme translation files. The check costs a few minutes: open the product's documentation, not its marketing page, and confirm the product does the thing the article's keyword is about. This is the difference between an article a buyer trusts and one that discredits the whole list.

> "Loco Translate is not a tool for translating any of the public facing content"

**Evidence:** HubSpot's page-one article for website translation plugin described Loco Translate as a translation tool when its own help docs state it is primarily a file editor that does not translate dynamic content.

**How to do it**

1. For every product on the list, open its help docs or developer documentation, not its homepage.
2. Search the docs for the core verb of your keyword and read what the product says it does and does not do.
3. Note any explicit does-not statements in the docs; those are the ones marketing pages hide.
4. If the product cannot do the thing the keyword is about, cut it from the list rather than writing a vague entry.
5. If you keep a narrow tool, open its entry by stating the narrow scope in the first sentence.
6. Never write an entry from the app-store or plugin-directory listing alone, since those are vendor-written summaries.
7. Where possible install or open a trial and confirm the primary workflow yourself.
8. Record the doc URL you checked in the draft so the fact-checker can verify it.

**Pitfall:** An obscure product is the highest-risk entry, because nobody on the team knows it and the writer has the least to work from. That is exactly where the mischaracterization lands, and it is the one a specialist reader will spot.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau's software listicles, the writer must open each competitor's help center and confirm the feature claims before writing the entry, and record the doc URL in the draft. Aesthetic practice software has many narrow tools that look like full systems on their homepage.

**Apply anywhere:** For your software listicles, open each competitor's help center and confirm the feature claims before writing the entry, and record the doc URL in the draft. Many narrow tools look like full systems on their homepage.

### 20. Refuse the fully hands-off outsourcing arrangement  `101.10`
*core · best practices · source 101*

Grow and Convert's third misconception is that outsourcing means you can be absent from the process. Vendors exist who will work that way, and they name it as one of the biggest reasons companies end up dissatisfied with third-party content. Their argument is mechanical: without your input the writer cannot portray the nuances of your brand's perspective on each topic, or your product differentiators, positioning and value propositions. So the fix is to choose vendors whose process includes you by design rather than as an escalation path. Their own version is interviewing subject matter experts inside the client company for each new topic. Even a small amount of participation, they say, is what separates content a brand is proud of from content it quietly buries.

> "that necessitates participating in the content creation process"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert interview subject matter experts inside the client company for every new content topic.

**How to do it**

1. Decide up front how many hours a month your experts can give to content and say the number out loud.
2. Choose only vendors whose stated process has a scheduled input point from your side per article.
3. Book the expert interview or review slot before the article is commissioned, not after the draft arrives.
4. Give perspective on the angle at the outline stage, when changes are cheap.
5. Review drafts specifically for differentiators, positioning and value props, not for grammar.
6. Treat any piece delivered with zero input from you as a warning sign, not efficiency.

**Pitfall:** Hands-off arrangements feel efficient for two months, then produce a library of generic articles nobody internally wants to promote.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not accept ghostwritten articles on clinic workflows without a Pabau expert giving the angle first, because the product differentiators against competitors cannot be inferred from public sources.

**Apply anywhere:** Never buy content on a fully hands-off basis. Book your experts' input at the outline stage and choose vendors whose process includes you by design; otherwise nobody can express your positioning or differentiators.

### 21. Refuse to publish on any topic where you have nothing original to say  `100.7`
*core · best practices · source 100*

Grow and Convert state the rule as a corollary to their own strategy: if we have nothing original to say on a topic, we don't write about it. Their process is to pick topics they know customers are struggling with, then write an honest, original take on how they themselves deal with it. This is a hard constraint on the editorial calendar, not a preference. It rules out filling a keyword map for coverage's sake and rules out assigning a topic to a writer who has no exposure to it. The framework gives an escape valve rather than a dead end. If the topic matters commercially and nobody in-house has an original take, the answer is to interview someone who does, not to publish a me-too piece. The cost of ignoring the rule is content that ranks briefly and impresses nobody who lands on it.

> "If we have nothing original to say on a topic, we don't write about it"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this as symbolic of their entire content strategy, illustrated by their 'How to get a marketing job' post ranking #2.

**How to do it**

1. Start topic selection from problems you know customers struggle with, not from a keyword export.
2. For each candidate topic, write one sentence stating your original take before it enters the calendar.
3. If nobody in-house can write that sentence, move the topic to an interview queue instead of a writing queue.
4. Drop topics that survive neither test, however good the search volume looks.
5. Have the editor reject briefs whose angle line restates the SERP consensus.
6. Re-check the angle against the live top 10 at the point of assignment, since the SERP moves.
7. Keep a list of dropped topics so they can be revisited when a genuine take appears.

**Pitfall:** Coverage targets quietly override the rule and the calendar refills with me-too pieces. The signal is briefs whose angle field paraphrases the keyword.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content calendar should route any topic without an in-house original take to an interview with a clinic customer instead of to a writer. That applies particularly to clinical and regulatory topics where Pabau has no first-hand authority.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not publish on a topic where nobody on your team has an original take. Move it to an interview queue or drop it, and start topic selection from customer problems rather than a keyword export.

### 22. Resolve which persona a mixed-intent SERP is actually serving  `153.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 153*

Grow and Convert's second ranking factor is matching search intent, and they push past the usual version of the advice. Their example is 'data visualization tool', where the top result is a tools list, the second is definitions and examples, and the third is a product page. A mixed SERP does not tell you the format to write. They add a second layer: intent varies by the searcher's role. A technical data analyst wants job efficiency, a non-technical business professional wants to present to a corporate audience, and a marketer wants client reports. Choosing which persona to serve is the actual decision. The article is explicit that this requires a thorough SERP analysis rather than a glance at the top result.

> "the search intent can vary depending on the user's role and industry"

**Evidence:** The 'data visualization tool' SERP carries three different content types in the top three positions, and Grow and Convert list three distinct personas with different goals for the same query.

**How to do it**

1. Search the target keyword and classify each of the top 10 by format: list, definitional, product page, comparison, tool.
2. Write down the mix rather than copying whichever format sits at position one.
3. List every plausible persona for that query, including their technical level and their job.
4. For each persona, write the specific outcome they want from the page in one sentence.
5. Read the ranking pages and note which persona each was written for, using the examples and vocabulary as the tell.
6. Pick the persona that is both underserved on the SERP and closest to your buyer.
7. Choose the format that serves that persona, even if it is not the majority format in the top 10.
8. Write the opening so that persona recognizes themselves in the first two sentences.

**Pitfall:** Copying the format of the number one result on a mixed SERP. You end up as a worse version of an existing page, serving a persona who already has a better answer.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau keywords with mixed SERPs, David should decide explicitly between the practice owner, the practice manager and the individual practitioner before outlining. Record that choice in the brief so the writer picks examples and vocabulary for one reader.

**Apply anywhere:** On a mixed-format SERP, classify each top-10 result by format and by the persona it was written for, then pick the underserved persona closest to your buyer. Record that choice in the brief before anyone outlines the page.

### 23. Restructure existing posts to show how the product ties into the topic  `138.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 138*

The project started with two suggestions from Benji Hyam, not one. The first was to go after smaller-volume but more targeted topics. The second, which came first in the conversation, was to restructure some existing posts to more clearly show how their products tied into the topic. Nat Eliason ran both at once. This is worth separating from the keyword work because it is faster: the posts already rank, so a restructure converts traffic you are already paying to acquire. The retrofit target is the middle of the audit output, posts with real traffic and weak but non-zero conversion, where the topic is genuinely product-adjacent and only the structure is failing. Posts with zero product fit, like 'green tea shot', are not restructure candidates.

> "restructuring some of our posts to"

**Evidence:** Benji Hyam's advice to Nat Eliason paired restructuring existing posts with targeting smaller, more targeted topics; the combined result was over 300% more SEO sales in two months.

**How to do it**

1. From the revenue-sorted audit, take posts with meaningful traffic and low but non-zero attributed revenue; skip the ones with zero product fit.
2. For each, write the one-sentence connection between the article's topic and a specific product or collection.
3. Rewrite the introduction so that connection appears in the first few paragraphs rather than being implied.
4. Add the inline plus visual CTA to the matching collection straight after the intro.
5. Go section by section and add the matched product callout for each.
6. Do not change the title or the target keyword; the post already ranks, and the fix is structural.
7. Re-measure attributed revenue per post after four to six weeks and compare against the pre-restructure baseline.
8. Batch the restructures ahead of new commissions, since they convert traffic you already have.

**Pitfall:** Restructuring the wrong tier. Applying this to a post with no genuine product fit produces an article that reads as a sales pitch and still earns nothing, because the problem there is the keyword, not the structure.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should run this on existing aesthetics articles that already rank and mention Pabau only in passing. Adding the early feature-page CTA and section-matched callouts is cheaper than commissioning new pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Before commissioning new pages, restructure the posts that already rank and are product-adjacent: state the product connection in the intro, add an early CTA to the matching destination, and add section-matched callouts.
