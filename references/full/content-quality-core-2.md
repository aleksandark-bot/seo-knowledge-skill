### 1. Ban rhetorical-question openers because the answer is always yes  `127.3`
*core · best practices · source 127*

Grow and Convert push back directly on the standard advice to open a post with a question. Their objection is empirical: really good, in-depth pieces rarely start like this. What does start with questions is scammy, cliche late-night TV ads, and bad blog posts. The failure mode is that the question is almost always so basic the target reader would never actually ask it. Their B2C example is a post titled 'How to Lose Weight' that opens with 'Do you have trouble losing weight?' The answer is obviously yes, that is why the reader is reading the article. So the question is unnecessary filler, and worse, it is a signal of more cliches to come, which sets the reader's expectation for the whole piece. This is a named contradiction of common content-marketing advice, not a stylistic preference.

> "Scammy, cliche late night TV ads"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite a ranking 'How to Lose Weight' article opening with 'Do you have trouble losing weight?' and call it unnecessary filler.

**How to do it**

1. Search your draft for a question mark in the first three sentences.
2. Answer the question yourself as the target reader would; if the answer is an obvious yes, delete the question.
3. Keep a question only where a genuine practitioner would disagree about the answer.
4. Replace the deleted question with the most specific promise or claim available in the draft.
5. Scan the rest of the intro for the cliches that usually travel with the question opener, such as 'in today's world' and 'when it comes to'.
6. Add the rule to your editorial checklist so writers stop submitting question openers.
7. Spot-check published pages for the same pattern and rewrite the worst offenders first.

**Pitfall:** The question opener passes editing because it reads as engaging and audience-focused. The signal it actually sends is that the writer had nothing specific to say, and the reader reads it as a preview of the whole article.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog intros should carry no opening question. A page on reducing no-shows must not start with 'Are no-shows hurting your practice?' It should open on the specific finding or method the article contains.

**Apply anywhere:** Delete rhetorical questions from your opening lines. If the target reader's answer is an obvious yes, the question is filler and it signals that the rest of the piece will be generic too. Open with your most specific claim instead.

### 2. Ban writer self-research on medical topics outright  `117.17`
*core · best practices · source 117*

Grow and Convert single out the default content-marketing practice of writers self-researching topics from existing articles. They call it problematic in most industries and simply unacceptable for healthcare products and services that describe medical topics. The mechanism is concrete rather than moral: when Olivia would have self-researched post-concussion headaches from Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic and WebMD, she would never have encountered autonomic nervous system dysfunction, neurovascular coupling dysfunction or vestibular causes, because those top-ranking pages say nothing about them. Self-research therefore guarantees a page that is at best a recombination of the pages already ranking, and at worst inaccurate in a category where inaccuracy costs credibility, compliance standing and possibly legal exposure. The replacement is a mandatory expert interview per article.

> "it's common practice in content marketing for writers to self-research topics"

**Evidence:** Three mechanisms in the Cognitive FX article appeared in none of the top-ranking pages from Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic or WebMD, and came only from the clinician interview.

**How to do it**

1. Write into the content brief that no medical claim may originate from another blog or a competitor page.
2. Require every clinical article to cite an internal expert interview as its source of substance.
3. Allow secondary research only for structure and coverage checks, never for the mechanisms explained.
4. Have the interviewed expert verify terminology and context in the draft, not just the facts.
5. Reject drafts whose distinctive claims all appear in the current top ten results.
6. Track which subtopics came only from the interview; that list is the article's differentiator.

**Pitfall:** Self-researched medical content passes a superficial edit because it matches what everyone else says, then fails to rank and cannot be defended if a claim is challenged.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau's procedure and diagnostic-code content, forbid sourcing substance from competitor blogs. Require the clinical mechanism to come from a practitioner or an authoritative primary source, and log which one.

**Apply anywhere:** Forbid writers from sourcing medical substance out of other blogs. Every clinical claim comes from an expert interview or a primary source, with secondary research used only to check coverage.

### 3. Beat WebMD by covering what page one does not cover  `137.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 137*

Grow and Convert were nervous about competing with WebMD, Healthline, the CDC and the Mayo Clinic for a client with no authority. What worked was writing at a level page one does not reach. Their Cognitive FX articles cover in-depth physiology and cutting-edge research that the competing page-one articles often do not cover, because the client's founding doctors publish peer-reviewed papers on the treatment. They point out this is only possible with a real expert informing each piece; asking a writer to Google around returns the same cliché advice the ideal patient has heard a thousand times, which fails against an audience that has already read everything. The result was a number one ranking for 'what does a concussion headache feel like' ahead of the Mayo Clinic, with their own images and infographics in the featured snippet.

> "competing with us on Page 1 of Google's SERP often don't cover"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX ranked number one for 'what does a concussion headache feel like' ahead of the Mayo Clinic, with their images and infographics in the featured snippet, and grew from 1,700 indexed keywords in February 2019 to 48,500 in February 2023.

**How to do it**

1. Read all ten page-one results for the target query and list the sub-topics every one of them covers.
2. List the sub-topics none of them cover, especially mechanism, recent research and edge cases.
3. Take that gap list into an interview with your internal expert and ask them to answer each gap point.
4. Write the article so the uncovered material is the substance, not an appendix.
5. Build original images or infographics for the mechanism you explain, since these can win the featured snippet.
6. Cite the specific research, ideally papers your own experts published, rather than restating consensus advice.
7. Recheck the SERP six months later and repeat the gap exercise if a competitor has caught up.

**Pitfall:** Writers hand back a comprehensive-looking piece that simply merges what page one already says. Against an audience that has read every existing article, that reads as the same advice again and does not convert.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau this means each /blog/ article needs a section that no page-one competitor covers, sourced from a Pabau product specialist or a real clinic workflow, plus an original diagram of that mechanism rather than a restated best-practice list.

**Apply anywhere:** List what every page-one result covers, then list what none of them cover, and build your article on the second list using an internal expert and original diagrams.

### 4. Build a competitor gap 'resource brief' before rewriting a ranking page  `50.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 50*

For a page ranking around position 13-14, the system's workflow is to analyze the current top 10 ranking pages for that query and identify exactly what structural sections and topics are missing from your page relative to them. The transcript's own example flags a vitamins page as having "solid structural bones" and good product picks, but missing a buying-guide section, a "how to" section, and an FAQ section entirely. It then separately fetches additional "SEO intel" to find missing entities the top-ranking pages cover that yours doesn't. All of this gets compiled into a "resource brief," which, combined with a freshly scraped clean source, is used to generate an updated or entirely new article rather than a superficial edit.

> "no buying guide section, "how to" section is missing, no FAQ"

**How to do it**

1. Identify pages currently ranking outside the top 10 but not far off, roughly positions 11-20, as refresh candidates.
2. Pull the current top 10 ranking pages for that page's target query.
3. Compare your page's structure section-by-section against the top 10, checking for a buying guide, a "how to" section, an FAQ section, comparison tables, and similar components, and list every structural section your page is missing.
4. Separately extract the named entities, such as brands, ingredients, specifications, or standards, that appear across the top 10 pages but are absent from your page.
5. Compile the missing-sections list and missing-entities list into a single written brief for that page.
6. Scrape or gather fresh, accurate source information covering each missing section and entity before writing.
7. Rewrite or expand the page using the brief so it adds the missing sections and entities rather than only lightly editing existing text.
8. Re-check the page's ranking position after the update to confirm the gap-filling moved it up (inferred verification step).

### 5. Build a page that answers each researched objection directly  `161.13`
*core · concrete actions · source 161*

At ThinkApps, an app development company, the research produced a specific list of industry-wide fears: people had been burned by dev firms and had spent 60,000 to 100,000 dollars for an unfinished project; firms gave no visibility into the process and withheld source code; clients waited weeks for a reply; companies feared their IP would be stolen; timelines were over-promised and under-delivered against pre-planned launches. Khanal's response was not a blog post. They built a page on the site that speaks directly to those concerns, then carried the same answers through the business model, the content, the messaging, the ad copy, the landing pages and the sales approach. Within a year the business grew 4x in revenue.

> "Here's a page on the site that speaks directly to concerns"

**Evidence:** ThinkApps mapped researched pain points into its business model, content, messaging, ad copy, landing pages and sales approach, and grew 4x in revenue within a year.

**How to do it**

1. List every fear and objection the research surfaced, in the customers' own words, with the count of how often each appeared.
2. Rank them by frequency and by how much money the customer said was at stake.
3. Write one page, such as a How We Work page, with a section per top objection.
4. Answer each objection with a concrete mechanism, not a reassurance: what you do, when, and what the customer sees.
5. Add the proof for each mechanism, such as a screenshot, a contract clause or a named response-time commitment.
6. Copy the strongest objection answers into the sales deck and the first sales call script.
7. Reuse the same wording in ad copy so the promise the ad makes matches the page.
8. Link the page from pricing, the demo form and every comparison article, since objections peak at those points.

**Pitfall:** Answering objections with adjectives instead of mechanisms. 'We are transparent' answers nothing; 'you get source code access from day one and a weekly build link' answers it. If your objection page could be published by a competitor unchanged, it is not doing the job.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build one page answering the objections practices actually raise, such as data migration risk, patient record security, training time and contract lock-in, with a named mechanism under each. It should be linked from pricing, book-demo and every competitor comparison page.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn your researched objection list into a single page that answers each one with a concrete mechanism and proof, then reuse that wording in ads, sales calls and comparison pages.

### 6. Build an original dataset by auditing 40 sites feature by feature  `100.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 100*

For a heavy-originality piece, Growth Rock spent months building data nobody else had. They wanted something valuable to ecommerce executives shopping for a CRO agency, their target customer. Rather than a top-10 tips list, they found 40 ecommerce sites with the most traffic according to Alexa, analyzed page by page exactly what features each had in mobile checkout, and assembled it all in a large spreadsheet. From that they produced graphs of what percentage of the top 40 used each feature, and cited their own AB test results where they applied to a feature under discussion. The author notes the writing was the last step, like a scientist writing a paper after two years of lab work. It ranks #1 for 'mobile checkout best practices' and #1 for 'mobile checkout trends'.

> "we found 40 ecommerce sites that had the most traffic"

**Evidence:** The mobile checkout study ranks #1 for 'mobile checkout best practices' and #1 for 'mobile checkout trends'; the team spent months on the data collection alone.

**How to do it**

1. Pick a topic that is a known pain point of your ideal client, not just a high-volume term.
2. Define the population, for example the 40 highest-traffic sites in your sector, and name the ranking source.
3. Write a fixed checklist of features to record so every site is scored identically.
4. Work through each site page by page and log every feature into one spreadsheet.
5. Convert the spreadsheet into percentage-of-sample charts, one per feature.
6. Attach your own test results to the features where you have them, so the piece carries data nobody can copy.
7. Say in the introduction that this is a study, not a post.
8. Target both the best-practices and the trends variants of the keyword, since the same dataset serves both.

**Pitfall:** Months of data collection on a topic your buyers do not care about produces a study that ranks and converts nobody. The signal is that you cannot name the client role that reads it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau could audit a defined sample of aesthetic clinic websites for booking-flow features, publish the percentages, and own the resulting terms. Pick a feature set that maps to what Pabau's online booking actually does so the study supports the product.

**Apply anywhere:** Build a study by scoring a defined sample of sites against a fixed feature checklist, then publish percentage charts. Choose a topic that is a known pain point of your ideal client and say in the intro that it is a study.

### 7. Build one dedicated page per keyword instead of bundling several  `102.9`
*core · best practices · source 102*

Grow and Convert say the best way to rank is to create a dedicated landing page or blog post for each individual keyword, and they call out targeting several keywords with a single piece as a common but less effective strategy. The mechanism they give is intent matching: search engines rank content by how well it satisfies the intent behind that specific query, so a page built for exactly one query beats a broader page that covers the topic among several others. They also allow the same move on existing content, strategically updating a page to serve one keyword rather than spreading it. The practical constraint is that this only works if the keywords are genuinely distinct intents; near-identical queries put on separate pages produce cannibalization instead.

> "create a dedicated landing page or blog post to go after each"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state this as their standing production rule and attribute it to intent-satisfaction ranking.

**How to do it**

1. For each target keyword, check whether the ranking pages are dedicated to that query or cover it as a section.
2. If they are dedicated, plan a dedicated page of your own rather than adding a section to an existing article.
3. Before splitting, compare the SERPs of the two keywords; if the results overlap heavily, keep them on one page instead.
4. Give each dedicated page its own title, URL, H1 and opening sentence built around its single query.
5. For existing pages that straddle several keywords, decide which one keyword the page will serve and rewrite it to that intent.
6. Spin the abandoned keywords out into their own pages, and internally link the set together.

**Pitfall:** Splitting keywords that share a SERP, which creates cannibalization and leaves two half-strength pages competing. Check SERP overlap before you split, not after.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should stop stacking related queries into single long guides and instead build one page per distinct intent, checking SERP overlap first so template pages and blog posts do not compete for the same query.

**Apply anywhere:** Build one page per distinct keyword intent rather than bundling several into a long guide, but compare SERPs first: if two queries return the same results, they belong on one page.

### 8. Build one dedicated page per keyword, and name the exception rule  `179.8`
*core · best practices · source 179*

Grow and Convert call the alternative the Sprinkle Method: hand writers a keyword list and ask them to sprinkle the terms through general articles. They say Google differentiates between content that loosely relates to a topic and content that specifically addresses the query, so a sprinkled page has a slim shot against competitors with dedicated pages. Their worked example: to rank for "project management software for construction companies", the sprinkle version writes a general project management article mentioning construction a few times, while the dedicated version is a page titled for construction that speaks to construction owners, addresses their workflow problems, and explains which features solve them. They add the exception their own agency uses. Most target pages are new articles, but when an existing page such as the homepage or a landing page is genuinely the best fit for a keyword, they point that page at it instead of building another.

> "if we feel like an existing page on the site"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert credit one-page-per-keyword with achieving positions 1-3 for clients, which is where they say the majority of traffic sits.

**How to do it**

1. List every target keyword and assign exactly one URL to each before any writing starts.
2. For each keyword, check whether an existing page such as the homepage, a product page or a landing page already matches that intent better than a new article would.
3. If an existing page matches, optimize that page for the keyword rather than creating a competing one.
4. If no page matches, brief a new page whose title contains the keyword and whose sections address that searcher's specific situation.
5. Write the page for the narrower audience named in the keyword, covering their workflow problems and the features that solve them.
6. Audit existing articles for sprinkled keywords: if a page mentions a term a few times without addressing it, remove the mention and give the term its own page.
7. Keep a keyword-to-URL map so a second page never gets briefed against a keyword already assigned.

**Pitfall:** Creating a dedicated page for a keyword an existing strong page already serves. That splits intent and internal links across two URLs, and the newer, weaker page usually loses.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's keyword-to-URL map should decide up front whether a term belongs on a new blog article, an existing template page, or the relevant product page. Feature and category terms often belong on product pages already live, not on new posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Assign exactly one URL to every target keyword before writing. First check whether a live page, including the homepage or a landing page, already matches that intent better than a new article would, and optimize it instead. Only brief a new page when nothing fits. Then audit old articles for sprinkled keywords and give each of those terms its own page.

### 9. Build the exhaustive public knowledge base LLMs can learn from  `60.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 60*

Joe's unprompted addition, and one of the most actionable ideas in the episode: for ChatGPT it helps to have a really extensive knowledge base on your website - help docs about your product, every single question asked, every single feature explained, every single integration. His reasoning is explicitly not about visitors: 'even if it's not for visitors, the more the LLMs learn about your company and brand, the more confident they'll be in recommending you.' He pairs it with the inverse judgement, which is worth noting because it cuts against a lot of content strategy: informational blog posts on your own website are probably not much of a play anymore, the same as the affiliate and niche-site model. A client had asked him whether to set up a separate blog linked only from the footer, heavily structured with schema, because the AI chat surfaces seem to like indexing that type of content.

> "extensive knowledge base on your website"

**How to do it**

1. Inventory every feature, integration and setting your product has.
2. Write a public help document for each one - not gated, not behind a login.
3. Add a page for every question your support team actually receives, in the customer's words.
4. Document every integration by name, since those are the terms comparison prompts use.
5. Structure it with schema and keep it crawlable and internally linked.
6. Don't judge these pages on traffic - their job is to make the model confident about what you do.
7. Shift effort away from general informational blog posts toward this documentation surface.

**Pitfall:** Putting the knowledge base behind a login or a support portal that blocks crawlers defeats the entire purpose - the value is in the model being able to read it.

**Apply at Pabau:** This is probably Pabau's highest-leverage AI visibility work: exhaustive public documentation of every feature, integration and setting, written in the language clinics use, so the engines can answer 'does Pabau do X' confidently instead of guessing.

**Apply anywhere:** This is probably the highest-leverage AI visibility work available: exhaustive public documentation of every feature, integration and setting, written in your customers' language, so the engines can answer 'does it do X' confidently instead of guessing.

### 10. Check intro and body for symmetry and fix whichever side is weaker  `124.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert see first drafts fail in two mirror-image ways. Type A has a strong introduction and a weak body: the intro names the key features a digital marketing dashboard needs, then the body steps back to explain why digital marketing matters, drops irrelevant stats about how important companies think it is, and never expands the value props the intro promised. Type B, which they say happens more often, has a fluffy pointless intro over a genuinely useful body about importing and segmenting data into reporting dashboards. The prescription differs by type. For A, take the good information in the intro and expand it in the body with more detail. For B, take the valuable information in the body and reverse-engineer the intro from it.

> "looking for symmetry between the intro and the body"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's two named draft patterns from their own client work, with the digital marketing dashboards example for both directions.

**How to do it**

1. List every claim or value prop the introduction promises, one per line.
2. List what the body sections actually deliver, one per line.
3. Compare the two lists and classify the draft as intro-heavy or body-heavy.
4. If intro-heavy, assign each unfulfilled intro promise to a body section and expand it with detail from the interview.
5. Delete body passages that back up to explain why the category matters, plus any stat about how important the category is.
6. If body-heavy, pull the three most valuable specifics out of the body and write the intro from them.
7. Cut every intro sentence that would still be true for a competitor's article.
8. Recheck the two lists match before submitting.

**Pitfall:** Fixing the weak side by adding words rather than moving information. An expanded fluffy intro is still fluff, and the mismatch between promise and delivery stays.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run an intro-promise versus body-delivery list on every Pabau refresh. Many older Pabau articles are body-heavy, where the practical workflow detail sits in section three and the intro explains why record keeping matters.

**Apply anywhere:** List what the intro promises and what the body delivers, then fix whichever side is weaker. Expand unfulfilled intro promises into the body, or reverse-engineer the intro from the body's best specifics.

### 11. Check you actually have something to add before commissioning content  `126.12`
*core · best practices · source 126*

Grow and Convert make a blunt precondition argument. Because the topics that convert are the ones closest to your product and aimed at your customer's specific pain points, people at your company must have deep expertise on those topics and must hold answers to the customer questions behind them. If they do not, then nobody at the company has anything important to say about their customer's specific product-related pain points, and one of two things is true: they should not be in this business, or they are in a commodity business and should expect their content to be as undifferentiated as their product. Their line is that you have no business complaining about content quality if you do not have anything to add to the conversation. This makes the internal expertise audit a step before, not after, keyword research.

> "You have no business complaining about content quality"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert argue a company with no internal answers is either in the wrong business or in a commodity business and should expect undifferentiated content.

**How to do it**

1. List your five highest-intent product-adjacent topics.
2. For each, name the internal person who could answer a customer's hardest question on it without preparation.
3. If no name exists for a topic, do not commission the article yet.
4. Fill the gap by having someone shadow sales or support calls on that topic first.
5. If several topics have no owner, treat that as a positioning problem and fix the product story before the content plan.
6. Re-run the audit each quarter as staff and product change.

**Pitfall:** Commissioning a full calendar with no internal expert behind half the topics. The pieces will read as commodity content and no amount of editing rescues them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau expands into a new content cluster, David should name the internal person who owns that cluster's expertise. If no one at Pabau can answer the hardest customer question on a topic, that cluster is not ready to publish.

**Apply anywhere:** Before you commission a topic, name the person inside your company who could answer a customer's hardest question about it cold. If nobody can, the article is not ready to be written and no editor will save it.

### 12. Choose one of four uniqueness sources before writing a piece  `153.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 153*

Grow and Convert break uniqueness into four concrete options rather than leaving it as an instruction to be original. Share original data that others have not covered. Offer a different perspective, approaching the topic from a new angle while still fulfilling search intent. Interview subject-matter experts to add depth and authority. Or discuss your own product or service, which they tie specifically to bottom-of-funnel keywords where you show how the product addresses a named pain point. They pair uniqueness with quality, which they define as information that matches the needs of the search term, and for bottom-of-funnel keywords, content that converts as well as ranks by meeting the audience at their knowledge level.

> "Uniqueness can manifest in several ways"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's four-part uniqueness framework, used across 80+ clients since 2017, with product-discussion reserved for bottom-of-funnel keywords.

**How to do it**

1. Before outlining, pick exactly one of the four sources: original data, new angle, expert interview, or product experience.
2. For original data, identify a number only you hold, such as internal usage figures or a customer survey you can run this month.
3. For a new angle, write the contrarian or reframed thesis in one sentence and check no top-10 page argues it.
4. For an expert interview, book one 30-minute call and pull three claims from it that appear nowhere in the SERP.
5. For product experience, tie the piece to a bottom-of-funnel keyword and name the specific pain point the product resolves.
6. Check that the chosen source still fulfills the search intent you identified, rather than replacing it.
7. For bottom-of-funnel pieces, set the reader's knowledge level explicitly and position the product as the solution to their stated problem.
8. Record the chosen source in the brief so the editor can check the finished draft actually delivers it.

**Pitfall:** Writing 'make it unique' in the brief with no source attached. The writer defaults to rearranging the top 10 and the piece is a Google research paper again.

**Apply at Pabau:** Each Pabau brief should name its uniqueness source in one line. Practice-operations articles can usually use product experience or a practitioner interview; industry topics should carry original Pabau data where possible.

**Apply anywhere:** Pick one uniqueness source per piece — original data, a new angle, an expert interview, or your own product experience — and write it into the brief. Reserve product-led pieces for bottom-of-funnel keywords, and check the choice still serves the search intent.

### 13. Choose which pain points to emphasize instead of listing all of them  `159.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 159*

Grow and Convert argue the harder pain point problem is not getting the pain point wrong, it is having too many. Almost every product solves a lot of pain points and you cannot emphasize them all. They point at QuickBooks' homepage, which leads with three pain points, and note the authors are QuickBooks customers whose own three pain points are taxes, understanding profit and loss per client, and planning future growth. None of those are the three QuickBooks chose. The forcing function is scarcity of space: you have one homepage and one hero unit above the fold, so you must pick. For their own agency they list content quality, writing, hiring writers, promotion and conversion as client pain points, then choose one overarching theme instead: content marketing has too many moving pieces and making them work together is hard.

> "you only have one "hero unit" above the fold"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's agency homepage reduces a five-item pain point list to one line: 'We'll run your entire content marketing operation.'

**How to do it**

1. List every pain point your product genuinely solves, without ranking them.
2. Pull the actual language customers used for each one from interviews, support tickets and survey replies.
3. Look for the overarching theme that sits behind several of the individual pain points rather than picking a favorite.
4. Check that you can serve that theme better than competitors can, and that they cannot easily claim it.
5. Put that single theme in the hero unit and nothing else.
6. Move the remaining pain points to sub-pages, feature pages and blog posts where there is room.
7. Re-run the exercise after each round of customer interviews, since the emphasis can shift as the buyer base changes.

**Pitfall:** Listing five value props in the hero because each one tested well individually. The result is a hero that argues nothing, and no customer can repeat back what you do.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's homepage and main product pages should lead with one overarching theme rather than a feature list, and push scheduling, charting, payments and marketing pain points to the sub-pages and blog where each has room to be argued.

**Apply anywhere:** Reduce your hero to one overarching pain point theme that sits behind the individual ones, and push the rest to sub-pages and blog posts.

### 14. Content rule: as short as possible, as long as necessary  `15.6`
*core · best practices · source 15*

James's standard for 'good quality content' is to identify every specific question a searcher actually has (his example: a 'plumber in Manchester' searcher wants to know cost, how fast they can come out, and what services are offered) and answer each directly and concisely, without making the reader hunt for it. He explicitly rejects both extremes: the old-school habit of stretching articles to 5,000-7,000 words and burying the actual answer many paragraphs deep ('playing hide-and-seek with your content'), and the opposite failure of publishing thousands of pages at under 50 words with no real substance. His stated rule of thumb: content 'needs to be as short as possible, but as long as necessary,' with all fluff removed but every real sub-question still covered - he notes he's seen pages rank successfully on just two or three sentences when that fully satisfies the exact intent of the page's H1.

> "as short as possible, but as long as necessary"

**How to do it**

1. Before writing a page, list every specific question the target searcher likely has for that exact topic/location/service.
2. Write a direct, concise answer to each identified question positioned where it's asked, rather than requiring the reader to read through preceding paragraphs first.
3. Cut any paragraph, anecdote, or sentence that doesn't directly serve answering one of those identified questions.
4. Match length to necessity per page rather than to a fixed word-count target - a page can be two or three sentences if that fully satisfies the H1's exact intent.
5. Avoid the old-school pattern of stretching content to 5,000-7,000 words and burying the direct answer many paragraphs deep purely to hit a word count.
6. Avoid the opposite extreme too - don't publish thin content at scale (thousands of pages under roughly 50 words with no real substance).
7. Audit existing long-form pages by checking whether the core answer to the primary question appears early, and move it up if a reader has to scroll deep to find it. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Padding articles to 5,000-7,000 words and burying the actual answer deep in the piece serves neither the reader nor rankings - pages have ranked successfully with just two or three sentences when that fully satisfies the H1's exact intent, so length should be driven by how many real sub-questions need answering, not a word-count target.

### 15. Convert FAQ answers into full solution-path pages  `29.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 29*

Hank's highest-leverage content change is replacing simple FAQ answers with full solution-path pages. His method: pull the 'People Also Ask' question clusters for a topic (via a tool like AnswerThePublic or by expanding PAA boxes manually) to identify the real pain point behind a group of related questions, rather than treating each question as an isolated line item. Instead of writing a one- or two-sentence FAQ answer, build a page that walks through the entire workflow needed to actually solve that pain point, step by step. He calls this the biggest content shift he's seen: FAQs answer the literal question and stop, while solution pages solve the underlying task the user came to accomplish, which is what drives real engagement and conversion.

> "instead of creating simple FAQs, create solution paths"

**How to do it**

1. Pull the 'People Also Ask' question set for your target topic using a tool like AnswerThePublic, or by manually expanding PAA boxes in Google's results.
2. Group the individual questions into clusters representing one underlying pain point rather than treating each question as its own FAQ line.
3. For each cluster, identify the actual workflow or task the user is trying to complete, not just the literal question text.
4. Replace the planned short FAQ answer with a full page or section that walks through the entire solution step by step.
5. Structure the page with concrete steps, examples, or screenshots that demonstrate the solution rather than a one- or two-sentence answer.
6. Give the solution page its own specific URL/section rather than folding it into a generic omnibus FAQ page, so it can be measured independently.
7. Compare engagement and conversion on the converted solution page against the old FAQ-style version to confirm the change is working. (inferred)

**Tools:** AnswerThePublic, Google People Also Ask

**Pitfall:** A simple FAQ answers only the literal question and stops, which fails users who actually need to be walked through solving the underlying problem - the page can 'technically' answer the query and still produce weak engagement.

### 16. Convert a single-product post into a comparison list when the SERP turns listicle  `119.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 119*

Grow and Convert describe a SaaS client post targeting an industry keyword around estimating software. It had climbed to page one, plateaued at positions nine and ten, then started to dip, giving them both a stall and a slip. Their SERP analysis showed page one had changed since the post was written: Google was now mostly ranking list posts comparing software options. Their post had no list and compared nothing, it only talked about the client's product. That had matched intent originally, when a single product landing page was part of the mix, but the SERP moved. They reworked the piece to include a list of competing software so readers could compare, kept the client's solution inside it, and changed the title to signal the wider coverage.

> "Google was now primarily ranking list posts comparing different software options"

**Evidence:** After the rework the post recovered and climbed to position #1, and since reaching the top has never dipped past position #3, sitting at #1 most of the time.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm the page shows both symptoms: a plateau on page one and then a downward drift.
2. Search the target keyword and classify each of the top ten results by format.
3. If comparison lists dominate and your page is a single-product piece, treat it as a format mismatch.
4. Pull the products that appear most often across those competing lists and shortlist them.
5. Add a comparison section reviewing those options honestly, with your own product placed inside it.
6. Keep the product's own pitch, but frame it as one option a reader is choosing between.
7. Rewrite the title so it promises coverage of the popular solutions, not just your product.
8. Track the keyword weekly afterward and stop updating once the position holds.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Adding a token competitor list while the article still reads as a product page. Readers and Google both see it, and the format mismatch is unchanged.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's pages that pitch only Pabau for a category keyword are exposed to exactly this shift. David should re-check the SERP for those keywords and convert to honest comparison guides that include competitors, with Pabau reviewed among them.

**Apply anywhere:** When comparison lists take over a SERP your product page used to rank in, rebuild the page as a comparison guide that includes your own option, and retitle it to match.

### 17. Convert every vague comparative into a number during the interview  `134.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 134*

Grow and Convert list the words experts use that mean nothing without a reference point: faster, slower, more often, easier to use, more effective, more efficient. They say those words also read as empty business jargon if you carry them into the writing, so you get the reference point on the call. They print the exchange verbatim. The expert says the process helps teams release new software updates really fast. The interviewer asks how fast is fast, once every two weeks. The expert says no, like three times a day. The interviewer then asks what's typical before they use you, and the expert says most are releasing a couple times a month. That is two numbers, the after and the before, from two questions. The second question is the one writers forget, and without it the first number has no baseline to beat.

> "How fast is fast? Once every two weeks?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's printed exchange moved 'really fast' to three releases a day against a baseline of a couple a month.

**How to do it**

1. Keep the list of trigger words visible during the call: faster, slower, more often, easier, more effective, more efficient.
2. When one appears, stop and ask for the number, offering a deliberately wrong guess to force a correction.
3. Follow immediately with 'and what's typical before they use you?' to get the baseline.
4. Ask which customer or segment those numbers come from, so the figure is attributable.
5. Write both figures into the notes as a before-and-after pair, never the after alone.
6. Cut any comparative from the draft that has no number attached to it.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
How fast is fast? Once every two weeks?
```

**Pitfall:** Recording the improved figure without the baseline. Three deploys a day means nothing to a reader who does not know the norm is a couple a month, and the claim reads as vendor noise.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles that say scheduling is faster or admin is easier should carry the before and after, sourced from a real practice. David should require both numbers before a comparative adjective survives the sentence gate.

**Apply anywhere:** Whenever an expert says faster, easier or more efficient, ask for the number and then ask for the pre-product baseline. Publish the pair or cut the adjective.

### 19. Cut any claim that is true of every product in the category  `92.2`
*core · best practices · source 92*

Grow and Convert's working definition of fluff is precise and usable: a word is not fluff by itself, the context makes it fluff. Their test is whether the claim distinguishes the product from the others on the list. In a roundup of website translation plugins, WPBeginner described TranslatePress as a full-fledged plugin that translates every aspect of your website. Every plugin on that list translates websites, so the claim says nothing. Their analogy is listing tennis racquets and writing that one of them can help you hit tennis balls. Another example from the same post described Polylang as good for a multilingual or bilingual site, which is redundant twice over, since bilingual is a subset of multilingual and every translation plugin does both. A third post called a product cloud-based, when almost all software is. The rescue is not deletion but defence: full-fledged becomes real copy the moment the sentence says what other plugins are limited to and what this one adds.

> "words by themselves aren't fluff, it's the context that makes them fluff"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert dissect three fluff examples from the pages ranking one and three for website translation plugin, contrasting them with their own client article ranking two.

**How to do it**

1. List every product on the page and the claims made about each one.
2. Cross out any claim that could be moved to another product on the list without becoming false.
3. For each crossed-out claim, either delete it or add the comparison that makes it specific: while most tools only do X, this one also does Y and Z.
4. Apply the earns-its-place test word by word to the first sentence of each product description, where fluff concentrates most.
5. Ban category-baseline adjectives outright in the style guide: full-fledged, robust, comprehensive, cloud-based, powerful, seamless.
6. Check redundant pairs where one term already contains the other, such as multilingual or bilingual.
7. Have a second reader who knows the category read the draft, since baseline claims read as normal to a non-expert and as amateurish to a buyer.

**Pitfall:** Fluff is not incorrect, which is why it passes fact-checking. It is true that a translation plugin makes a site multilingual. Savvy buyers read it as amateurish while your reviewers see nothing wrong.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's competitor listicles should never describe a practice management system as cloud-based, all-in-one, or comprehensive without saying what the alternative lacks. Those words are category baseline in aesthetics software and mark the article as written by someone who has not used the tools.

**Apply anywhere:** Never describe a product with an adjective that is baseline for its category unless the sentence also says what the alternatives lack. Those words mark the article as written by someone who has not used the tools.

### 20. Cut any sentence stating what your reader already knows  `97.2`
*core · content insights · source 97*

The article contrasts two results for the query 'how to get more SEO traffic'. The weak one opens with lines like 'Getting traffic is one of the most important things for any web master' and offers headings such as 'Optimize Your Website for the Search Engines'. The strong one, from Brian Dean's Backlinko, never says 'search engines have always been a major way to get traffic for free' because the author knows the reader already knows that. Grow and Convert's point is that stating the obvious is not neutral filler. It actively tells an informed reader you are not the expert, and they bounce and go find better advice. The reaction they describe is blunt: 'No shit, thanks for the advice'. So the edit rule is to delete any sentence whose content the target reader could have written themselves.

> "you likely already know that"

**Evidence:** Side-by-side comparison of a mirage SEO-services post and Brian Dean's Backlinko post for 'how to get more SEO traffic'.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the exact job title and experience level of the reader you want as a customer, not a generic persona.
2. Read each draft as that person and strike every sentence they would already agree with before reading.
3. Delete category-definition intros entirely and start the post at the first non-obvious claim.
4. Replace any advice item that is a restatement of the goal ('get more links') with the specific method for achieving it.
5. Check the H2s in isolation: if a heading reads as a goal rather than a method, rewrite it as a method.
6. Ask a practitioner in that role to read the draft and mark anything they knew already, then cut what they marked.

**Pitfall:** Writers keep obvious openers as a warm-up or for beginner readers. If your buyer is experienced, those lines cost you the sale in the first screen even when the rest of the post is good.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles should not open by explaining what practice management software is to an audience of clinic owners already shopping for it. David should cut those intros and open on the specific decision the reader came to make.

**Apply anywhere:** Delete any sentence your target reader already agrees with, especially category-definition intros, and open the post at the first non-obvious claim.

### 21. Cut opening lines that state the obvious to a searcher  `124.14`
*core · best practices · source 124*

Grow and Convert diagnose the most common intro failure as a writer trying too hard to relate to the audience and stating what the reader already knows. Their example intro for employee time tracking software opens on how every employee loves taking time off and how busy a business owner is. Their point is that this piece is for people googling employee time tracking software, so they already know they need to track time, recognize it as a problem, and are actively shopping for software. The opener adds nothing. They also show the editor-mode critique in action: do your job and run your business are the same thing, and keep your company staffed is vague and possibly wrong. The instruction that replaces the obvious opener is to start from the specific things the reader is trying to solve.

> "come across as a writer trying too hard to relate"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's annotated employee time tracking software intro, where they show three separate faults in a five-line paragraph.

**How to do it**

1. Write out what the searcher already knows by virtue of typing your target keyword.
2. Delete every intro sentence that restates any of it.
3. Check the remaining sentences for pairs that say the same thing twice, such as do your job and run your business.
4. Delete or replace any vague claim you cannot support, since a vague claim is often also wrong.
5. Open instead on the specific problems the reader is trying to solve, taken from the client interview.
6. Make each of those problems concrete enough that a competitor could not have written the same line.
7. Reread as an editor focused on the audience's interests, not on your own understanding of the topic.
8. Repeat the pass on the first paragraph after each major heading.

**Pitfall:** Confusing empathy with relevance. Sentences about how busy and frustrated the reader is feel audience-focused but tell a shopper nothing they did not know before searching.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau intros should not open on how busy a practice is or how admin takes up the day. David should replace those lines with the specific problem behind the keyword, sourced from the interview, and check for restated pairs.

**Apply anywhere:** Write down what the searcher already knows from typing the keyword, then delete every intro line that restates it. Open on the specific problems they are trying to solve, and check for two phrases that say the same thing.

### 22. Cut sentences that restate a point you already made  `72.12`
*core · best practices · source 72*

Khanal's second editing test, applied right after the delete-and-reread test, is to ask whether a sentence is genuinely new or just says what you already said. He is blunt that most of the time repetition slows the writing down, and allows the exception where you deliberately want emphasis. He applies it to himself, noting that in cutting a line he is doing exactly what he accuses AI writing of doing. He also names repetition as an AI tell earlier in the same session, pointing to a Reddit post that asked the same question three ways as evidence it was machine-written. The same failure appears in human drafts: he says he sees writers on his own team submit copy with the same tendency to overwrite.

> "is this just repeating something I already said"

**Evidence:** Khanal used repeated phrasing as the tell that identified a top-ranking Reddit post as AI-written, then caught the same failure in his own draft minutes later.

**How to do it**

1. After the delete test, ask of each remaining sentence whether it introduces a new idea or restates one.
2. Compare it directly against the sentence or two above it, not against the article as a whole.
3. Keep a restatement only where you consciously want emphasis on that specific point.
4. Cut trailing clauses that add a consequence you already implied, such as naming the common mistake after listing the categories.
5. Check that consecutive sentences are making different arguments, not the same argument in two registers.
6. Apply the same check to AI drafts and human drafts, since both overwrite in the same way.

**Pitfall:** Assuming repetition is an AI-only problem. Khanal says human writers on his team submit the same overwritten copy, so an all-human draft still needs the pass.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a repetition check to the Pabau editorial pass that compares each sentence to the two before it. This catches both AI padding and the overwriting habit in human drafts.

**Apply anywhere:** Check every sentence against the two before it and cut anything that restates rather than advances the point, in AI drafts and human drafts alike.

### 23. Cut the not-X-but-Y construction as a giveaway AI sentence  `71.6`
*core · best practices · source 71*

Devesh initially praised a sentence in the generated intro, then reread it and cut it. His reason: it had the feeling of 'it's not X, it's Y', which he calls a very AI thing. The specific line said the free tools give you a template, not a brief. He objected on two grounds. The construction itself now reads as machine-written because models overuse it. And the substance was vague, since 'it's a template' does not tell the reader what is missing. He replaced it with a direct, bolder claim that the briefs are not very good, followed by the concrete list of what they do and do not contain. His note to himself was that this is where you have to be human.

> "feelings of it's not X it's Y"

**Evidence:** Devesh cut 'The problem is this is a template, not a brief' from his own generated intro and replaced it with 'The problem is that these briefs aren't very good. They tell you very surface level things like word count and keywords to include.'

**How to do it**

1. Search every AI draft for the not-X-but-Y pattern, including its variants 'this isn't a Y, it's a Z' and 'not just X, but Y'.
2. For each hit, ask what concrete claim the sentence is actually making.
3. Replace it with a direct assertion, for example 'these briefs are not very good' instead of 'this is a template, not a brief'.
4. Follow the direct assertion immediately with the specifics that prove it, such as what the briefs do contain and what they leave out.
5. Do the same pass on your own writing, since the construction is a vagueness tell, not only an AI tell.
6. Re-read the edited passage aloud to confirm it now states something a reader could disagree with.

**Pitfall:** The construction reads well on first pass, which is why Devesh praised the sentence before cutting it. The tell is that you cannot say what the sentence claims without rereading it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add not-X-but-Y to Pabau's AI-tells checklist in the editorial pass, alongside the existing tells, and require the replacement to name the specific shortcoming rather than restate the contrast.

**Apply anywhere:** Add the not-X-but-Y construction to your AI-tells checklist. Replace each one with a direct claim plus the specifics that prove it, and run the same pass over human drafts.
