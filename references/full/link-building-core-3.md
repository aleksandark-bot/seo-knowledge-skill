### 1. Run citation outreach to the sites already cited for your key prompts  `84.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 84*

Grow & Convert sell this as a service and describe the mechanic plainly: they reach out to the sites most frequently cited for the prompts that matter to a client and work to get the client mentioned in those articles. The sequencing is the point. Start with the prompts and topics that matter, look at which sites the models list as sources for those prompts, and work backwards to an outreach list. They concede that a mention in any reputable publication helps overall brand marketing, but argue most teams have limited time and budget, so if the goal is specifically AI search visibility for particular prompts, the cited-domain list is the only list worth working. This is evidence-based prospecting rather than following a vague rule about what LLMs supposedly prefer.

> "We reach out to the sites most frequently cited for the prompts that matter"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert run this as a paid citation outreach service for agency clients, built off their Traqer topic reports.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the 10 to 20 prompts whose answers actually influence your pipeline.
2. Log every domain cited across those prompts and count frequency.
3. Drop your own domain and direct competitors from the list.
4. Sort the remaining publications and comparison sites by citation frequency.
5. For each, find the specific cited article, not just the homepage.
6. Pitch an addition or update to that exact article, offering data, a quote or a missing option.
7. Prioritize articles that are listicles or roundups, since those are the ones the models quote for recommendation prompts.
8. Re-run the prompt set after placements land to confirm the new mention is being cited.

**Tools:** Traqer

**Pitfall:** Pitching the publication generally instead of the specific cited article usually fails, because the citation attaches to a URL, not a domain.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's outreach list should be assembled from the aesthetic-software comparison articles that ChatGPT and Perplexity actually cite, and pitches should target those exact URLs to get Pabau added to the list.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your outreach list from the exact articles cited for your key prompts, and pitch an addition to those specific URLs rather than the publication in general.

### 2. Set a written maximum bid before any domain auction opens  `67.7`
*core · best practices · source 67*

Asked where people lose the most money in drop catching, Dirk answers immediately: in the auction. He describes bidding wars where ego takes over and buyers pay far more than they were willing to when they started, and calls it a gambling addiction in its own right. His safeguard is that a business should set the maximum bid for each specific domain in advance and stick to it. The second loss he names is bad homework: monitoring a domain, assuming it is worth some figure, and discovering the real value is significantly less, so you overpay for a domain that returns little. He says overpaying for little value is the outcome in most cases.

> "you set kind of this is the maximum bid I'm willing to do"

**Evidence:** Dirk names auctions as the single place buyers lose the most money, ahead of bad domain selection.

**How to do it**

1. Value each target domain before the auction opens, using ownership history, indexation age and live backlinks rather than DR.
2. Write down a single maximum bid per domain and record the reasoning behind it.
3. Give the number to whoever is bidding and remove their authority to exceed it.
4. Stop bidding at the number even when you are the underbidder by one increment.
5. After each auction, log the final price against your valuation so you can calibrate the next round.
6. Track your hit rate: if you are winning most auctions you are probably valuing too high.
7. Re-run the value check if the auction stretches over days, since new information may appear.

**Pitfall:** Ego bidding. Dirk says people routinely pay much more than they were willing to, and that in most cases the outcome is significant overpayment for a domain that returns little value.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any domain Pabau bids on, whether a defensive brand domain or a competitor's lapsed name, gets a written ceiling and a named person who cannot exceed it.

**Apply anywhere:** For any domain auction, write a maximum bid before it opens, hand it to whoever is bidding, and remove their authority to exceed it.

### 3. Source link exchanges with cheap Twitter ads and a form  `56.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 56*

Cody buys link-exchange partners with paid social instead of prospecting for them. He runs Twitter ads offering a link exchange, targeted globally, with the max CPC bid set at $0.01 - he reports paying about $0.003 per link click. The ad sends to a plain form asking for name, email and company URL. He then filters the inbound list for the highest-value links. The structural detail matters: rather than a reciprocal swap, he links from the partner's site to the asset he wants to build links to, and links to the partner from a different property he owns - so three sites are involved rather than two. He calls it an easy way to build links at scale, particularly when you only want links from one vertical.

> "we do link exchanges all the time"

**How to do it**

1. Set up a landing page that is just a short form: name, email, company URL, and what they want a link for.
2. Run Twitter/X ads offering a link exchange, with global targeting and a max CPC bid at the floor (he uses $0.01).
3. Let volume accumulate, then filter inbound submissions by the metrics you care about - relevance first, then traffic.
4. For each accepted partner, arrange the link to your target asset from their site.
5. Give the return link from a different site in your portfolio rather than the one receiving the link, making it a three-site arrangement rather than a swap.
6. Keep a record of which property linked to whom so patterns don't repeat across partners.

**Tools:** Twitter/X Ads

**Pitfall:** Link exchanges are a link scheme under Google's guidelines regardless of whether a third site is inserted to obscure the reciprocity - the triangle reduces the footprint, it doesn't make the practice compliant.

**Apply at Pabau:** This is not a route Pabau should take - but it is worth knowing that competitors in the practice-management space can be acquiring links at fractions of a cent, which is part of why their link velocity can look inexplicable.

**Apply anywhere:** Not a route to take, but worth knowing that competitors may be acquiring links at fractions of a cent - which is part of why a rival's link velocity can look inexplicable.

### 4. Swap links with adjacent, non-competing local businesses  `39.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 39*

The single strongest link building method identified is adjacent niche outreach: find businesses in complementary industries that serve the same audience in the same location and are not direct competitors, then propose a reciprocal link swap, listing each other as trusted partners, commonly in the website footer. The named example is a roofing company swapping links with a gutter installer, since both serve the same homeowner without competing for the same job; the reasoning given for why this works is that the pitch makes obvious logical sense to both sides, so response rates run higher than cold outreach, and genuine adjacent partners refer business back as well as links. The stated watch-out is that finding the right adjacent businesses at scale takes real time, and the source cautions against overdoing it by listing too many partner links in one place.

> "find businesses in complementary industries serving the same audience"

**How to do it**

1. List every complementary, non-competing business type that serves the exact same customer or audience as your business.
2. For a B2B SaaS business, translate this to other software vendors or service providers selling into the same buyer rather than direct practice-management competitors — for Pabau, this could mean payment processors, insurance or billing services, patient-marketing agencies, or POS vendors serving clinics and med spas. (inferred adaptation to B2B SaaS)
3. Identify specific named companies within each adjacent category that already have brand recognition or traffic worth linking to.
4. Reach out proposing a straightforward link swap: each business adds the other as a trusted partner or recommended provider, typically on a footer, partners page, or integrations/marketplace page.
5. Only pursue this with businesses you would genuinely refer customers to, since these are framed as real, trust-based relationships rather than a pure link-for-link trade.
6. Cap the number of these reciprocal partner links to a small, genuine handful rather than listing dozens, to keep the list credible.
7. Track response rate on this outreach against other outreach types, since adjacent-niche pitches are said to get higher response rates because the logic is immediately obvious to both sides. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Treating this as a numbers game and adding too many reciprocal partner links instead of keeping the list to a genuine, trusted handful of businesses you would actually refer customers to.

### 5. Test every link prospect: would it exist if Google didn't?  `39.7`
*core · best practices · source 39*

A featured comment on the original post argues relevance usually beats raw authority: a contextual link from a smaller but genuinely relevant industry site can move rankings more than a high-domain-rating placement that has zero audience overlap with your business. The proposed winning combination is relevant partnerships plus linkable assets plus outreach that actually makes logical sense, and the practical filter offered for evaluating any prospective link opportunity is to ask whether this link would still make sense if Google didn't exist — if the answer is yes, meaning the link provides real, direct value or traffic to a real audience independent of any SEO benefit, that is treated as a strong positive signal for the link's quality and durability.

> "a contextual link from a smaller but super relevant industry site"

**How to do it**

1. Before pursuing or accepting any link opportunity, ask directly whether this link would still make sense to a human reader or business if Google and SEO did not exist.
2. If the answer is no, meaning the link only exists to manipulate rankings with no real audience or business logic behind it, deprioritize or reject that opportunity regardless of the site's domain rating.
3. If the answer is yes, prioritize that opportunity even if the linking site's raw domain authority or rating is lower than other options being considered.
4. When comparing two possible link placements, weight topical and audience relevance to your actual customer base above the linking site's raw authority metric.
5. Apply this same test when evaluating whether to build a piece of content as a linkable asset, checking whether it provides genuine standalone value to an audience beyond its link-earning purpose. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Chasing a high domain-rating placement that has zero audience overlap with your actual customers, instead of a lower-authority but genuinely relevant, contextual placement that a real audience would find and click regardless of SEO value.

### 6. Theme backlinks to your ICP, not just PageRank  `40.14`
*core · content insights · source 40*

Kasra describes moving away from his old approach of building backlinks purely to pass PageRank and DR, which he now calls 'an old man's game,' toward 'theming' backlinks around who you are, what you do, and who you serve, for example positioning links so they collectively reinforce that a brand is a great SEO who serves SaaS companies. He argues this ICP-relevance theming now matters more than raw authority metrics when building a link profile. He also relays a secondhand claim from Rand Fishkin, attributed to Mike King, that unlinked brand mentions are 'becoming as important as backlinks, if not more important' for building the same kind of consensus signal, though Kasra caveats he doesn't want to misquote Fishkin.

> "Theming backlinks around who you are and your ICP"

**Evidence:** Kasra's own framing shift away from pure PageRank/DR chasing toward 'theming' backlinks around who you are, what you do, and who you serve, illustrated with Edward Sturm as 'a great SEO' who 'serves SaaS companies'; corroborated by a secondhand claim relayed from Rand Fishkin, attributed to Mike King, that unlinked mentions are becoming as important as backlinks or more so.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's link-building targeting should prioritize topical and ICP relevance, meaning sites that plausibly serve or discuss B2B SaaS and practice-management/clinic operations, over raw DR, and unlinked brand mentions should be tracked and valued as a real authority signal in their own right, not dismissed just because they carry no link equity.

**Apply anywhere:** Your link-building targeting should prioritize topical and ICP relevance — sites that plausibly serve or discuss the buyers and the operations you sell into — over raw DR, and unlinked brand mentions should be tracked and valued as a real authority signal in their own right, not dismissed just because they carry no link equity.

### 7. Treat PageRank as fundamental because Google's own guide calls it that  `74.15`
*core · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid takes issue with people claiming backlinks no longer matter, and now that they do not matter for LLMs either. His argument is textual. Google's starter guide says PageRank uses links and is one of the fundamental algorithms at Google. He looks up the word: fundamental means essential, cannot do without. Google also frames it as one of many signals, but he says the word fundamental is all you need to read. He pairs this with the guide's line that the vast majority of new pages Google finds each day come through links, and with its statement that anchor text gives more context on a topic for both users and search engines, which he calls as old as the hills and never changed. He also flags the guide's advice to use nofollow on user-generated content and advertisements to cut spam.

> "Fundamental means essential, cannot do without"

**Evidence:** Google's starter guide describes PageRank as one of the fundamental algorithms at Google, and states the vast majority of new pages are found through links.

**How to do it**

1. Keep a standing link-acquisition budget rather than pausing it because of AI-search commentary.
2. Write descriptive anchor text that states the topic of the destination, since the guide says anchors give topical context.
3. Apply nofollow to user-generated content and any paid or advertised placement on your own site.
4. Prioritize links from pages that already rank and pass real visitors over volume counts.
5. When someone tells you links are dead, ask them to reconcile that with the word 'fundamental' in Google's own guide.
6. Track referring domains alongside rankings so the link programme has its own reported metric.

**Pitfall:** Cutting link investment on the argument that AI search has changed the model. David's counter is that the same guide that answers everything else still names PageRank as fundamental.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep link acquisition funded as its own line rather than folding it into content production. David can use the guide's wording internally when the link budget gets challenged.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep link acquisition funded as its own workstream, write topical anchor text, and nofollow user-generated and paid placements on your own site.

### 8. Trigger manual link building only once a piece already ranks for something  `182.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 182*

Grow and Convert's long-term promotion prong is manual link building, and the trigger they name is specific. They deploy links when certain pieces start ranking for keywords, in order to boost them to page one or to the top of page one. That ordering matters. Links go to pages that have already proved they can rank, so the outreach effort compounds an existing position rather than gambling on a page Google has not yet placed. It also gives you an objective way to choose targets each month: the pages sitting just below the threshold, not the pages you personally like. The paid prong carries the piece during the window before it ranks, so nothing is idle while you wait for the signal.

> "strategically deploy link building to boost them to page 1"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use this as the long-term half of a two-pronged promotion process, with paid ads covering the short term.

**How to do it**

1. Wait until a new piece appears in the rankings for one of its target keywords before spending any outreach time on it.
2. Each month, list pages ranking below the top of page one for a commercial term and rank them by how close they are to the threshold.
3. Pick the pages where a position gain converts, meaning bottom-funnel pages, not top-funnel ones.
4. Run outreach for those pages only, and stop when the page reaches the position you targeted.
5. Keep the paid promotion prong running on pieces that have not started ranking yet, instead of pointing links at them.
6. Re-check the list monthly, since the set of near-threshold pages changes as pieces mature.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Pointing links at a brand-new page that has not ranked at all wastes the strongest asset you can spend, because you cannot tell whether the page was capable of ranking in the first place.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should pick link targets from Search Console each month by proximity to page one on commercial queries, favoring the practice-software comparison and best-software pages over blog explainers.

**Apply anywhere:** Spend link outreach only on pages that already rank for a commercial term and sit just below the position you want, and use paid promotion to cover pages that have not started ranking.

### 9. Upload original images to stock sites with a linked credit  `14.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 14*

A scalable, largely passive backlink tactic (credited to Charles Floate): create original images — phone photos, or Canva/Photoshop graphics such as location photos, data visualizations, infographics, and charts — relevant to your niche, then upload them to free stock-image platforms (Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay, Flickr, Creative Commons) and put your website link directly in the attribution/credit field. Because these stock platforms carry very high domain authority and their images get pulled constantly by bloggers, journalists, and designers, every subsequent use that honors the attribution field generates a free backlink with zero outreach effort. For uses that strip the attribution, a reverse image search (Google Images or TinEye) surfaces the unauthorized users, who convert unusually well when emailed a direct request for a source-credit link, since they already know they used your content without permission.

> "In the attribution credit fields, link back to your website"

**How to do it**

1. Take original photos relevant to your niche from your own phone/camera, or create original graphics in Canva or Photoshop — location photos, data visualizations, infographics, and charts all qualify.
2. Create an account on each major free stock-image platform: Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay, Flickr, and relevant Creative Commons repositories.
3. Upload your original images to each platform.
4. In each platform's attribution/credit field, add a link back to your website.
5. Continue uploading new relevant images on an ongoing basis, since volume compounds the effect — more images uploaded means more links generated over time.
6. Periodically run a reverse image search (Google Images or TinEye) on your uploaded photos to find sites using them without the attribution link intact.
7. Email each site owner found in that search directly, asking them to add a source-credit link since they're already using the image, or delegate this outreach to an AI-assisted email workflow.
8. Track responses; expect an unusually high conversion rate on these asks specifically because the site owner already used your content without permission and has an easy, low-cost way to correct it.

**Tools:** Canva, Photoshop, Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay, Flickr, Google Images, TinEye

### 10. Use a press release to raise your link-velocity ceiling  `58.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 58*

This is the most transferable idea in Jesper's link routine. Google looks at link velocity, so if you build 100 manual link-in-bio and profile links in two days it looks unnatural. But a press release delivers 400 links in one day and doesn't look unnatural, because that's what press releases do. What he observes is that in the aftermath of the press release he can build more links than he otherwise could, because Google is already seeing a legitimate spike - so you can squeeze more links in right after. His rule of thumb when pressed: with a 400-link press release, he'd see no problem building around 40 manual links in the same month - about 10% - split as roughly 30 cheap profile links plus 5-10 guest posts. He'd keep that up for month two, but warns that if you build 40 a month for three months and then pause for two, your velocity resets to zero and you can't jump straight back to 40 - you have to ramp again. He's honest that he doesn't know the exact numbers, only that velocity is being watched. Cody's own baseline is more conservative: five to ten links a month depending on site size, peppered with no-follow social links for early sites.

> "press release has 400 links"

**How to do it**

1. Issue the press release first, so the volume spike has a legitimate explanation.
2. In the same month, add manual links at roughly 10% of the press release's link count.
3. Split those between cheap profile links and a small number of genuine guest posts.
4. Sustain a similar volume in following months rather than spiking and stopping.
5. If you pause, ramp back up gradually - he says velocity resets and you can't resume at the previous rate.
6. For a smaller or newer site, use the conservative baseline instead: five to ten links a month plus no-follow social links.

**Pitfall:** Both hosts are clear that even for their own strongest sites they wouldn't buy more than five or ten links a month - Jesper says he'd rather take it slow because Google might spot the buying pattern.

**Apply at Pabau:** The genuinely useful part for Pabau is the velocity discipline: a sudden burst of links is only defensible if there's a real event behind it, and stopping and restarting a link programme is worse than a steady modest rate.

**Apply anywhere:** The genuinely useful part is the velocity discipline: a sudden burst of links is only defensible if there's a real event behind it, and stopping and restarting a link programme is worse than a steady modest rate.

### 11. Use competitor consensus domains as a "negation set" for link targets  `10.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 10*

When reverse-engineering competitor backlinks, analyze at least 3-5 competitors together, not just one, to find the "consensus domains" — referring domains that link to multiple or all of your top competitors — because those are your highest-priority link targets (if all 5 top competitors have a link from one niche blog, or all have a Crunchbase profile, go get that placement too). Separately, determine whether each competitor link is powering their whole root domain or one specific page, since the real value sometimes flows through an internal link to a supporting page, so you must analyze the competitor's entire site link profile, not just links pointing directly at the ranking page. For links you can't replicate — an internal link, a very old news link, or a direct competitor-to-competitor link e-commerce sites sometimes give each other — Charles's fallback is the "negation set" technique: use your competitors' combined backlink profile as an exclusion list, then hunt for other domains that are still powerful and trusted in your niche but do not already appear in that competitor set, since those uncontested domains become link opportunities your competitors don't have.

> "you can see which are the consensus domains between all of them"

**How to do it**

1. Pull backlink profiles for at least 3-5 of your real SERP competitors, not brand competitors, using a tool such as Ahrefs.
2. Identify referring domains that appear across multiple competitors' profiles ("consensus domains") and prioritize outreach or acquisition from that overlap list first.
3. For each competitor's top-ranking page, check whether its strongest referring links point directly to that page or flow in via an internal link from a different, stronger page on the same domain, reviewing the whole site's link profile rather than just page-level backlinks.
4. Filter out spam or low-value links in the backlink export (Ahrefs has a known spam problem such as recurring Telegram spam) while being aware that "best links only" filters can also hide genuinely good links.
5. Flag any competitor links that are unreplicable — very old news links, internal links, or direct competitor-to-competitor links — and write them off rather than wasting outreach time on them.
6. Compile the full list of domains already linking to your competitor set to build your "negation set."
7. Search for other domains that carry real authority or trust in your niche but do not appear in that negation set.
8. Prioritize outreach to those uncontested domains, since acquiring them gives you a link-profile advantage your competitors structurally cannot match.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, AI/LLM for overlap analysis

**Pitfall:** Ahrefs' backlink data has a real spam problem (e.g., recurring Telegram spam links), and applying a "best links only" filter to clean it up will also hide many genuinely good, worth-targeting links — filter manually rather than trusting either extreme.

### 12. Use paid promotion to find which articles convert, then build links to those  `108.15`
*core · concrete actions · source 108*

Grow and Convert describe a prioritization method that removes guesswork from link-building targets. They run paid promotion to their published articles, watch which pieces generate conversions from that paid traffic, and then prioritize those specific articles for link building. The reasoning is that the article has already proved business value at a small cost, so if they can get it ranking organically for its target keyword, they are confident it will keep converting. This inverts the usual approach of pointing links at whichever page has the most search volume or the weakest backlink profile. It also gives a cheap, fast read on conversion performance long before organic rankings arrive, which normally takes months. The cost is the ad spend, and the requirement is conversion tracking accurate enough to attribute signups to individual articles.

> "When we run paid promotion to articles and see that certain pieces are generating"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's agency practice: prioritize link building for articles already generating conversions from paid promotion, on the basis that organic rankings will reproduce that conversion behavior.

**How to do it**

1. Set up conversion tracking that attributes demo requests and signups to the landing article, not just to the site.
2. Pick the ten bottom-funnel articles you would most like to rank and run a small paid campaign to each.
3. Send comparable spend and traffic to each article so the comparison is fair.
4. Run each test long enough to record conversions, not just clicks and time on page.
5. Rank the articles by conversions per hundred visits.
6. Move the top performers to the front of the link-building queue.
7. Build links to those specific URLs, through guest posts, listicle inclusion or PR placements.
8. Leave the non-converting articles unlinked and diagnose whether the intent match or the product section is at fault.
9. Repeat the test each quarter as new articles publish.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Running the test with only site-level conversion tracking. Without per-article attribution the ranking is meaningless and the link budget goes to whichever page happened to get the most clicks.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau could run small paid campaigns to its bottom-funnel blog articles and template pages, measure demo bookings per article, then aim outreach at the two or three that actually convert instead of the highest-traffic posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Run small paid campaigns to your bottom-funnel articles, measure conversions per article, and point your link building at the proven converters. It buys a conversion read months before organic rankings would give one.

### 13. Vet every link target by real traffic and rankings, never by DA  `08.16`
*core · best practices · source 08*

Before acquiring any link, especially through a guest-post marketplace, every candidate domain should be run through Ahrefs and/or Semrush to check whether it gets real organic Google traffic and whether it ranks for keywords topically similar to your own targets — Domain Authority/Domain Rating is explicitly disregarded as a decision factor. Sites ranking only for suspicious, unrelated queries designed to inflate apparent keyword counts are rejected outright. For previously-approved sites, a small decline in rankings (e.g., dropping from roughly 1,000 to 200-250 ranking keywords) is tolerated with reduced risk exposure (using a brand or bare-URL link instead of exact-match anchor text), while a collapse to near-zero keywords means dropping the site entirely; a running spreadsheet of vetted sites and their ranking keywords is maintained so future placements can be pulled from an already-approved pool.

> "I don't care about DA at all"

**How to do it**

1. Before acquiring any link from a marketplace or outreach contact, pull the candidate domain through Ahrefs and/or Semrush rather than judging it by eye or marketplace reputation.
2. Explicitly disregard Domain Authority/Domain Rating as a decision factor.
3. Check whether the domain is getting real organic Google traffic at all.
4. Check whether the domain ranks for keywords topically similar to your own targets — it doesn't need an exact category match, but the rankings should reflect genuine topical authority, not manipulated or irrelevant queries.
5. Reject any site whose visible rankings are for suspicious, unrelated terms seemingly designed to fake keyword-count authority.
6. Maintain a running spreadsheet of vetted sites and the keywords each ranks for, so future link placements can be pulled from an already-approved pool.
7. For a previously-approved site showing a moderate ranking decline (e.g., from roughly 1,000 to 200-250 ranking keywords), keep using it but switch to a brand or bare-URL link instead of exact-match anchor text.
8. For a site collapsing to near-zero ranking keywords, drop it entirely rather than continuing to use it at reduced risk.
9. Split link types by site strength across every batch: reserve keyword-rich anchor text for your strongest vetted sites, and pair every anchor-text link with an accompanying brand-mention link and a bare-URL link elsewhere in the same batch to keep a natural-looking ratio.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, iCopify

**Pitfall:** Judging link quality by Domain Authority or by trusting a marketplace's own listing quality — a named marketplace (iCopify) is called out as having 'a lot of trash sites on it that'll ruin your link profile' if candidates aren't independently verified for real traffic and topical rankings first.

### 14. Vet guest-post targets for real traffic before pitching  `39.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 39*

For niche blog outreach, search your service or niche plus the word blog, then contact the top-ranking sites about publishing an article mentioning your client, since this works because it is contextually relevant and editors are already actively publishing in your niche. The critical watch-out before agreeing to any placement is to check that the target site has real organic traffic and clicks on the actual page you would be published on, and specifically confirm that traffic is steady or growing rather than declining, since falling organic traffic is a signal the site may be a link farm or is losing Google's trust. Most outreach targets will not reply, but the ones that do are worth pursuing, and Google is noted as being good at detecting spammy guest-blogging networks used purely for link building, which is exactly what this vetting step protects against.

> "check the site that you would publish a guest post on"

**How to do it**

1. Search Google for your service or niche term plus the word blog, and separately try the niche term plus the phrase write for us in quotes, to surface candidate sites.
2. For each candidate site, check its organic traffic trend using a tool like Ahrefs or Semrush, specifically looking at whether traffic is steady or increasing over recent months.
3. Reject any candidate site whose organic traffic is declining, since this is a red flag for link-farm status or a site losing Google's trust.
4. Check that the specific page or section you would be published in shows real organic clicks, not just raw traffic elsewhere on the site.
5. Confirm the site's other guest posts show topical variety and genuine editorial quality rather than being a uniform run of interchangeable guest-post content.
6. Only proceed with outreach and pitch an article mentioning your client or business once a candidate site passes all of the above checks.
7. Expect a low reply rate overall, and treat the sites that do respond positively as the valuable outcome rather than judging the method by its overall response percentage. (inferred)

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Google Search

**Pitfall:** Agreeing to a guest post placement without checking the target site's real organic traffic trend first — publishing on a site that is actually a link farm or has declining organic traffic risks the placement being worthless or risky, since Google is described as good at detecting spammy guest-blogging networks.

### 15. Vet link opportunities by traffic trend, not DA/DR  `24.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 24*

Before exchanging, requesting, or paying for a backlink, the recommended check is to ignore third-party vanity metrics like Domain Authority or Domain Rating — explicitly noted as metrics Google itself doesn't use — and instead pull the target page or subfolder's organic traffic-over-time graph in Ahrefs or Semrush. Reject placements on pages/subfolders showing a sharp decline down to near-zero traffic, since that signals the section may be losing relevance or being deindexed, and separately confirm the specific page is both indexed and currently has real ranking keywords (not just that the domain overall carries authority). This reframes link vetting around the specific page's health rather than the domain's aggregate score.

> "Look at traffic over time from the websites with graphs"

**How to do it**

1. Before accepting, exchanging, or paying for a backlink placement, ignore third-party metrics like Domain Authority (Moz) or Domain Rating (Ahrefs).
2. Open Ahrefs or Semrush and pull the specific target page or subfolder's organic traffic-over-time graph, not just the domain's overall score.
3. Reject any page/subfolder showing a sharp decline down to near-zero traffic.
4. Confirm the specific page/subfolder where your link would live is actually indexed by Google. (inferred: via a site: search or GSC URL Inspection)
5. Check that page/subfolder's ranking-keywords report in Ahrefs or Semrush to confirm it currently ranks for real terms.
6. Only proceed with the exchange, placement, or payment once the page passes both the traffic-trend and ranking-keywords checks.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Judging a link opportunity by domain-wide DA/DR scores instead of the actual traffic trend and ranking keywords of the specific page or subfolder your link will live on, since DA/DR are third-party metrics Google doesn't use.

### 16. Wait a few weeks after publishing before deciding whether to build links  `128.5`
*core · best practices · source 128*

Grow and Convert do not build links to a new article immediately. They wait a few weeks to see where it settles, because Google tests a new article in various positions in the SERPs to see how people react before assigning it a stable position. Until that test period is over, the position you are looking at is not the position you would be buying links against, so both the decision and the budget can be wrong. Their mechanical step is to add the target keyword to Ahrefs Rank Tracker at publication, so the settling curve is recorded rather than spot-checked. Only after the position stabilizes do they run their prioritization: where it sits with no links built, and whether it has shown conversion potential elsewhere.

> "We typically wait a few weeks to see where the article sits"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert wait a few weeks post-publication because Google tests articles in various SERP positions, and they log each target keyword in Ahrefs Rank Tracker at publish.

**How to do it**

1. At publication, add the article's target keyword to Ahrefs Rank Tracker with the article URL noted against it.
2. Build no links to the piece for the first few weeks.
3. Watch the tracked position through the test period rather than checking rank once.
4. Treat the position after it stops swinging, not the first reading, as the article's real starting point.
5. Once settled, record the position and whether any backlinks were built.
6. Run the link-priority check only then: unlinked position, plus conversion evidence from another channel.
7. Keep the keyword in the tracker after links go live so you can attribute movement to the link building.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Reacting to the first week's ranking. Google's testing phase can show a position several pages away from where the article settles, so links get assigned to the wrong articles.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should add every new Pabau target keyword to a rank tracker on the day the page publishes, and hold any outreach or internal-link push for that page until the position has stopped moving.

**Apply anywhere:** Add each new article's target keyword to a rank tracker on publication day and build no links for the first few weeks. Google tests new pages across positions before settling them, so the first reading is not the baseline you should be spending a link budget against.

### 17. Wait weeks before building links, then add one or two a month  `153.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 153*

Grow and Convert treat link building as a triage tool rather than a default. They wait several weeks after publishing to see how a piece performs on its own. If it ranks well immediately or is steadily climbing, they leave it alone and put attention on other posts. Only when a post stalls, or when they want a specific post to move faster, do they start building links, and the pace is one or two links per month until the rankings arrive. Then they stop. This is a deliberately small number set against the common advice to build lots of backlinks, and it fits their finding that time and topical relevance carry most of the result.

> "we'll start building one or two links per month until we get the rankings we want"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated process across 80+ clients since 2017: wait several weeks post-publication, then build one or two links per month only for posts that stall.

**How to do it**

1. Publish the post and add no links to it for at least several weeks.
2. Check its position weekly and label it climbing, flat, or not ranking.
3. Leave anything climbing alone and spend the effort on a different post.
4. For a flat post you care about, start a link campaign at one or two links per month.
5. Prioritize links from sites in the same topic, since relevance is what is carrying the rankings.
6. Recheck position monthly and stop building as soon as the target ranking is reached.
7. Keep a list of stalled posts so link budget always goes to the ones that need it, not the newest ones.
8. Do not start link building for a post that has been live under a month.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Buying or building links to every new post on publication. You spend budget on pages that would have ranked anyway, and you cannot tell which links did anything.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not commission links for new /blog/ or /templates/ pages at launch. David should run a monthly review of pages live for six weeks or more, and direct link effort at the flat ones only, one or two links a month each.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish with no links, wait several weeks, and only start building links for posts that have gone flat. One or two links a month per stalled post is enough, and you stop as soon as it ranks.

### 18. Widen to adjacent-industry sites, keep a tight on-topic content chunk  `32.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 32*

When a niche is too small to find enough directly on-topic websites for guest content (the case study is a venture-backed petrochemical/chemical-manufacturing startup), pivot from targeting sites that exactly match the niche to broader, open-ended adjacent industry sites (general engineering/industrial sites instead of only chemical-manufacturing ones), while keeping a specific, tightly-scoped content block — three to four paragraphs structured with H2 and H3 subheaders directly about the precise sub-topic — embedded within the more generic host article, carrying the target keyword. In this case study, the pivot took a further two months to fully pay off but resulted in ranking an "annoyingly complicated" target keyword ahead of major, much larger chemical-manufacturing competitors.

> "to rank an annoyingly complicated keyword ahead of these major"

**How to do it**

1. When your niche has too few directly-matching websites to sustain a link-building campaign, identify the next ring of adjacent industries or topics outward from your exact niche.
2. Pitch and place content on these broader adjacent sites instead of holding out only for perfect-topic matches.
3. Within each placement, write a dedicated 3-4 paragraph block structured with its own H2 and H3 subheaders that stays tightly focused on your specific sub-topic and target keyword, even though the surrounding article is more generic.
4. Include the target keyword naturally within that tightly-scoped block rather than diluting it across the more generic surrounding content.
5. Continue this placement pattern consistently for at least two months before judging ranking impact, since the case study's ranking gain took that long to materialize.
6. Track the target keyword's position against named major competitors specifically, not just generic rank movement, to measure whether the pivot is working.

**Pitfall:** Holding out for perfect niche-relevance matches when your industry is too small to supply enough of them causes campaigns to stall — insisting on directly on-topic sites only, rather than widening to adjacent industries, is the mistake this case study pivoted away from.

### 19. Your investors' portfolio is the fastest available link source  `57.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 57*

Cody's standing advice to the startups he works with: the biggest asset you have is that you can get a backlink from everybody in your investors' portfolios, and from the investors' own sites. He describes taking domain authority from zero to around 70 in weeks, purely through guest posts placed across portfolio sites that already carry substantial authority. It's a route new companies systematically overlook because it doesn't look like link building - it looks like an introduction. Jackie's counterpoint in the same exchange is worth keeping: he says link building is his weakest area and his strength is finding long-tail terms and scaling the operation, which is a reminder that these are separable skills.

> "you can get a backlink from everybody in their portfolio"

**How to do it**

1. List every investor on your cap table, and for each one, list their full portfolio.
2. Filter that list for companies with a blog and real domain authority.
3. Ask the investor for the introduction rather than cold-pitching the portfolio company.
4. Offer a genuine guest post on a topic where your team has expertise their audience wants - the relevance is what makes it worth publishing.
5. Include the investor's own site and any portfolio-wide publications in the list.
6. Sequence the placements over weeks rather than landing them all at once.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Portfolio-wide link exchanges arranged by an investor can produce a recognisable footprint if every company links to every other. Relevance and pacing are what keep it defensible.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's equivalent is the partner and integration ecosystem - suppliers, integration partners, industry bodies and customer clinics are the natural analogue to a portfolio, and guest content there is both relevant and available.

**Apply anywhere:** If you have no investors, the analogue is your partner and integration ecosystem - suppliers, integration partners, industry bodies and customers are the natural equivalent of a portfolio, and guest content there is both relevant and available.
