### 1. Name the adjacent pain a feature removes, not just the feature  `92.14`
*useful · best practices · source 92*

Grow and Convert credit HubSpot with one good line about their client's product: it offers automatic translations and access to professional translators if needed. Those are the right value props, automation and accuracy. But beyond the casual if needed framing, they point out the line misses a critical related value prop: you can order professional translations directly from the platform, which solves the massive pain of having to find your own translators. That is the pattern worth generalizing. A feature usually removes a second, adjacent chore that the buyer dreads more than the headline task, and that adjacent chore is what makes the copy land. Their broader list for this product includes translating SEO meta information automatically and translating a site without developers, both of which are adjacent-pain value props rather than headline features.

> "you can order professional translations right from the platform"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list three adjacent-pain value props uncovered in interviews: ordering professional translation in-platform, automatic translation of SEO meta info, and translating everything without developers.

**How to do it**

1. For each feature you name in the draft, ask what the buyer had to do separately before this feature existed.
2. Ask specifically whether the old way involved finding, hiring or coordinating a third party.
3. Ask whether it required a developer, and say so plainly if the feature removes that dependency.
4. Write the value prop as feature plus removed chore in one sentence.
5. Check the removed chore against interview notes to confirm buyers actually complain about it.
6. Place the adjacent-pain sentence immediately after the feature sentence, not in a later bullet.

**Pitfall:** Naming the feature and stopping is technically complete and commercially flat. The signal is a bullet list of capabilities with no sentence describing what the buyer no longer has to arrange.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau content names a feature, it should also name the coordination the practice stops doing: no separate booking tool to reconcile, no paper consent form to scan, no developer needed to change a form. That is the sentence practice owners react to.

**Apply anywhere:** When you name a feature, also name the coordination the buyer stops doing: no separate tool to reconcile, no third party to hire, no developer needed. That is the sentence buyers react to.

### 2. Name the specific scenario a page serves in its own copy  `78.14`
*useful · best practices · source 78*

The mechanism running through Grow and Convert's whole approach is matching: LLMs understand what a user needs, search the web, read the results, combine that with prior knowledge and recommend the product that best fits the user's specific pain points. They are explicit that models are not just surfacing articles from high-authority domains. For that matching to land on your page, the page has to state the scenario in plain words a model can align with a user's description. Constitution Lending's content names scenarios like LLC mortgages and DSCR loans for specific property types rather than describing lending generally. The rule that falls out is simple: every commercial page should say who it is for and in what situation, in its own copy.

> "recommend the product or service that best fits"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe LLMs reading web results and recommending the product that best fits the user's specific pain points, not simply surfacing high-authority domains.

**How to do it**

1. Write a one-sentence scenario statement for every commercial page: who the reader is and what situation they are in.
2. Put that sentence in the opening paragraph and echo it in the title where it fits naturally.
3. Use the customer's own phrasing from interview recordings rather than internal category language.
4. Name the competitor or prior tool the reader is likely leaving, where that is part of the scenario.
5. Avoid pronouns when naming the entity, so the scenario and the brand appear together in the same sentence.
6. Reject any draft whose scenario statement would fit three other pages on the site unchanged.

**Pitfall:** Writing pages that describe a product but never state who it is for. The model has nothing to align with the user's stated situation, so it recommends a competitor whose page does say it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau commercial and template page should open with the practice type and situation it serves, written in the words practices use, rather than opening with a definition of practice management software.

**Apply anywhere:** Give every commercial page a one-sentence statement of who it is for and what situation they are in, written in the customer's words. If that sentence would fit three other pages unchanged, it is not specific enough.

### 3. Name your product inside the answer to the job the reader searched  `142.13`
*useful · best practices · source 142*

Grow and Convert describe how they make jobs-to-be-done posts convert despite the searcher not knowing the brand or the category. They say they insert their clients' names into these conversations by creating content that specifically solves what the reader is searching for, in the context of the client's solution. So the post genuinely answers the how-to query, and the product appears as the way to do that job, not as a bolted-on pitch at the end. That framing is what produced 9.71% on 'collect video testimonials', 12.5% on 'how to run standups in Slack' and 10.48% on 'record website visitors'. The reader arrives unaware of the brand and leaves having started a trial, because the product was presented as the mechanism for the task they already decided to do.

> "We insert our clients' names in these conversations"

**Evidence:** JTBD posts written this way converted at 9.71%, 12.5% and 10.48% for three different clients.

**How to do it**

1. Pick a JTBD query where finishing the job requires software.
2. Outline the genuine step-by-step answer to that query first, with no product mentions.
3. Identify the step in that walkthrough your product performs.
4. Rewrite that step to show the product doing it, with a screenshot and the exact clicks.
5. Keep the rest of the walkthrough honest and useful, including steps your product does not cover.
6. Close with the job completed, then the trial or demo link as the next step.
7. Compare the post's conversion rate against the 2.44% JTBD benchmark after 90 days.

**Pitfall:** Writing the how-to neutrally and adding a product CTA at the bottom. That structure leaves the reader to make the connection themselves, and the conversion rate falls back toward the informational baseline.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's how-to articles on practice tasks should present Pabau as the way to complete the task inside the walkthrough itself, with screenshots, rather than adding a demo CTA at the end and leaving the body vendor-neutral.

**Apply anywhere:** Present your product as the way to complete the task inside the walkthrough itself, with screenshots, rather than writing a neutral how-to and appending a CTA at the end.

### 4. Never narrate your own SEO research to the reader  `71.9`
*useful · best practices · source 71*

Devesh cut a passage because it talked about the SERP too directly. His rule is that you cannot write an SEO piece that says here is what I saw when I was researching this SEO piece. The reader did not come for your process; they came for the answer. He replaced the research narration with a reader-facing frame, turning 'to fix these problems above' into 'if you want to avoid the problems above', and then pushed the whole passage toward second person. The same instinct led him to cut a paragraph about Grow and Convert's history, which he described as a VC pitch a reader looking for a content brief generator does not need. Both cuts come from the same rule: speak to the reader's situation, not about your own work.

> "You got to actually speak to the reader"

**Evidence:** Devesh cut a passage for talking about the SERP too directly, and separately cut a paragraph on Grow and Convert's decade of work, calling it a VC pitch the reader does not need.

**How to do it**

1. Search the draft for first-person research narration, phrases like 'when we analyzed', 'looking at the SERP' or 'in our research'.
2. Rewrite each into a second-person frame stating what the reader should do or avoid.
3. Replace directive openers like 'to fix these problems' with reader-choice openers like 'if you want to avoid these problems'.
4. Cut company-history and credentials paragraphs from the body unless the keyword is a brand or comparison query.
5. Keep the underlying research finding, only change who the sentence is about.
6. Read the section back and confirm every paragraph is about the reader's problem, not your process.

**Pitfall:** Research narration feels like proof of rigor to the writer and reads as self-absorption to the searcher. The signal is a paragraph whose subject is 'we' when the keyword is a how-to or a tool query.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog and template articles should keep the audit or research finding but frame it as the reader's problem. The company-credentials paragraph belongs in the Pabau section before the Conclusion, not scattered through the body.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep your research findings but strip the narration of how you got them, and move company credentials out of the body into the one section where they belong.

### 5. Outsourced lifestyle content loses on both conversion and brand  `112.14`
*useful · content insights · source 112*

Grow and Convert name the specific failure mode of the default B2C content program. The freelancers choose random topics loosely connected to the space, such as best hiking trails for an outdoor apparel company, and produce thin, unoriginal content. The result is that these brands end up getting the worst of both worlds. The topics do not relate to selling the product, so they barely convert to sales, and the writing does little to impress or engage potential customers, so there is no brand building either. They tie this to a related belief they consider wrong: because B2C customers are largely beginners, many consumer brands think they can hire any writer. Their counterexample is the '10 tips for runners' post full of cliches about stretching and consistency that every runner has already heard a thousand times.

> "brands end up getting the worst of both worlds"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's agency observation of B2C blogs, with the hiking trails and '10 tips for runners' examples.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's audience is professional, so the beginner-writer assumption never applied. The transferable point is topic selection: a piece that a freelancer could have chosen at random from the aesthetics space is the piece to cut, whatever its word count.

**Apply anywhere:** Outsourced lifestyle content usually fails twice: the topics are too distant from the product to convert, and the writing is too generic to build the brand. A post that any writer could have picked at random from your industry is the one to cut.

### 6. Outspend an amateur category on design because everyone else looks cheap  `171.10`
*useful · best practices · source 171*

Sol Orwell attributes the ongoing success of the Research Digest to deliberate differentiation on production quality. His read on the fitness and health information market is blunt: most products in it are run amateurly. He lists the tells, saying there are typos, the images are bad, the graphics are bad, the correspondence is bad. Examine's response was to invest properly in graphic design so the digest looked, in his word, gorgeous. This sits alongside the $41,000 domain as the other place where they spent upfront rather than running lean. The point Grow & Convert draw out is that lean versus not-lean is not a binary; it is hundreds of separate decisions about where quality is the differentiator.

> "There are typos, the images are shit"

**Evidence:** Sol says most fitness and health products carry typos and poor graphics; Examine invested in design to make the Research Digest look 'gorgeous', and it remained their durable product.

**How to do it**

1. Buy or download the five best-selling products in your category and audit them for typos, image quality and email correspondence.
2. Write down the specific failures you find, since those are the differentiators available to you.
3. Hire a real designer for the deliverable the customer holds, not for the marketing around it.
4. Proofread every customer-facing asset, including transactional and follow-up emails, to the same standard as the product.
5. Spend upfront on the pieces the customer judges you by, and stay lean everywhere else.
6. Re-audit competitors annually, because the gap closes once someone else notices it.

**Pitfall:** Applying the same spend level to everything. Examine was lean on most decisions and expensive on two, the domain and the design, and the skill is deciding which is which per decision rather than adopting a philosophy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's templates and downloadable resources compete in a category full of plain Word documents. David should keep the design and visual investment on downloadables high, because production quality alone separates them from what practices currently use.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit the top products in your category for typos, weak graphics and sloppy emails. Where the category is amateur, production quality alone is a differentiator, so spend properly on the artifact the customer actually holds and stay lean elsewhere.

### 7. Paraphrase the expert back and invent an analogy to test understanding  `134.9`
*useful · best practices · source 134*

Grow and Convert's fifth interviewing tactic is a comprehension check performed live. You repeat back what you think the expert said, and you do it in language that balances being accessible to a wide audience with staying technically precise, which they argue is simply what good writing is. If the paraphrase is wrong the expert corrects it on the call rather than after publication. The writer's favorite variant is to invent an analogy on the spot and check it. Their running example is that everything is a type of car: a spoon is a car that carries cereal from the bowl to the mouth, an email is a car that carries words from one laptop to another. The analogy does double duty, since a paraphrase the expert signs off on is usually the sentence that ends up in the article.

> "Paraphrase Everything the SME Says Back to Them"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert credit this with writing at the level of QA specialists in a field they had not worked in.

**How to do it**

1. After each substantial answer, say 'so what I think you're saying is' and restate it in plain language.
2. Aim the restatement at a smart non-specialist while keeping every technical detail intact.
3. Invent an analogy for the mechanism and ask directly whether it holds or where it breaks.
4. Note the exact correction when the expert pushes back; that phrasing is more accurate than yours.
5. Keep the approved paraphrases as draft sentences rather than rewriting them later from raw notes.
6. Send the final analogy back for sign-off if it will carry a whole section.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
So what I think you're saying is [plain-language restatement]. It's a bit like [analogy]. Is that right, or where does that break down?
```

**Pitfall:** Nodding through an explanation you half-understood and reconstructing it from notes days later. The error surfaces in review from the expert, or worse in the comments from the audience.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau writers should paraphrase clinical or billing explanations back on the call and get the analogy approved there, since an error in a procedure-code or diagnostic-code page is expensive to correct after publication.

**Apply anywhere:** Restate every expert answer in plain language on the call and test an analogy out loud. The version the expert approves is the sentence you publish.

### 8. Pick a niche narrow enough that nobody can match your content  `168.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 168*

CPC Strategy specialized in data-feed driven advertising, a narrow slice of the broader ad-strategy market. Nii Ahene frames the niching decision as a content advantage rather than a positioning one: because the specialty was so specific, few companies could compete with them on creating relevant content for it. The same narrowness made the early SEO work easier, since the keywords in a very narrow niche had far less competition than general paid-media terms. It also made the PR work easier because there were few alternative sources. The pattern is that a single narrowing decision paid off across three channels at once, and each of those advantages erodes as the niche broadens.

> "Niching down in the competitive business of ad strategy"

**Evidence:** CPC Strategy focused on data-feed driven advertising and grew to 135 people, with Nii Ahene attributing its content edge to the narrowness of the specialty.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the broad market you nominally serve, then name the specific mechanism or workflow you know better than anyone in it.
2. Test the narrowing by asking whether more than five companies could write your top ten planned articles credibly. If yes, narrow again.
3. Check keyword difficulty for the narrowed terms and confirm the SERPs are held by generalist pages rather than dedicated specialists.
4. Count how many citable sources already exist for the niche's core statistics. Zero or one means the PR opening is real.
5. Publish your first 20 articles entirely inside the narrow slice, resisting the pull toward the broader category.
6. Only broaden once you rank for the narrow terms and have an audience that would follow you outward.
7. Re-audit annually, because a niche that attracts entrants stops being a content moat.

**Pitfall:** Broadening too early to chase larger search volume drops you into SERPs held by better-resourced generalists, and you lose the content advantage that made the narrow terms winnable.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's edge is aesthetic and healthcare practice operations, not practice management software generally. Blog and template topics should sit inside treatment-specific and clinic-workflow-specific territory where a horizontal SaaS competitor cannot write credibly.

**Apply anywhere:** Narrow until fewer than five companies could credibly write your planned articles, confirm the SERPs are held by generalists, then publish your first 20 pieces entirely inside that slice before broadening.

### 9. Pick the pain-point keyword one step away from the purchase decision  `139.14`
*useful · content insights · source 139*

Brandon's test for whether a pain-point keyword was worth targeting was proximity to the money problem, not commercial words in the phrase. He prioritized terms where the searcher was already looking for a way to save money or handle their taxes, such as 'receipts for taxes', 'track miles for taxes', 'home office deduction' and 'how much should I set aside for 1099 taxes', over higher-volume general terms like 'tax advice'. His justification for 'receipts for taxes' is instructive: a person searching it at least indicates they are saving or thinking about saving receipts, so some fraction of them have business expenses and could want software that finds more of them. That is the whole qualification test. The searcher does not have to be product-aware, only one identifiable step from the problem the product solves.

> "these keywords have higher conversion intent than say, "tax advice"

**Evidence:** Keeper Tax prioritized 'receipts for taxes', 'track miles for taxes' and 'home office deduction' over 'tax advice', and signups from content grew over 700% a month.

**How to do it**

1. List every candidate informational keyword from the interviews and the term tools.
2. For each, write the sentence describing what the searcher is doing in their life at that moment.
3. Ask whether that action costs them money or time that your product recovers.
4. Drop candidates where the searcher is browsing for general education with no cost attached.
5. Keep candidates where a plausible fraction of searchers have the exact problem you fix.
6. Rank the survivors by how directly the article can introduce the product as the fix.
7. Write each article to solve the problem manually first, then name the product as the faster route.
8. Compare per-page signup rates after 90 days and drop keyword shapes that do not convert.

**Pitfall:** General category terms like 'tax advice' pull large volume from people with no specific problem to solve, and the resulting traffic reports well and converts near zero.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should favour queries where a practice is already losing money or time, such as reducing no-shows or chasing unpaid invoices, over general clinic-management education terms with more volume.

**Apply anywhere:** Prefer informational keywords where the searcher is already dealing with the costly problem your product fixes, over higher-volume general education terms in the same subject.

### 10. Plan for a flooded web where human content carries a premium  `174.8`
*useful · general insights · source 174*

Khanal's forecast is that AI written content will be a race to the bottom. Companies experiment, the web fills with more bad content, and in a year or two the content most valued will be the pieces produced by humans. His reasoning is about demand rather than algorithms: people have always wanted to read something that challenges their thinking, educates them on something new, or offers a differing opinion, and averaged output cannot do any of those. Written in 2023, the prediction is a planning assumption rather than a proven result, and it argues for a counter-cyclical bet. As competitors increase volume, the scarce and therefore valuable asset becomes the piece nobody else could have written. That reframes budget away from more pages and towards fewer pages with real input behind them.

> "AI written content will be a race to the bottom"

**Evidence:** Khanal's prediction, written when ChatGPT-driven content production was accelerating: within a year or two the most valued content will be human-produced, because readers want to be challenged, taught something new or given a differing opinion.

**How to do it**

1. Model your content plan on scarcity: assume the number of competing pages on your topics doubles or more.
2. Shift budget from page count to input cost, funding interviews, original data collection and product access.
3. Pick two or three topics a quarter where you can publish something nobody else can, and over-invest there.
4. Keep the routine coverage lean, since its competitive value falls as everyone can produce it.
5. Track whether your differentiated pieces earn links, citations and shares at a higher rate than routine ones.
6. Reallocate the following quarter's budget towards whichever type actually earned attention.

**Pitfall:** Treating this as a reason to stop publishing routine content entirely. Routine coverage still catches search demand; the mistake is funding it at the expense of the few pieces that differentiate you.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should reserve part of the pabau.com calendar each quarter for pieces only Pabau could publish, such as anonymized patterns from how aesthetic practices configure booking and follow-ups. Keep routine template coverage efficient and fund the differentiated pieces properly.

**Apply anywhere:** Assume the number of competing pages on your topics keeps rising, and plan for scarcity. Move budget from page count to input cost: interviews, original data, product access. Reserve two or three slots a quarter for pieces nobody else could publish, keep routine coverage lean, and rebalance based on which type actually earns links and citations.

### 11. Predict the article from its title to catch generic ideas early  `98.3`
*useful · best practices · source 98*

Benji Hyam's cheap screening test. He argues the title alone gives away whether a post will be boring, and demonstrates by predicting the shape of 'How to write valuable content' before it exists: a generic intro about content marketing being the key to success, then a paragraph teeing up the problem, then six or seven high-level ideas each covered in one to three paragraphs. His point is that readers do this prediction unconsciously and skip the article. He calls this the number one reason companies fail to write compelling posts, and says everyone has seen four hundred of these come through their social feeds in the past year. The test is fast enough to run on every headline in a content calendar.

> "Your Audience Can Smell the Stench of These Generic Ideas From Just the Title"

**Evidence:** Hyam names generic-topic selection as the number one reason companies fail to write compelling blog posts.

**How to do it**

1. Take each headline on the content calendar and write out, in three bullets, what you predict the article will contain.
2. If your prediction is a listicle of six or seven high-level ideas at one to three paragraphs each, kill the headline.
3. If you cannot predict the content because the title names a specific situation or claim, keep it.
4. Rewrite killed headlines so they name one concrete situation, decision or contrarian claim.
5. Re-run the prediction test on the rewritten headline before it goes into a brief.
6. Apply the same test to competitor headlines on the SERP to find topics where every ranking page is predictable.

**Pitfall:** Writers defend a predictable title by promising the body will be better. It rarely is, because a broad title forces broad coverage. Fix the title first.

**Apply at Pabau:** Run this on Pabau's blog headlines. Any title where the likely body is 'seven tips for X, three paragraphs each' should be reworked into a single narrow question a practice owner actually asks.

**Apply anywhere:** Before commissioning, predict the article from its headline. If you can guess the whole thing, so can the reader, and the piece will not stand out no matter how well it is written.

### 12. Prefer described scenarios over statistics when backing a pain point  `99.4`
*useful · best practices · source 99*

Khanal makes a deliberate contrarian point about stats. Many bloggers cite statistics to back up pain points, and he thinks that is generally less effective than describing scenarios, behaviors and feelings. His test case: which lands harder for an attribution pain point, the multi-touch scenario, or a stat like '86% of marketers say they're not measuring conversions from content'. His reaction to the stat is 'who cares what percentage of marketers aren't doing this? I want to know that you understand me and my problems.' Note this is a split, not a blanket rule. Elsewhere in the same article he treats data as a legitimate backup type for claims, and he says data should be told directly. The distinction is by target: stats back claims well, scenarios back pain points well. He flags that the point is subjective and that a million exceptions exist.

> "They don't make people feel like the author really understands their life"

**Evidence:** Khanal's comparison of the attribution scenario against the '86% of marketers' style stat, with his stated reader reaction.

**How to do it**

1. Sort each unbacked passage into claim or pain point before choosing a backup type.
2. For claims, allow a statistic, a client figure or a case result as the backup.
3. For pain points, write a described scenario or consequence instead of a survey stat.
4. If a stat is the only thing you have for a pain point, use it as a supporting aside after the scenario, not in place of it.
5. Drop any industry-survey stat that describes marketers or practitioners in aggregate rather than the reader.
6. Check the pain-point passage would still work if the stat were deleted.

**Pitfall:** Aggregate industry stats read as filler because the reader is not curious what percentage of their peers share the problem; they want evidence the author understands their specific situation.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau intros lean on industry statistics about missed appointments. Keep those in the body as claim support, but open the pain-point passage with the described clinic scenario instead.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep industry statistics in the body as claim support, but open pain-point passages with a described scenario rather than a survey number.

### 13. Publish the failure post-mortem instead of another success story  `183.9`
*useful · content insights · source 183*

This entire piece is a post-mortem on a launch that hit a little under half its revenue goal, published deliberately. Grow and Convert open by saying it is not another online course success story and is quite the opposite. Their argument is that the space is full of posts making it look easy, of the shape build an email list, create a course, and you are at six figures. Publishing the mistake does two jobs: it differentiates against a SERP of success stories, and it demonstrates the diagnostic skill they sell, because the value is in the analysis of why personas and positioning were wrong, not in the outcome. They also use the post to announce the new agency and to poll readers about a course for individuals. A failure post with a real diagnosis is a positioning asset, not an admission.

> "it's important to share our mistakes so that we can help prevent you"

**Evidence:** The launch reached a little under half its revenue goal, and the post about that failure carries the announcement of the agency that replaced it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog leans toward best-practice guides. One honest post about something that did not work in a real practice, with the numbers and the diagnosis, would stand out in a SERP of identical advice articles and demonstrates the operational understanding the product claims.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish the launch or campaign that failed, with the numbers and a real diagnosis of why. It differentiates against a SERP of success stories and proves the diagnostic skill you sell far better than another win does.

### 14. Push each generated claim one notch bolder before you accept it  `71.8`
*useful · best practices · source 71*

Repeatedly through the edit, Devesh's instruction to himself was to be bolder. Where the draft said a brief that ignores what is currently ranking cannot tell you what angles would compete, he rewrote it as borderline useless, then added the mechanism: it cannot tell you what Google is already favoring for that keyword. Where the draft hedged on quality, he made it flat. He also tested and rejected a bolder version, considering a funny analogy about starting a business and abandoning it because it would be too belabored. So the discipline is not maximum aggression but one deliberate notch, kept only when the sharper wording still carries the reasoning. He remarks that nobody writes intros like the one he ended up with, which is the point.

> "is borderline useless"

**Evidence:** Devesh rewrote a hedged line into 'A brief that ignores what's currently ranking is borderline useless, because it can't tell you what Google is already favoring for that keyword.'

**How to do it**

1. Go through the generated draft and mark every hedged or neutral assessment.
2. Rewrite each one as the strongest version you actually believe, using plain words like 'useless', 'bad' or 'not very good'.
3. Immediately follow the bolder claim with the mechanism or evidence, so the boldness is earned rather than asserted.
4. Try a metaphor or analogy if one occurs to you, then cut it if it needs more than a sentence to land.
5. Read the bolder version back and revert any claim you could not defend to a customer.
6. Keep the bold claims aimed at practices and categories, not at named individuals.

**Pitfall:** Boldness without the mechanism attached reads as posturing. Devesh always paired the sharper wording with the reason, and he abandoned an analogy he judged would be too belabored.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles can be bolder about broken practice workflows, such as paper consent forms or manual rebooking, provided each claim is immediately followed by the reason it fails. This suits template and blog pages more than code-reference pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Push each hedged claim one notch bolder, then attach the mechanism that justifies it. Drop any analogy that needs more than a sentence.

### 15. Put your own product first in your own listicles  `87.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 87*

Grow and Convert flag it in passing while showing a Calendly listicle that ranked for a fan-out query: Calendly put itself first on its own list of the 13 best appointment scheduling apps, and Grow and Convert say they always recommend this. In the context of the study it matters because listicles are the single largest slice of the 78% of citations that are ordinary marketing content. If your listicle is the cited source and your product is buried at position nine, the model has less reason to surface you as the recommendation. The listicle earns the citation; the ordering decides whether the citation converts into a mention of your brand.

> "they put themselves first on the list"

**Evidence:** Calendly's 13 best appointment scheduling apps post ranked for the fan-out query about scheduling without back-and-forth emails, with Calendly first.

**How to do it**

1. Audit every listicle you own where your product is eligible for inclusion.
2. Move your own product to position one and justify it with a specific use case, not a superlative.
3. Give your entry the same structure as the others: pricing, features, who it suits, limits.
4. State honestly who the other tools suit better, so the piece survives an editorial read.
5. Add a pricing section for every entry using figures from each provider's own site.
6. Re-check citation results for those prompts after the pages are re-crawled.

**Pitfall:** Listing yourself last or omitting yourself out of modesty. The page still earns the citation but the model recommends whoever sits at the top.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau listicles about practice management or clinic software, Pabau belongs at position one with a real use case attached, and every entry needs the pricing table from the provider's own site.

**Apply anywhere:** Put your own product first in listicles you publish, with a specific use case attached, and give every entry the same pricing and features treatment.

### 16. Raise how-to posts above beginner level to earn customer-content fit  `159.18`
*useful · concrete actions · source 159*

Grow and Convert's verdict on the format is direct: most how-to blog posts suck, plain and simple, because they are way too beginner and lack customer-content fit. They treat fixing that as a positioning asset, not just a quality improvement. A how-to written at the level your actual buyer already operates at proves you know what you are talking about, which is exactly the proof criterion four demands. The implication for planning is that beginner-level how-tos, which are the ones with the highest search volume, are the ones least able to prove anything about your delivery. Publishing them satisfies a content calendar while doing nothing for the positioning the rest of the site is trying to establish.

> "way too beginner and don't have customer-content fit"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name how-tos as one of three content frameworks that prove delivery, but only once the beginner level and customer-content fit problems are fixed.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the level of knowledge your actual buyer already has before drafting any how-to.
2. Read the top three ranking posts for the target query and list the sections they all cover.
3. Cut those shared beginner sections from your draft or compress them to a single sentence.
4. Spend the reclaimed space on the step where your buyer actually gets stuck, using detail only a practitioner would know.
5. Include the specific tool settings, thresholds or numbers you use, since those are what prove competence.
6. Check the finished post against the positioning claim it is meant to prove and cut anything that does not support it.
7. Reject any how-to topic where writing at your buyer's level would leave nothing to say; that is a keyword you should not target.

**Pitfall:** Optimizing for volume pushes every how-to toward beginner queries, which produces content indistinguishable from competitors and proves nothing about delivery.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog how-tos should be written for someone already running a practice, with the specific workflow detail a manager would recognize, rather than repeating the definitional sections every competing article already has.

**Apply anywhere:** Write how-tos at the level your buyer already operates at. Cut the beginner sections every ranking competitor already covers and spend the space on where the practitioner actually gets stuck.

### 17. Rank detail above typo, plugin and on-page checklist advice  `99.10`
*useful · content insights · source 99*

Khanal closes by dismissing the standard how-to-write-a-blog-post advice as beginner tips for new bloggers: avoid typos, use Grammarly, use the Yoast WordPress plugin, proofread, use short sentences, do on-page SEO, optimize subheadings, optimize meta descriptions. This is a prioritization claim rather than a rejection of on-page work. The mechanism he implies is that on-page optimization changes how a page is read once it exists, while detail is what makes it worth linking to at all.

> "These things don't matter if your piece is not memorable"

**Evidence:** Khanal's own attribution chain from detail to links, shares, CTA clicks and brand recall, supported by the Specificity Strategy article's 124 backlinks from 27 referring domains with no promotion.

### 18. Re-check whether a market shift added a second audience to your keyword  `119.7`
*useful · content insights · source 119*

Grow and Convert use time tracking software as the example. Before COVID and widespread remote work, that SERP was specific to time clock software for businesses tracking employee hours. Today the results cover two solution types: time clock software for businesses and time tracking apps for individuals monitoring their own remote work. The intent did not flip, it widened. People still search for traditional time clocks, but a second audience arrived. Their conclusion is that the competitive answer is no longer a post about one solution type; it is a review guide explaining both categories, reviewing software in each, and selling your product to whichever fraction it fits. They also give the general version: new products, competitors and technologies appear, and a pre-2023 trends post that never mentions AI or ChatGPT is now incomplete.

> "societal changes can impact SERP results and search intent"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's time tracking software example: a pre-remote-work SERP covering only business time clocks now covers both business and individual remote-work tracking.

**How to do it**

1. For each money keyword, write down the audience you assumed when the page was written.
2. Search the keyword now and read the top ten for who each result is addressing.
3. Note any result serving a persona that was not in your original assumption.
4. If a second persona has appeared, do not rewrite for it, write for both.
5. Restructure the page as a guide that explains the categories first, then reviews options in each.
6. Place your own product in the category it genuinely fits, and say who the other category is for.
7. Repeat this check on any post covering technology trends, where new entrants date a page fastest.

**Pitfall:** Reading a widened SERP as a changed one and rewriting entirely for the new audience. You then lose the original searchers who still make up much of the volume.

**Apply at Pabau:** Aesthetic and healthcare software terms shifted the same way as telehealth and self-booking grew. David should check whether Pabau's category pages now face a SERP serving both practice owners and solo practitioners, and cover both.

**Apply anywhere:** Check whether your keyword picked up a second audience since you wrote the page. If it did, restructure into a guide covering both groups rather than choosing one.

### 19. Re-optimize by fixing the angle first, then the on-page basics  `116.9`
*useful · concrete actions · source 116*

Grow and Convert say the specifics of re-optimizing change too much per keyword to script, but they name the order they work in. First is angling and intent: the question they hold in mind through every edit is whether the positioning matches the searcher's intent, and the updated piece has to satisfy the intent of both the primary and the secondary keyword, since related keywords overlap but are not identical. Only then do they do on-page basics, checking title, headings, meta description and alt tags, and making sure the new keyword appears through the copy. They flag the obvious failure: keyword stuffing and forced phrasing. The sequencing matters because on-page changes on a page pointed at the wrong intent do nothing.

> "does the angling/positioning match the searcher's intent"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list angling and intent first and on-page basics second as their general re-optimization guideline.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the intent of the primary keyword and the intent of the secondary keyword as two separate sentences.
2. Identify what the two have in common and where they differ.
3. Re-angle the introduction and the section order so the piece satisfies both, leading with the shared intent.
4. Add or rewrite the sections that only the secondary keyword needs.
5. Only then update the title, H1, subheadings, meta description and image alt text to include the secondary keyword.
6. Read the body for natural mentions of the new keyword and add them where the sentence would carry it anyway.
7. Read the draft aloud to catch forced phrasing and stuffing before publishing.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Doing the on-page pass first. Adding the secondary keyword to the title and headings of a page still angled at the old intent produces a mismatch that reads as stuffing and does not rank.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau refresh briefs should state both intents at the top and require the angle change before any keyword insertion. Editors otherwise default to swapping terms in the H2s and leave the article pointed at the old reader.

**Apply anywhere:** State both intents at the top of every refresh brief and require the angle change before any keyword insertion, or editors will just swap terms in the headings.

### 20. Re-test the persona whenever you reuse a proven pitch on a new channel  `183.12`
*useful · best practices · source 183*

Grow and Convert had a pitch that closed in-person workshops with C-level executives at venture-backed startups. When they built the landing page for the online course, they used that same pitch. It failed, because the audience reading the page was their email list of individual marketers, not the executives the pitch was built for. The mechanism is that a proven pitch carries an implicit persona, and moving it to a channel with a different audience breaks it silently. Nothing about the copy looks wrong. They describe the error as a subtlety in the language, which is why it survived review. The lesson for anyone reusing sales-call language on a page is to check who the channel actually reaches before checking whether the copy is good.

> "we used the same pitch that we gave to sell our in-person workshops, to sell our online training"

**Evidence:** A pitch proven on C-level workshop sales produced under half the revenue goal when reused on a landing page read by individual marketers.

**How to do it**

1. Before reusing any pitch, write down the persona it was built for: role, seniority, and what they are accountable for.
2. Write down who the new channel actually reaches, using list data or analytics rather than assumption.
3. If the two personas differ on who owns the outcome, rewrite the promise rather than editing the wording.
4. Check the primary benefit sentence names a payoff the new persona personally receives.
5. Test the rewritten page against the original with a segment of the list before the full launch.
6. Ask three people from the new channel's audience to say what the page is offering and who it is for.
7. Treat any mismatch between their answer and your intent as a positioning problem, not a copy problem.

**Pitfall:** Assuming a pitch that converted in one channel is validated copy. It reads well and closes nobody, and the team blames the offer or the price instead of the persona swap underneath.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's sales-call language is built for practice owners. When that language is copied onto blog CTAs or template pages read by practice managers and practitioners, David should rewrite the promise for the reader who lands there rather than pasting the demo pitch.

**Apply anywhere:** A pitch carries the persona it was written for. Before reusing sales-call language on a landing page, confirm the channel reaches the same persona, and if it does not, rewrite the promise rather than the wording.

### 21. Read organic sharing by target customers as an audience-fit signal  `91.14`
*useful · content insights · source 91*

Grow and Convert say they routinely see the disruption stories they write for clients shared or discussed on social media, and they treat that as a diagnostic rather than a vanity metric. Their examples are a social post sharing Rainforest QA's story and a message in the Mirascope Community Slack from a user saying the disruption story was the reason he got interested in Mirascope. Their reading is specific: if you really nail the pain point and value props, this kind of organic sharing is common, and it indicates you are bringing in the right audience. The condition matters. It counts as a signal only when the people sharing are your target customer, because in that case the piece is probably converting too. Sharing by peers, marketers or competitors tells you the writing is good, not that the positioning is right.

> "this kind of organic social sharing is common with disruption stories"

**Evidence:** A Mirascope community member wrote in the Mirascope Community Slack that the disruption story was why he became interested in the product; a Rainforest QA story was shared publicly on social.

**How to do it**

1. Search your brand name and the article title on social platforms and in relevant communities weekly after publishing.
2. Log every unprompted share or mention with the sharer's job title and company.
3. Classify each as target customer, peer, or neither.
4. Treat a run of target-customer shares as confirmation the pain point landed, and keep the promotion running.
5. Treat peer-only sharing as a warning that the piece is admired but not aimed correctly.
6. Quote the strongest target-customer reactions back into sales material and social posts.
7. Cross-check the sharing signal against the conversion numbers before drawing conclusions.

**Pitfall:** Sharing by other marketers and industry peers feels like success and often means the piece is about the craft rather than the customer's problem. Check who is sharing before celebrating.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should watch aesthetic-practice owner communities and groups for unprompted mentions of its positioning content, and treat mentions by practice owners as the fit signal rather than mentions by other software vendors or marketers.

**Apply anywhere:** Watch for unprompted sharing of the piece and record who is sharing it. Shares by target customers confirm the pain point landed; shares only by industry peers mean the piece is well written but aimed at the wrong reader.

### 22. Rearrange a listicle by use case when the SERP lists tools flat  `100.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 100*

Grow and Convert show that a generic intent can still carry a nugget. For their client Leadfeeder the keyword clearly wanted lists of tools, so trying to be specific by deep-diving two tools would have missed the intent. Instead they published 41 lead generation tools but arranged them by category, on the stated logic that the tools you need depend on your specific goal. That arrangement was the entire nugget, and they verified it by checking the other 10 results in the SERP, where nobody had organized the list that way. The post ranks #2 for the term and drives leads because Leadfeeder is itself a lead generation tool. They also note it can create a small aha for a reader used to treating all tools in a category as the same.

> "arranged by categories"

**Evidence:** The Leadfeeder post listing 41 lead generation tools by category ranked #2 for the target term at the time of writing and drove leads.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm the intent really is a flat list before changing format, by reading the top 10.
2. Choose an organizing axis the reader already thinks in, such as goal, use case, practice size or budget.
3. Check all 10 results and confirm none uses that axis.
4. State the organizing logic in the introduction so the reader knows why the list is grouped.
5. Group the entries and give each group a short paragraph explaining who it is for.
6. Keep the entry count competitive with the SERP so you still satisfy the list intent.
7. Place your own product inside the category it genuinely belongs to, not at the top of every group.

**Pitfall:** Grouping by an axis nobody shops on makes the list harder to scan and loses to a plain alphabetical competitor. The signal is that you struggle to write a one-line description of each group.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's listicles of clinic software, aesthetic devices or booking tools should be grouped by the practice's goal or size rather than presented flat. David should check the live SERP first to confirm no competitor already groups them the same way.

**Apply anywhere:** On a list-intent keyword, keep the list but group it by an axis the reader shops on, and confirm no page-one result already does. State the grouping logic in the intro.

### 23. Recognize the AI slop pattern: same structure, same arguments, reworded  `88.4`
*useful · content insights · source 88*

The author opens by naming why marketers view AI negatively: identical LinkedIn posts with the same structure and the same lazy arguments, comments that add nothing, and blog posts that say what every other blog says with slight rewording. His diagnosis is not that the tool is bad but that people use it to think less and avoid hard work. This is a useful editorial checklist because it names the specific detectable signatures rather than a vague quality feeling. The test he implies: if the piece contains no argument you had to have expertise to make, it is slop regardless of how well written it is. The base's related finding is that pogo-sticking, not AI detection, is what kills this content.

> "using it to try to think less"

**How to do it**

1. Read the draft and try to name the one argument in it that a competitor could not have written; if you cannot, kill or rewrite it.
2. Check the structure against three competing articles on the same query; identical H2 sequences are the strongest slop signal.
3. Search a distinctive sentence from the draft; if near-identical phrasing appears on other sites, rewrite that section.
4. Require at least one proprietary input per piece: your own data, a customer quote, a screenshot of your product doing the thing.
5. Cut any paragraph that restates the previous one in different words.
6. Confirm the piece answers the query in the first two sentences, since a late answer causes pogo-sticking.
7. Have a subject expert, not the writer, sign off on whether the point of view is real.

**Pitfall:** Writers mistake polish for originality. A well-edited article with no proprietary argument still reads as generic to a searcher who has already read three of them, and they bounce back to the SERP.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau article already needs an originality nugget under the two-bar rule. Make the proprietary input explicit on the brief: named practice data, a Pabau product screenshot with a caption explaining how the feature helps, or a stat from Pabau's own usage.

**Apply anywhere:** Require one proprietary input in every article: your own data, a customer quote or a product screenshot. Polished prose with no original argument is still slop and searchers bounce off it.
