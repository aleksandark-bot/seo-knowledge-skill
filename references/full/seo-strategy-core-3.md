### 1. Gate content investment on two early-stage tests before scaling  `160.1`
*core · best practices · source 160*

Grow and Convert define an early stage company by two markers: it has not proven it can market and sell through at least one cold channel, and its positioning and messaging are still being worked out. They say they will not take clients that fit that criteria going forward, because their agency's value proposition is tied to driving leads and revenue, and traffic cannot fix either gap. The point is not that early companies should skip content. It is that the goals attached to content must change when either marker is unmet. Run the two checks before committing budget to a content program with revenue targets, and if either fails, reframe the program as long-term asset building rather than a lead channel.

> "They haven't proven that they can market and sell through at least one cold channel"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert reviewed every client engagement that was challenging or did not lead to a successful outcome and found the common theme was always early stage companies.

**How to do it**

1. Ask whether the business has closed revenue through at least one cold channel: inbound content, paid, or outbound email and phone.
2. Ask whether positioning and messaging are settled, meaning one written pitch the whole team agrees closes strangers.
3. If both pass, set lead and revenue targets for content and hire or scale accordingly.
4. If either fails, set the content goal to audience and asset building only, with no conversion target for the first phase.
5. Write the chosen goal into the brief so writers and stakeholders judge output against it.
6. Re-run both checks quarterly and switch the program to revenue targets only when both pass.

**Pitfall:** Attaching lead targets to content while positioning is unsettled. The signal is endless debate over which benefit to feature in each article, and a conclusion that 'content marketing doesn't work for us' when the real blocker is product or messaging.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau clears both tests, so the blog can carry lead targets. But when David briefs a page for a brand-new module or a new market segment, treat that segment as early stage: check there is a proven cold-channel sale into it before setting conversion goals on those pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Before setting lead targets on a content program, confirm the business has already sold through a cold channel and has a settled pitch. If either is missing, run content as long-term asset building and judge it on traffic and learning, not conversions.

### 2. Google ranking boils down to relevance plus authority  `41.1`
*core · general insights · source 41*

Edward frames Google's ranking system as fundamentally simple despite what 'gurus' claim, boiling down to two forces: relevance and authority. Relevance comes from having your target keyword language present in specific on-page locations - the URL slug, page title, H1, and the beginning of the first sentence. Authority comes primarily from backlinks pointing at your ranking pages and referral traffic, possibly reinforced by brand mentions, and additionally from your own pages ranking well and not seeing visitors immediately click back to the search results after landing. He concedes there is real complexity in how Google detects spam and manipulation, but insists the core decision of what ranks where is still just this relevance-plus-authority calculation.

> "It prioritizes relevance, so just having the language that you are targeting"

**Evidence:** Edward's simplified ranking model: relevance signals come from having the target keyword/language in the URL slug, page title, H1, and the beginning of the first sentence; authority comes from backlinks on ranking pages, referral traffic, possibly brand mentions, and from a site's own pages ranking well and not seeing users immediately click back to search results.

**Apply at Pabau:** Use this exact four-location relevance checklist, URL slug, page title, H1, first sentence, as the baseline audit for every page Pabau wants to rank, and remember that authority is earned by both external backlinks/referral traffic and by good on-site user behavior, so technical and UX quality on ranking pages is itself part of the authority signal, not a separate concern.

**Apply anywhere:** Use this exact four-location relevance checklist, URL slug, page title, H1, first sentence, as the baseline audit for every page you want to rank, and remember that authority is earned by both external backlinks/referral traffic and by good on-site user behavior, so technical and UX quality on ranking pages is itself part of the authority signal, not a separate concern.

### 3. Homepage authority doesn't excuse cramming in many keywords  `41.2`
*core · content insights · source 41*

Edward diagnoses why so many marketers make this mistake: the flawed logic is that because the homepage collects the most backlinks and therefore has the most authority, it should be the place to cram every important keyword. This reflects a broader misconception he says is common among SEOs and marketers, that of thinking about SEO mostly in terms of backlinks and authority while badly underweighting relevance, when in reality relevance matters more than most SEOs realize, especially for competitive keywords being targeted by many other sites. He adds that the practitioners making this mistake are typically also going after obvious, high-competition keywords in the first place, since most people default to the obvious keyword choices, which compounds the problem: a single homepage stuffed with many keywords is being asked to be maximally relevant to several competitive terms at once, which relevance-based ranking doesn't reward.

> "the homepage gets all of the backlinks, and so therefore"

**Evidence:** Edward's diagnosis of the root cause: marketers conflate a homepage's high authority, from collecting the most backlinks, with a license to target many keywords on it, while underweighting that Google's relevance signal requires a page to be tightly focused on one keyword or topic, not spread across several, especially when those keywords are competitive.

**Apply at Pabau:** When auditing Pabau's homepage or any hub page, check whether it's carrying more than one distinct keyword target in its H1/H2s purely because it has strong backlink authority - authority does not substitute for topical relevance, so a page trying to rank for several competitive terms at once will likely underperform dedicated single-keyword pages for each term.

**Apply anywhere:** When auditing your homepage or any hub page, check whether it's carrying more than one distinct keyword target in its H1/H2s purely because it has strong backlink authority - authority does not substitute for topical relevance, so a page trying to rank for several competitive terms at once will likely underperform dedicated single-keyword pages for each term.

### 4. Interrogate a traffic case study with seven follow-up questions  `178.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 178*

Grow and Convert argue that a percentage in a case study is not evidence and give the exact follow-ups that turn it into evidence. For any claim like grew organic traffic 200%, ask how many articles it took, whether one article produced most of it or the gain spread across the set, which keywords ranked, how and why those keywords were chosen, how long the pages took to rank, what the client's domain authority was at the start and at the end, whether link building was part of it, and what did not work. The last question is the one most agencies cannot answer, and the domain-authority question exposes the case study that only works for an already-strong site. They frame transparency about method as the proxy for whether the results are replicable on your site.

> "Did one article produce most of that or was it spread out?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish six long-form case studies themselves specifically because they could not find this level of detail when they were the buyer.

**How to do it**

1. For each case study, write the headline number down and treat it as unverified.
2. Ask how many articles produced the result and over what period.
3. Ask whether one page carried the gain or it spread across the set; a single outlier is not a process.
4. Ask which keywords ranked, and how and why the agency selected them.
5. Ask how long each page took to reach its position.
6. Ask for the client's domain authority at the start and at the end, and whether link building ran alongside.
7. Ask what did not work in the engagement, and treat a blank answer as a red flag.
8. Compare the starting domain authority to yours; discount the case study if the gap is large.

**Pitfall:** Accepting a case study whose client started with far more authority than you. The tactics may be real and still unreproducible on a site with no link profile.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau evaluates an SEO or content vendor, David should require the starting domain authority and the article count behind any headline number, since pabau.com competes against far stronger aesthetics-industry domains.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn every case-study percentage into seven questions: article count, concentration, keywords, keyword selection logic, time to rank, starting and ending authority plus link building, and what failed.

### 5. Interrogate an SEO case study down to pages, keywords and conversion rate  `163.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 163*

Grow and Convert's final vetting step is to demand proof of business results, then refuse to accept it at surface level. A headline like 'we grew traffic from 0 to 100,000 pageviews' tells you nothing about whether the method transfers. They list the follow-up questions to ask about any case study: which pages got that traffic, whether it came from a couple of pages or many, whether unique content was created, who created it, which keywords the traffic came from, why those keywords are useful for that business, and whether leads or conversions were measured and at what rate. The answers separate a repeatable process from one lucky viral page or an inherited authority domain. If a candidate cannot answer at that resolution about their own work, they did not run the strategy.

> "Don't be satisfied with surface level case studies"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish seven detailed case studies including Leadfeeder signups over 200 per month and Circuit going from 920 to 14,577 sessions in six months, and invite this level of questioning of their own work.

**How to do it**

1. Ask for in-depth case studies with data, not logos or testimonials.
2. For each one, ask which specific pages produced the traffic and pull them up during the call.
3. Ask whether the result came from a handful of pages or a wide spread, since a single viral hit is not a process.
4. Ask whether unique content was created for it and who wrote it, in-house or freelance.
5. Ask which keywords drove the traffic and why those keywords mattered commercially for that client.
6. Ask whether leads or conversions were measured and what the conversion rate was.
7. Check the named pages still rank today, which tests whether the result held.
8. Discount any candidate who answers in ranges and adjectives rather than page URLs and numbers.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Accepting an aggregate traffic chart. It hides that the growth came from one informational page with no commercial value, or from a domain that already had authority.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David evaluates an SEO vendor for Pabau, ask for the URLs and check them in Ahrefs during the call. A healthcare or SaaS case study that cannot name pages and conversion rates should not shortlist.

**Apply anywhere:** When evaluating an SEO vendor, ask for the actual URLs and check them in Ahrefs during the call. A case study that cannot name pages and conversion rates should not shortlist.

### 6. Judge a content strategy by whether traffic compounds month to month  `136.15`
*core · general insights · source 136*

The test that ended Grow and Convert's story-first strategy is a single diagnostic. With story content promoted in communities, every month they were effectively starting from 0 traffic, because there was no base of organic traffic to build on. Leadfeeder's CEO asked the two questions that exposed it: will this grow traffic and leads long term, and why are we writing stories about other companies on our own blog. The strategy was working in absolute terms, better than what the client had before, and it still failed the compounding test. Their own reflection on how they got there is honest: they had avoided SEO because they thought most SEO content was bad and they did not want to present the agency as something it was not. Preference, not evidence, had set the strategy.

> "we were effectively starting from 0 traffic"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert grew Leadfeeder to 3,000+ pageviews in month one with story content and $0 paid spend, then abandoned the approach because organic baseline traffic never accumulated.

**Pitfall:** A channel that produces good absolute numbers can still have a flat baseline. The signal is that this month's traffic does not depend on last month's publishing, which means the program can never stop spending.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should chart pabau.com's organic sessions from posts published more than six months ago as a separate line. If that line is flat while total sessions rise, the program is renting traffic and the mix needs more search-led pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Chart traffic from posts older than six months as its own line. If it is flat while total traffic moves with your publishing rate, you are renting attention rather than building a base, and the strategy will not survive a pause.

### 7. Judge posts on conversion rate, not sessions, when auditing the blog  `110.3`
*core · best practices · source 110*

Grow and Convert show a client landing-pages report where the top post drew 21,000 sessions in three months and produced seven account creations from 55 total, a 12 percent share. The post they produced drew 1,900 sessions, nine percent of that traffic, and produced more last-click signups than any other post on the site. Its conversion rate was 0.52 percent against a blog-wide 0.08 percent. Their reading is that a bottom-funnel piece delivers the same conversions on a fraction of the traffic because its rate is so much higher. They also note the shape of the report matters as much as the winners: the vast majority of posts on that blog generate almost no business at all, which they call status quo for SaaS, and most companies are worse off because they have the dead posts without any of the converting ones.

> "The conversion rate was a whopping 0.52%"

**Evidence:** Client blog, three months: top post 21,000 sessions and seven of 55 account creations; Grow and Convert's post 1,900 sessions at 0.52 percent conversion against a 0.08 percent blog average.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the landing pages report for the blog over a three-month window with sessions and conversions.
2. Add a conversion rate column and sort by rate, not by sessions.
3. Compute the blog-wide conversion rate as the baseline to compare each post against.
4. Mark every post with zero conversions in the window as dead and count what share of the blog that is.
5. Identify the posts beating the blog baseline by more than 3x and read what topic type they share.
6. Commission the next quarter's briefs against that topic type rather than against traffic potential.
7. Stop refreshing high-traffic zero-conversion posts until the converting topic list is exhausted.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Ranking posts by traffic makes the best performer look useless. Grow and Convert's post pulled nine percent of the top post's traffic and still led the blog on signups, so a traffic-sorted report would have had it near the bottom.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should sort pabau.com's blog report by conversion rate to demo requests, not sessions, and compute the site-wide baseline first. Expect the /templates/ pages and comparison articles to clear the baseline and the general explainers to sit near zero.

**Apply anywhere:** Sort your blog's landing-pages report by conversion rate and compare each post to the blog-wide baseline. Count the posts with zero conversions, find the topic type shared by posts beating the baseline threefold, and brief against that type next quarter.

### 8. Keep conversions as the content goal even for consumer brands  `137.7`
*core · best practices · source 137*

Grow and Convert state that when they took on Cognitive FX, a business-to-consumer clinic, their goal stayed conversions from content. They did not switch to a vanity metric like shares, views or impressions just because it was consumer facing. Their whole method, Pain Point SEO, rests on prioritizing conversion intent of a keyword over search volume, and they kept that unchanged for B2C. The reasoning is that B2C is not automatically a brand-awareness game; that assumption only holds for low-readability commodity products. Where readability is high, the same conversion-first content approach works, and they proved it by growing consultation requests from zero to over 200 a month with content driving over 50% of consultations.

> "some vanity metric like shares, views,"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX content grew consultation requests to over 200 a month, with agency content responsible for more than 50% of overall consultations.

**How to do it**

1. Set the content goal as one revenue-linked conversion before any keyword work, even for consumer audiences.
2. Refuse briefs whose success metric is shares, impressions or pageviews.
3. Score every candidate keyword by conversion intent first and search volume second.
4. Report content performance monthly as conversions and as a share of total site conversions.
5. Only accept a low-conversion piece into the plan if it has a named secondary job, such as social distribution or brand story.
6. Re-run the intent scoring quarterly using actual conversion data per post rather than the original estimate.

**Pitfall:** Consumer-facing teams quietly downgrade to awareness metrics because traffic is easier to move, then cannot show revenue when budget is reviewed. The signal is a monthly report led by pageviews.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog reporting should lead with demo requests attributed to content and content's share of total demo requests, not sessions, even for consumer-facing aesthetic topics.

**Apply anywhere:** Hold content to a revenue-linked conversion goal even for consumer audiences, and report conversions and share of total site conversions rather than pageviews.

### 9. Keep programmatic SEO running, but stop expecting it to drive traffic  `28.8`
*core · best practices · source 28*

When AI Overviews began eating their programmatic sign-language dictionary pages, the team's first instinct, expanding the same programmatic pages to be more detailed and informative, didn't help, since AI Overviews still just take the content and don't give a lot back regardless of page depth. Rather than shutting the programmatic layer down, they kept it running, since it retains value as a resource and as underlying infrastructure, while redirecting new investment into traditional, editorially-driven content covering top, middle, and bottom of the funnel — which is what actually produced a recovery in both traffic quality and engagement, including crossover visibility into LLM recommendations, not just Google Search.

> "AI Overviews still just takes your content and doesn't give"

**How to do it**

1. Audit which of your programmatic or templated pages are showing the great-decoupling signature in GSC, meaning steady or rising impressions with falling clicks, as a sign AI Overviews are extracting their answers directly.
2. Do not respond by simply expanding those same programmatic pages with more depth or detail as the primary fix; test this on a small sample first if attempted, since it did not work in this case.
3. Keep the existing programmatic pages live rather than deleting them, since they retain value as a resource or dataset even without driving new organic clicks.
4. Redirect new content-production budget toward traditional, editorially-planned articles that explicitly map to top-, middle-, and bottom-of-funnel intents, rather than more programmatic variants.
5. Track recovery not just in Google Search clicks but also in LLM or AI-answer recommendation appearances, since both channels responded to the same shift in this case.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Assuming that making programmatic or thin pages 'more detailed and informative' will defend them against AI Overview extraction — the team tried this directly and found AI Overviews still take the content and don't give traffic back, regardless of depth added.

### 10. Log 3-4 SEO test hypotheses every month  `29.3`
*core · best practices · source 29*

Hank runs SEO as a standing monthly testing practice rather than a one-off project: he keeps 3-4 hypotheses queued every month, some new and some carried over, covering small isolated variables like title-tag phrasing, first-paragraph clarity, adding 3-4 bullets near the top of a page, or moving Q&A content higher up. He built this discipline originally at Raptive with Nick Swan's SEO Testing tool, running controlled tests across publisher pages rather than guessing. His argument against relying on generic 'best practice' lists is that by the time a rule reaches you secondhand it has lost the context that made it true - a schema rollout that averaged a 20% lift, for instance, actually hid subsegments getting a 60% lift and others getting nothing, depending on their existing authority and context. The discipline isn't about any single test proving a universal law; it's about building an evidence base specific to your own site and forcing yourself to validate assumptions instead of assuming them.

> "every month there are three or four hypotheses we have"

**How to do it**

1. Keep a running monthly log of 3-4 SEO hypotheses for the site at all times.
2. Source hypotheses from your own specific hunches ('what happens if I change X') rather than solely from published best-practice lists.
3. Isolate one variable per test - e.g., title-tag phrasing, first-paragraph clarity, adding 3-4 bullets near the top, or moving Q&A content higher on the page.
4. Run the variant against a control using a dedicated SEO split-testing tool such as SEO Testing.
5. Let the test run long enough to reach a statistically stable read for that page's traffic level before concluding. (inferred)
6. Record the result even when inconclusive, to build a site-specific evidence base rather than relying on secondhand rules.
7. Before adopting any external 'best practice,' test it yourself on your own site first instead of deploying it site-wide on faith.
8. When a test shows an average lift, check for subsegment variance before generalizing the result to the whole site. (inferred)

**Tools:** SEO Testing (Nick Swan)

**Pitfall:** Treating a single test's average result as a universal law (e.g., 'schema gives a 20% lift') hides the fact that the average can mask huge subsegment variance - and adopting best-practice lists secondhand means inheriting conclusions stripped of the context and authority signals that made them true in the first place.

### 11. Make the bundle the differentiator when no single part is unique  `169.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 169*

None of Grow and Convert's three claims is unique alone. Plenty of agencies write, some promote, some do links. Hyam's uniqueness argument is that they solve all three pain points as a single service provider, and that this combination cannot be bought elsewhere. He spells out the three: accountability for leads generated rather than deliverables or traffic; the ability to cover advanced topics, backed by capping output at three posts a month and by named specialist pieces; and a three-step promotion process of community content promotion, paid Facebook promotion paid from the agency's own budget, and targeted link building for SEO. He closes it with a testable claim, that it is virtually impossible to find a single employee or agency doing all of this, and that nobody would do all of it for $10,000 a month. That is how a commoditized category gets escaped without inventing a new capability.

> "the fact that we solve the above pain points as a single service provider"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert bundles lead accountability, advanced-topic writing capped at 3 posts/month and a 3-step promotion process, and sustains $10,000/month with rare price pushback.

**How to do it**

1. List every capability a buyer needs to get the outcome, not just the ones you currently sell.
2. Check each capability against the market and accept that most are individually common.
3. Find the combination of three or more that no competitor and no single hire delivers together.
4. Attach a concrete proof to each part of the bundle, such as a named specialist article or a budget you fund yourself.
5. State the bundle claim as a falsifiable sentence a prospect could try to disprove.
6. Price the bundle against the cost of hiring for each part separately.
7. Add capabilities to the bundle over time and raise price with them, as Grow and Convert added link building and Facebook ads on the way from $6k to $10k.
8. Rewrite the sales page so the bundle, not any single part, is the headline.

**Tools:** Facebook Ads

**Pitfall:** Claiming uniqueness on one component that a prospect can immediately name three competitors for. The bundle only holds if each part is genuinely delivered, so a weak leg makes the whole claim look like marketing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's differentiation pages should lead with the combination that competitors split across separate tools, booking, clinical notes, payments and marketing in one system, and name the cost of buying each separately.

**Apply anywhere:** When every capability you offer exists elsewhere, make the combination the differentiator. Find the set of three or more nobody delivers together, prove each part concretely, and price the bundle against hiring for each piece separately.

### 12. Match domain type (EMD vs. aged) to niche competitiveness  `15.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 15*

James's rule for starting a new site is to first classify it by business model (local lead-gen vs. affiliate, etc.) and by how competitive the specific target term is, not the industry as a whole. A narrow, specific long-tail sub-niche (his example: help with repayments on debt tied to one named catalog brand) can support a fresh exact/partial-match domain microsite, because competition at that granular level is thin. But a broad, contested head term in the same industry (his example: 'insolvency practitioners,' held by DR 70+ incumbents) requires buying an aged or expired domain that already carries topical power and trust, and even then he budgets four to five months before expecting real ranking traction.

> "It's probably going to take four or five months"

**How to do it**

1. Before starting a new site or content vertical, classify it by business model and by how competitive the specific target term actually is.
2. For a narrow, specific long-tail sub-niche with thin competition, use a fresh exact-match or partial-match domain/microsite targeting that specific phrase.
3. For a broad, contested head term already held by high-authority incumbents (DR 70+ in James's example), do not launch fresh - buy or use an aged/expired domain that already carries topical power and trust instead.
4. If launching fresh into a competitive space anyway, budget four to five months minimum before expecting meaningful ranking traction while topical authority builds.
5. Make this decision per-project rather than as a blanket industry-wide policy, since the same industry can support both narrow fresh-domain plays and broad aged-domain plays depending on term specificity.

**Pitfall:** Launching a fresh domain against an entrenched, highly competitive head term wastes months, because a new domain has no topical authority to compete at that scale - fresh domains only work when narrowed to a specific enough long-tail sub-niche that established competitors haven't targeted.

### 13. Model SEO as fixed cost up front and value arriving after month five  `129.5`
*core · content insights · source 129*

Grow and Convert say the misunderstanding they see most from executives and experienced marketers is treating SEO value as if it arrived evenly with spend. Their shape: costs are largely fixed and predictable, because you are paying people to do keyword research, pick keywords, build pages and build links at a steady monthly rhythm. Value starts near zero because a published page cannot convert until Google ranks it. Rankings typically begin arriving between months five and ten, traffic and conversions tick up subtly, and around the one-year mark compounding rankings produce more value per month than the monthly spend. From there they say recouping the earlier deficit does not take long, and ROI grows roughly linearly until high-buying-intent keyword opportunities in the space are exhausted, at which point it levels off.

> "There's a period of initial investment where the value generated"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report, across dozens of clients, rankings arriving between months 5 and 10 and value exceeding monthly spend around the one-year mark, with the deficit period covering roughly the first 6 to 10 months.

**How to do it**

1. Present SEO to stakeholders as two curves, not one number: a flat monthly cost line and a value curve starting near zero.
2. Mark months five to ten on the chart as when first rankings and subtle traffic gains should appear.
3. Mark the one-year area as when monthly value is expected to exceed monthly spend.
4. State the levelling-off point explicitly: ROI flattens when high-intent keyword opportunities run out.
5. Attach the two preconditions to the chart — real search volume in the space, and a strategy aimed at buying-intent keywords rather than traffic.
6. Agree in advance which month the first review happens, so a month-three report is not read as failure.
7. Re-plot the curves with real data each quarter instead of defending the original projection.

**Tools:** Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Reviewing the program during the deficit window and cancelling it. Grow and Convert put the deficit at roughly the first six to ten months, which is exactly when an impatient stakeholder asks for the ROI number.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau launches a new page family, set the review calendar to match this curve: a crawl and index check at month one, first ranking movement at months five to ten, and the value-versus-cost verdict no earlier than month twelve.

**Apply anywhere:** Explain SEO with two lines: a flat monthly cost and a value curve that starts near zero. Expect first rankings between months five and ten, monthly value to exceed monthly spend around the one-year mark, and ROI to flatten once high-intent keywords are exhausted. Fix the review dates to that curve before you start.

### 14. Move the 50-plus high-intent keywords your product pages cannot hold to the blog  `108.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 108*

Grow and Convert size the gap numerically. A typical B2B SaaS marketing site has fewer than ten feature and solutions pages, excluding all-in-one platforms. Of those, only two to five carry enough content to be reshaped into something that could rank for a competitive term. So the core site can realistically hold a handful of bottom-of-funnel category keywords. Meanwhile they say many of their SaaS clients have 50-plus keywords whose searchers are potential buyers. If the only vehicle for high-intent terms is the product site, dozens of opportunities go unworked. Their conclusion is that blog content is where the remaining high-intent keywords get targeted, and that most companies never do this because they reserve the blog for top-of-funnel traffic plays.

> "it's less than ten"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: fewer than ten feature and solutions pages typical, only 2-5 morphable, against 50-plus buying-intent keywords for many of their SaaS clients.

**How to do it**

1. Count your feature and solutions pages and mark the two to five with enough depth to compete.
2. Assign one realistic category keyword to each of those pages and stop there.
3. Build the full bottom-of-funnel keyword list: category variants, competitor comparisons and jobs-to-be-done terms.
4. Subtract the handful already assigned to product pages.
5. Confirm the remainder is in the dozens; if it is under 15, the research was not exhaustive enough.
6. Assign every remaining term to its own blog post rather than adding sections to existing pages.
7. Sequence publication by buying intent, not by search volume.
8. Track conversions per post by target keyword so the blog's high-intent tier can be defended separately from traffic posts.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Companies run the keyword research, see that the product site cannot house the list, and quietly drop the surplus terms instead of commissioning posts for them. The symptom is a keyword sheet with 50 rows and a content calendar with six.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's high-intent list for aesthetics practices runs well past what /features/ pages can hold. Terms like software for injectable clinics, competitor alternatives and consent-form workflows each need their own blog article, not a paragraph on a solutions page.

**Apply anywhere:** Your product site can only hold a handful of bottom-funnel keywords. Count them, then move every remaining buying-intent term to its own dedicated article instead of dropping it from the plan.

### 15. Never make one to three category head terms your whole SEO program  `94.2`
*core · best practices · source 94*

Grow and Convert name this as the biggest SEO mistake they see: a company puts all its budget into ranking for one to three category-definition keywords, such as a hosting company chasing 'web hosting' or a CRM chasing 'CRM software'. The companies already ranking for those terms have very strong domains, are known brands, have spent tens of thousands of dollars mostly on links to the ranking page, and have been working on it for years. The terms are not bad; the sequencing is. When a head-term-only program takes years to move, the company concludes SEO does not work for them and stops, which is the real cost. Their rule is to go long-tail first and move up the funnel as domain authority builds.

> "you'll get disheartened and wrongly conclude "SEO isn't for us"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report the incumbents on these terms have typically spent tens of thousands of dollars and several years defending them, mostly through links to the ranking page.

**How to do it**

1. Count how many target keywords your current program has; if it is under five and they are all category nouns, stop and rebuild the list.
2. Keep the head terms on the roadmap, but move them to a phase-two section of the sheet with an explicit condition for starting them.
3. Fill phase one with longer, qualified buying-intent terms tied to your differentiators.
4. Set the phase-two trigger in writing, for example once ten qualified pages rank in the top three.
5. Report on phase-one leads and revenue, not head-term position, so the program is judged on results it can actually produce.
6. Re-check the head-term SERP each quarter to see whether the incumbents' links and tailored pages still make it unwinnable.

**Pitfall:** Executives judge the program by the one keyword they personally type. If you do not agree the phase-two trigger and the reporting metric up front, phase one gets cancelled before it compounds.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not measure its blog program by position on 'practice management software'. Set the reporting metric as demos from qualified long-tail pages and keep the head term as a later phase.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not let one to three category head terms be your entire SEO program. Keep them on the roadmap with a written trigger for when to start, and judge phase one on leads from qualified long-tail pages instead.

### 16. Only chase cannibalization fixes on money or strategic keywords  `49.7`
*core · best practices · source 49*

It's easy to over-invest time hunting down and fixing every instance of keyword cannibalization across a large site, so the recommended filter is to only prioritize fixing it for keywords that are core to acquisition (customers, users, leads) or core to strategy where you aren't ranking consistently well — typically bottom-of-funnel keywords — and even then, to first confirm cannibalization is actually the cause of poor performance rather than assuming it. For everything else, the time cost of fixing cannibalization isn't justified if the keyword was never going to bring in revenue.

> "it's really easy to go overboard on fixing keyword cannibalization"

**How to do it**

1. Before starting any cannibalization audit, define your list of money or strategic keywords (bottom-of-funnel, core acquisition terms) as the only in-scope targets.
2. When you find a cannibalization case involving an out-of-scope keyword (informational, low commercial value), deprioritize or skip fixing it rather than spending time on it.
3. For in-scope keywords showing poor or inconsistent rankings, first verify cannibalization is actually present via the GSC and SERP-overlap checks before assuming it's the root cause.
4. Budget cannibalization-fixing time proportionally to keyword commercial value, not to how many cannibalization instances exist sitewide.

**Pitfall:** Spending significant SEO time fixing cannibalization on keywords that were never going to drive revenue or strategic value is a common time sink — be selective rather than treating every detected instance as equally worth resolving.

### 17. Only invest in a channel that passes three simultaneous filters  `43.2`
*core · best practices · source 43*

Rand's rule for picking marketing channels/tactics, used identically for a video game studio and a B2B SaaS tool, is to only invest in a channel that satisfies three tests at once. First, it must be where your specific audience already pays attention (LinkedIn/podcasts/Reddit for Alertmouse's B2B audience vs. Steam/Twitch/YouTube for a video game). Second, it must be something you or your team have genuine passion to sustain, since he explicitly ruled out TikTok for Alertmouse despite plausible audience fit, purely because it wasn't his 'vibe.' Third, the channel must let you provide unique value nobody else in that space already provides, since he singles out the SEO/marketing content space as an example so crowded with identical educational content that most participants fail to stand out.

> "the thing that always holds true for me"

**How to do it**

1. List every channel/platform where your target buyer persona actually spends attention, using audience-research data rather than assumption (e.g., a tool like SparkToro).
2. Cross off any channel from that list where your team has no genuine personal interest or passion to sustain content long-term, even if the audience is technically there.
3. For each remaining channel, define the specific unique angle or value you can offer that competitors in your space are not already doing (tone, format, perspective, depth).
4. Reject any channel where you can't articulate that unique angle, even if it passes the first two tests, since a crowded field of identical content was Rand's explicit warning sign.
5. Commit resources only to channels that pass all three filters simultaneously, rather than spreading effort across every channel where the audience merely exists (inferred).
6. Revisit the three-filter list periodically (e.g., quarterly) as team interests, bandwidth, and competitive crowding in each channel change (inferred).

**Tools:** SparkToro

**Pitfall:** Choosing a channel just because the audience is provably there, while ignoring personal/team passion and competitive crowding — Rand turned down Discord for Snack Bar Studio and TikTok for Alertmouse despite audience fit, because the other two filters failed.

### 18. Only publish at high velocity where branded search is already rising  `56.16`
*core · best practices · source 56*

Pressed on when high-velocity publishing is safe, Cody gives one gate: he would only do it on a site that is getting branded search and where branded search is increasing month over month. Everything else follows from that. He has done it on exact-match keyword domains too - buy an XYZ-generator.com, build the tool on the homepage, get it ranking for itself with a handful of DA50 backlinks, then build content on the back of that - but the majority of the time it's a branded site that looks like a real company. He is emphatic about the risk boundary: 'if it is a critical part of your business, if SEO drives 80% of your revenue, do not do this.' He has been running these sites since October 2022, has had a ridiculous number of them clapped, and has intentionally tried to get them clapped to find the threshold. A new brand can qualify, but only if you know you are going to invest in the brand - his example is a Chrome extension where he knows Google Ads and Facebook remarketing will drive branded search over time.

> "only do this on a site that you're getting branded search"

**How to do it**

1. Check branded search volume in Search Console and confirm it is rising month over month before considering high-velocity publishing.
2. If SEO is a critical revenue channel for the business, stop here - he says explicitly not to do this.
3. Confirm the site reads as a real company: about page, privacy policy, real social presence, real people.
4. Confirm the content you plan to publish is directly or tangentially related to the product, not general traffic bait.
5. If branded search isn't there yet, put the demand-generation in place first (paid remarketing to site visitors, product usage) so branded search will follow.
6. Run the experiment on a portfolio site you can afford to lose before touching a revenue-critical property.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** He repeats the caveat several times unprompted - he is describing what he is comfortable doing with sites he can afford to lose, having had many penalised deliberately, and does not want listeners nuking a business on it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau is exactly the case he says not to do this on: organic is a core acquisition channel, so publishing volume should stay conservative and every page should earn its place - the branded-search gate is the useful part to keep.

**Apply anywhere:** If organic is a core acquisition channel, this is exactly the case he says not to try: publishing volume should stay conservative and every page should earn its place. The branded-search gate is the part worth keeping.

### 19. Only test a new tactic when the current one is measurably broken  `176.1`
*core · best practices · source 176*

Grow and Convert's core rule against shiny object syndrome is a gate on new tests: you only need to test something new if there is something wrong with the current method. Marketers get an idea, test it, and before giving it enough time to work they hear of another idea and switch. The result is that leads, trials and demos never arrive while the marketer feels busy, because things are happening yet nothing is working. The fix is not more ideas but a written condition under which the current channel counts as failing. Until that condition trips, new tactics go on a backlog and the team keeps executing the process that is producing results now. They note that almost every large company grew largely through one channel and only moved on when they started to exhaust it.

> "you only need to test something new if there's something wrong with the current method"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert have stuck to one system, Pain Point SEO, for over five years after committing to it.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the one channel or process currently producing your leads, trials or demos.
2. Define the failure condition in numbers before you need it: for example conversions per published article falling below X for two consecutive quarters.
3. Put every new tactic idea on a dated backlog instead of into the calendar.
4. Review the backlog only when the failure condition trips, not on a monthly cadence.
5. When it does trip, pick the backlog item that has the most public evidence behind it, not the newest one.
6. Keep executing the working process at full volume the entire time the backlog sits idle.

**Pitfall:** Teams that test continuously mistake activity for progress. The signal you have hit this is a stakeholder complaint about lead volume alongside a marketer's list of everything they are trying.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should name bottom-of-funnel blog and template pages as the committed channel and write the failure condition down. New tactics such as a fresh AI-visibility experiment go on a backlog and only get budget if demo requests from organic fall below the agreed threshold.

**Apply anywhere:** Name the one channel currently producing your pipeline and write down, in numbers, what failure would look like. Park every new tactic on a backlog until that condition trips.

### 20. Open every new engagement with pain point SEO from day one  `136.16`
*core · best practices · source 136*

Grow and Convert's closing rule from 2.5 years at Leadfeeder: they now lead with pain point SEO as the main strategy for every SaaS client, because they have shown across multiple companies that it is the fastest way to accelerate traffic and leads. Starting from day one rather than discovering it in month six is what gets conversion results early in an engagement. The three learnings they name are a shift in content strategy, a shift in promotion strategy, and doubling down on leads as the measure of success, and they stress there is rarely a straight line, only constant testing and doubling down on what works. The practical reading for anyone starting a program is to skip the top-funnel phase entirely rather than earning the lesson at their own expense.

> "we lead with pain point SEO as the main strategy"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say leading with pain point SEO from day one has produced conversion results early for multiple clients beyond Leadfeeder, whose blog reached 225+ attributed signups a month, around 12% of total.

**How to do it**

1. In the first week of a program, build the bottom-funnel list from customer interviews and the competitor set, before any calendar exists.
2. Publish the first six to ten pieces exclusively from the high-intent frameworks: comparisons, alternatives, category lists, manual how-tos.
3. Set the reporting metric to leads per article from the first month, not sessions.
4. Attach a promotion plan to each piece at commissioning, since these formats will not spread on their own.
5. Log two or three tactical tests per quarter alongside the main program, which is how the frameworks were found in the first place.
6. Only open the mid-funnel how-to backlog once the bottom-funnel list is genuinely exhausted.
7. Review conversions per framework each quarter and reallocate production toward the frameworks that convert.

**Pitfall:** Programs that start with brand awareness content spend the first year building traffic that does not convert, then have to run the painful transition, with its dip and its four-to-six month lag, from a worse position.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any new pabau.com content push, including a new vertical or a new market, should open with competitor comparisons, alternatives and category pages for that segment, not with introductory aesthetics guides. Set demo bookings per article as the reported metric from the first month.

**Apply anywhere:** Start any new content program with bottom-funnel pain point content rather than brand awareness pieces. Publish comparisons, alternatives, category lists and manual how-tos first, measure leads per article from month one, and open the top-funnel backlog only when the high-intent list is exhausted.

### 21. Pair paid-promotion content with SEO content while a young domain warms up  `133.4`
*core · best practices · source 133*

Grow and Convert knew Circuit's DR 18 domain would take a long time to produce organic traffic, so they deliberately ran two content tracks at once. One track targeted SEO keywords. The other was written to work on paid social, aimed at the audience rather than at a keyword. Their example is an article on adding local delivery to a small business during covid-19, written because Circuit's team was seeing demand for that use case, and promoted to small business owners on paid social. That track produced both traffic and trial signups in the months when organic produced none. Their reasoning is a portfolio one: combine short-term channels, meaning paid ads, with long-term channels, meaning SEO and link building, so the monthly report has something in it while the compounding channel is still flat. It also buys the political room to keep investing in SEO through the slow months.

> "a combination of distribution tactics that focus on both short term channels"

**Evidence:** Circuit: paid promotion of purpose-written articles produced traffic and Teams trial signups during the first four months, a period in which organic produced zero conversions.

**How to do it**

1. Estimate how long organic will take given the domain's backlink strength relative to the SERPs you are targeting, and assume it is longer on a young domain.
2. Split the content calendar into two tracks: keyword-targeted pieces and audience-targeted pieces built for paid distribution.
3. Source audience-targeted topics from what the sales and support teams are hearing demand for right now, not from a keyword tool.
4. Put paid social budget behind the audience pieces and target the buyer persona directly.
5. Track conversions separately by track so the paid contribution is visible in the monthly report.
6. Keep the SEO track running at full volume regardless of the paid results, since it is the compounding asset.
7. Retire or reduce the paid track once organic conversions start arriving.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Letting the paid track eat the SEO track because it shows results first. The paid conversions stop the day the budget stops, and Grow and Convert are clear that the compounding growth comes from ranking, not from promotion.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep a small track of articles written for paid distribution to aesthetic practice owners, sourced from what sales is hearing that quarter, so new content areas show return before rankings arrive. Report those conversions separately from organic so neither track hides the other.

**Apply anywhere:** On a young domain, run a paid-distribution content track alongside the SEO track. Write the paid pieces for the audience and the moment rather than for a keyword, promote them on paid social, and report the two tracks separately so the compounding SEO investment survives the flat months.

### 22. Penalties only lift at the next core update  `18.14`
*core · content insights · source 18*

The model presented is that ranking penalties/suppressions are not applied at crawl or indexing time — they run as a separate post-indexing analysis layer, meaning a page can be indexed and technically fine while still being held down by a heuristic score ('backstop') that keeps it capped at, say, position 55 regardless of how many genuine improvements are made. Per the speaker, that suppression only lifts when a new core algorithm update rewrites or replaces the underlying heuristic — not through incremental on-page or off-page work — so a site 'could be doing 100 good things' and see zero measurable change until the next core update lands. This is offered as the explanation for why some sites hold a positive ranking state despite bad practices for a long stretch while others do good work for months with nothing to show for it, which the speaker says makes it especially hard for agencies to prove ongoing value within normal client-reporting timelines.

> "they don't run at indexing time, they run as a post-indexing analysis"

**Evidence:** The speaker's model: penalty heuristics run post-indexing and only get rewritten when a core update lands, illustrated by the 'position 55 backstop' example and the claim that recovery timelines regularly outlast a 12-month agency-client engagement.

**Apply:** When a page or section plateaus despite real improvements, don't conclude the work isn't paying off — treat core-update cycles, not weekly/monthly reporting, as the honest timeframe for judging whether a suppressed page has recovered, and log fix dates against core-update dates to test this later.

### 23. Pick keywords that showcase the features your retention data favors  `131.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 131*

Smartlook was known for heatmaps and session recording, but their retention data showed customers who used the deeper product analytics features, such as event tracking, funnel analysis, retention tables, crash reports and cross-platform analytics, got more value and stayed longer. So the SEO objective was not just traffic. It was repositioning the brand from a web analytics tool to a product analytics tool, to attract users likely to need those sticky features. Grow and Convert then chose keywords, including comparison pieces against Amplitude and Mixpanel rather than only Hotjar and FullStory, that let the content demonstrate the advanced feature set. The lesson is to let churn and retention data pick which features the content program argues for, then choose keywords whose searchers need those features.

> "got more value out of the tool and stuck around longer"

**Evidence:** Smartlook shifted positioning from web analytics to product analytics on the strength of retention data, and Cisco's stated acquisition rationale echoed the benefits the content had focused on.

**How to do it**

1. Pull retention or churn data by feature adoption and identify which features correlate with customers staying longest.
2. Write down the user persona who needs those features and how their search language differs from your current audience.
3. List the competitors that persona already knows, which may not be the competitors you usually compare against.
4. Build comparison and alternatives keywords against that second set of competitors, not only the obvious ones.
5. Add JTBD keywords that describe the jobs the sticky features perform.
6. Brief every piece so the sticky feature is the demonstrated answer, with screenshots of the actual workflow.
7. Track sign-ups from these pieces separately and check whether their feature adoption matches the target profile.
8. Revisit the retention cut every two quarters and reprioritize the keyword list against it.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Ranking for the keywords your best-known feature owns brings in users who churn. The signal is strong traffic and sign-up volume alongside flat retention.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should check which modules correlate with retention, likely online booking plus automated recalls plus stock or reporting, and steer the blog keyword list toward the searches made by practices that need those, rather than toward generic 'clinic software' traffic.

**Apply anywhere:** Find which features correlate with your best retention, then build the keyword list around searches made by people who need those features, including comparisons against the competitors that audience already knows.
