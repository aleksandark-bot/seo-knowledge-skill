### 1. Test early product ideas with Google Doc sales pages to an existing audience  `160.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 160*

Grow and Convert built the audience before the product. They grew the site to over 24,000 monthly readers in about six months, then tested products against that audience one at a time: a phone course, an in-person workshop, and an online course. Each was sold from a Google Doc sales page rather than a built site. The results told them what to keep. The phone course sold out, the workshop sold a couple, and the first online course sold nine seats off an email list of thousands and a site doing more than 10,000 visitors a month. They read the nine seats as a product problem, not a traffic problem, and kept iterating until inbound emails pointed them at the agency service.

> "a phone course, an in-person workshop and lastly an online course"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert reached over 24,000 monthly readers in roughly six months, then sold out a phone course, sold a couple of workshop seats, and sold nine seats on their first online course off an email list of thousands.

**How to do it**

1. Grow an audience on one clearly defined topic before building anything to sell.
2. Write the offer as a plain Google Doc sales page: who it is for, what they get, price, and how to pay.
3. Send it to the email list and link it from the relevant articles rather than building a site or funnel.
4. Record units sold per offer and compare against list size and monthly visitors.
5. Treat a low number, such as nine sales off a list of thousands, as evidence the product is wrong rather than the traffic.
6. Read the replies you get after a weak launch; asks for something adjacent are the next product to test.
7. Change the product, not the content strategy, and re-run the same page format for the next offer.

**Tools:** Google Docs

**Pitfall:** Building a polished sales site or funnel before knowing whether anyone wants the offer. It hides the product signal behind conversion-rate excuses and costs weeks per test.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau builds a full landing page for a new add-on or template product, David can validate demand with a single plain page linked from the two or three articles that already rank for the related topic, and judge by signups rather than by page polish.

**Apply anywhere:** Validate new offers with a plain document sales page sent to your existing audience before building anything. Low sales against a large list mean the product is wrong, not the traffic, and the replies to a weak launch usually name the offer people actually want.

### 2. The channel results: YouTube launched it, X sustains it, Google failed  `59.8`
*core · content insights · source 59*

Jackie's channel numbers are unusually candid and cut against the obvious assumptions. Meta ads work but disappoint: about $120 cost per acquisition, payback in two to three months, churn at 10-15% - profitable but worse than he expected. Organic X, running the swipe-file funnel, is by far the most profitable. YouTube and Google ads did not work at all: their CAC there was around $400, and they spent roughly $20k to find that out. Reddit ads perform about as well as Meta, targeting the SEO and local SEO subreddits. But the most important number is the launch: he gave a coupon on X and a coupon on YouTube, did about $15k MRR at launch, and 90% of it came from YouTube. His conclusion is that if he were starting a new software company he'd go all in on YouTube and dominate an industry - he'd been talking about local SEO on YouTube for around a year before launching a product about it. Cody's framing is that this is pent-up demand: you build the audience, then capture the demand with an audience that already trusts you.

> "let me pull up our meta ads"

**How to do it**

1. Separate paid acquisition performance from launch performance - they answer different questions.
2. Talk publicly about the problem for months before you have a product to sell against it.
3. At launch, give trackable coupons per channel so you can see where the revenue actually came from.
4. Don't assume the channel that launched you is the channel that sustains you - for him it flipped from YouTube to organic X.
5. Set a spend ceiling for testing a paid channel before you start; he spent $20k discovering YouTube and Google ads didn't work for him.
6. Measure paid channels on CAC against payback period and churn, not on cost per lead.
7. Test Reddit ads against your subreddits - he found them comparable to Meta.

**Tools:** Meta Ads, Reddit Ads, YouTube

**Pitfall:** The channel that worked organically (YouTube) was the one that failed as a paid channel for the same company - the audience relationship is what performed, not the platform.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the launch lesson generalises: sustained public teaching about the problem builds the demand that a launch captures, and channel performance should be attributed with per-channel codes rather than inferred.

**Apply anywhere:** The launch lesson generalises: sustained public teaching about the problem builds the demand that a launch captures, and channel performance should be attributed with per-channel codes rather than inferred.

### 3. The swipe-file funnel: give the whole thing away, gate the copy  `59.7`
*core · ai workflows · source 59*

Jackie's most profitable channel turned out to be organic X, and the funnel is reproducible. He has years of accumulated checklists, ebooks, presentations and swipe files. He posts the entire thing as a wall of text on X - his example format is 'if I had to get local SEO clients or I'll be homeless, this is what I'd do' with 'bookmark for later' - so the value is genuinely delivered in the post. The CTA at the bottom asks people to comment a keyword to get it emailed, which drives engagement and therefore reach: about half a million impressions in a month, which he values at roughly $20k of CPM on Meta. But they don't send the asset on the comment - the DM asks for an email address first. After signup: a VSL immediately, heavy retargeting, and a 40-email flow. The lead magnet is a 65-point checklist, and the landing page is at localrank.so/checklist.

> "multiple checklists or like ebooks"

**How to do it**

1. Take an existing internal asset - a checklist, an SOP, a swipe file - and post the whole substance of it publicly as a text post.
2. Frame it as a scenario ('if I had to start over tomorrow, this is what I'd do') and tell people to bookmark it.
3. Put a comment-triggered CTA at the bottom asking for a specific keyword, which drives the engagement that buys reach.
4. DM everyone who comments, but ask for an email address before sending the asset.
5. Send them to a dedicated landing page for the asset so you can pixel them.
6. Show a VSL immediately after signup rather than just confirming.
7. Retarget hard and drop them into a long email sequence - his is around 40 emails.
8. Add a second CTA inside the thread to join the paid offer directly, for the people already convinced.

**Tools:** Twitter/X, Meta Ads

**Pitfall:** The post has to genuinely deliver the value or the engagement bait doesn't work - the reason the wall of text earns reach is that people find it worth bookmarking.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has exactly these assets already (compliance checklists, template packs, onboarding SOPs) - posting the full substance publicly and gating only the formatted copy is a cheap way to turn them into email signups.

**Apply anywhere:** If you already have checklists, template packs and internal SOPs, posting the full substance publicly and gating only the formatted copy is a cheap way to turn them into email signups.

### 4. Topic selection lifted blog conversion rate from 0.2% to under 0.8%  `141.9`
*core · content insights · source 141*

Grow and Convert's longest-running client in the article, a B2B SaaS company selling to marketers and salespeople, had been with them nearly two years. Over that period blog conversion rate moved from a little over 0.2% to just under 0.8%, which they call a 300% increase. Their emphasis is on what did not produce it: no content upgrades, no A/B testing of lead magnets or button colors, no hacks. They attribute the whole gain to the content strategy, specifically pain-point SEO, and back it with their own earlier claim that the biggest determinant of whether a post converts is the idea behind the post, not the call to action inside it. The same account crossed 150 signups a month on a fairly linear climb. They accept the traffic looks unimpressive for two years, around 14,000 organic a month, because the goal was signups rather than head-term traffic.

> "a little over 0.2% conversion rate, to now right under 0.8%"

**Evidence:** B2B SaaS client over roughly two years: conversion rate from just over 0.2% to just under 0.8%, a 300% increase, and over 150 signups a month, with organic around 13,845 sessions in July 2019.

**How to do it**

1. Calculate current blog conversion rate as conversions divided by blog sessions, and record it as the baseline.
2. Bucket every existing post by funnel stage and compute conversion rate per bucket rather than site-wide.
3. Stop the CRO backlog on low-converting posts; changing the CTA on a post about the wrong topic does not fix it.
4. Build the next quarter's topics from customer pain points with buying intent, ignoring reported search volume.
5. Publish, then measure conversion rate on the new cohort separately from the legacy cohort.
6. Expect the site-wide rate to move slowly, since the legacy posts stay in the denominator.
7. Report signups per month alongside the rate so a growing denominator is not read as a decline.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Teams try to fix a low blog conversion rate with content upgrades, lead magnets and button tests; Grow and Convert say none of that produced their gain and the topic choice did.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before David commissions any CRO work on Pabau's blog, bucket existing posts by intent and check the conversion rate per bucket. If low-intent aesthetics explainers dominate, the fix is the topic list, not the CTA placement.

**Apply anywhere:** If your blog converts poorly, change the topics before you change the CTAs. One agency moved a client's blog conversion rate from 0.2% to just under 0.8% over two years purely through pain-point topic selection, with no content upgrades or button testing.

### 5. Track articles at three depths: demos, trials and influenced deals  `181.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 181*

Grow and Convert describe article-level tracking at three distinct depths rather than one conversion number. They track which articles drive demos, which keywords convert to trials, and which content influences closed deals. The three are different measurements and they need different plumbing: demo attribution is a form-level source field, trial attribution runs at the keyword level rather than the page level, and influenced revenue needs the CRM to record every content touch on an opportunity, not just the last one. Keeping them separate matters because an article can produce a lot of trials and no revenue, or almost no demos while appearing on most closed-won deal paths. They present this decade of conversion data as the thing that distinguishes them from agencies reporting rankings and traffic.

> "We track which articles drive demos, which"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite nearly a decade of conversion data across 100+ clients since 2016 as the basis for this reporting split.

**How to do it**

1. Add a hidden field to every demo and contact form capturing the landing page and the last content page viewed.
2. Pass the entry keyword or query into the trial signup record so trial conversion can be read per keyword, not just per page.
3. Turn on multi-touch content tracking in the CRM so every article viewed on an opportunity path is recorded, not just the first or last.
4. Build one report per depth: demos per article, trials per keyword, and closed-won deals with at least one content touch.
5. Review the three together each quarter and look for articles that rank high on one and low on another.
6. Promote and link-build to the articles that appear on closed-won paths, regardless of their traffic.
7. Do not report rankings and sessions as the headline metric once these three are running.

**Tools:** HubSpot, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Reporting a single blended conversion rate hides the articles that produce trials that never buy. The signal is a top-converting article that never appears on a closed-won deal.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should record the last article viewed on every demo request and carry the entry query into the signup record, so David can rank blog and template pages by demos and by influenced deals rather than by sessions.

**Apply anywhere:** Track content at three depths separately: demo requests per article, trial signups per entry keyword, and closed-won deals with a content touch, and prioritize links to the pages that appear on won deals.

### 6. Treat 0.5% blog-to-lead as the achievable direct conversion benchmark  `147.3`
*core · best practices · source 147*

Grow and Convert say they have seen single SaaS blog articles convert anywhere from just above zero to over 5% into a free trial, and that for a whole blog half a percent is average and achievable when traffic sits in five-figure monthly uniques. They stress three exclusions that make this figure usable: it is not a landing-page rate, not a homepage rate, and specifically the rate from blog posts. They warn that B2B enterprise, agency, subscription and ecommerce businesses vary wildly on this step and have to measure their own. They also repeat that the rate falls as traffic grows. The practical use of the benchmark is as a floor: if your blog converts far below 0.5% direct, the problem is the topics you write about rather than the CTA.

> "half a percent is average/achievable when traffic"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report single SaaS blog articles ranging from just above zero to over 5% to free trial, with 0.5% typical blog-wide.

**How to do it**

1. Define the lead event precisely as the first step toward purchase: trial start, demo request, or booked sales call.
2. Filter GA4 to blog-post URLs only, excluding the homepage and any standalone landing pages.
3. Divide blog-attributed lead events by blog unique users over 30 days.
4. Compare the result against 0.5% for five-figure monthly traffic.
5. If you are far below, audit topic and intent before rewriting CTAs, since range across single articles runs from near zero to 5%.
6. Break the rate out per article and rank articles by it, so high-converting topics can be identified and repeated.
7. Set your own internal benchmark once you have three months of data, since B2B enterprise, agency and ecommerce rates vary widely.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Borrowing a landing-page conversion benchmark and applying it to blog posts. The article is explicit that these are different measurements and mixing them makes a healthy blog look broken.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should compute pabau.com's blog-to-demo rate per article and treat 0.5% as the pass mark. Template and comparison pages should clear it comfortably; definitional blog posts that sit near zero are refresh or consolidation candidates.

**Apply anywhere:** Compute your blog-to-lead rate per article and treat 0.5% as the pass mark. Commercial and comparison pages should clear it; definitional posts sitting near zero are refresh or consolidation candidates.

### 7. Treat 40% very disappointed as the product/market fit bar  `161.2`
*core · best practices · source 161*

Question two of the survey asks how the customer would feel if they could no longer use the product. Khanal gives the threshold plainly: 40% or more of respondents should say they would be very disappointed. Below that, you have not reached product/market fit, and the fix is product work rather than more marketing. The open-ended box under the question is where the value sits, because it asks people to explain why they feel that way. He treats the score as a gate to retest: change the product or service based on what the open box says, then run the same question again and compare. The point is that a marketing team scaling acquisition against a sub-40% score is buying churn.

> "40+% of respondents should answer that they would be very disappointed"

**Evidence:** Khanal cites Hiten Shah's product/market fit method, which uses qualitative feedback and the 40% very-disappointed threshold, learned at a GrowthChats presentation.

**How to do it**

1. Ask 'How would you feel if you could no longer use X?' with options very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed.
2. Add a required open-ended box asking why they chose that answer.
3. Send it only to people who have actually used the product, not to all signups.
4. Calculate the percentage answering very disappointed and compare against the 40% bar.
5. If under 40%, stop scaling paid acquisition and read the open boxes for the missing value.
6. Ship the product or service changes those boxes point at.
7. Re-run the same question with the same audience after the changes and compare the two percentages.
8. Repeat until you clear 40%, then scale acquisition.

**Tools:** Survey Monkey, Typeform

**Prompt / template:**

```text
How would you feel if you could no longer use (Product or Company Name)?
```

**Pitfall:** Sending the question to everyone on the list, including trial signups who never logged in, drags the score down and hides the fact that active users love the product. Segment before you read the number.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should run this question separately for practices in their first 90 days and for practices past a year, so onboarding problems do not get read as a product problem.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask active users how they would feel if the product disappeared, and hold yourself to 40% answering very disappointed before scaling acquisition spend.

### 8. Treat a generic intro as a conversion leak, not a style problem  `127.8`
*core · content insights · source 127*

Grow and Convert build the whole case for intros on a chain of consequences rather than on taste. A high-school-paper opener immediately gives away that the author does not know the reader. That implies to some extent that they do not really care about the reader. They do not get you. So the questions that follow are: why should you trust them, why should you keep reading, and, in bold, why should you opt in to their list or request a demo or contact sales. The intro is where the reader decides whether the company understands their needs. Their closing rhetorical turn makes the mechanism concrete: if this company really understood you, why did they have to tell a 20-year sales veteran that most businesses will need a CRM at some point. This reframes intro quality as a measurable lead-generation input, not a copywriting nicety.

> "immediately give away that the author doesn't know you"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert trace the chain from a generic opener to lost trust to a lost opt-in, demo request or sales contact.

**Pitfall:** Because the damage shows up as a slightly lower demo-request rate spread across a whole library, no single page ever looks broken. Teams keep optimizing CTA placement while the leak sits in the first two sentences.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau article gets traffic but no demo requests, David should read the intro before touching the CTA block. An opener that explains the basics of practice management to a clinic owner is the likeliest cause.

**Apply anywhere:** When a page gets traffic but few conversions, audit the first two sentences before you move the CTA. An opener written below the reader's level tells them you do not understand their business, and that is the moment they decide not to trust you enough to convert.

### 9. Treat the AI chat as the first sales call and write the sales material  `80.6`
*core · best practices · source 80*

Grow and Convert make a structural point about where selling now happens. In the Google era you showed up for a topic, drove the visitor to your site, and the site did the selling. Now the first sales touchpoint is the AI chat, and the model is the one selling your solution. That reframes what content is for: you are not writing to earn a click, you are supplying the model with the material it needs to pitch you to the right person in the right way. They say the companies recommended most will be those whose published content articulates features, benefits, differentiators and use cases in depth, and that it has to be highly specific because the conversations are highly specific.

> "where AI is now the one selling your solution"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state the first sales touchpoint has moved from the website to the AI chat, and attribute their own recommendation to eight years of published content on one specific topic.

**How to do it**

1. List the questions a prospect asks on a real sales call, in the order they ask them.
2. Check that each answer exists in published content, not only in a deck or a sales email.
3. Write the missing answers as page sections with the same directness a rep would use, including who the product is not for.
4. Include the objections and the honest limits, since a model that only has marketing claims cannot position you against a specific need.
5. Cover features, benefits, differentiators and use cases separately rather than blending them into one benefits list.
6. Add a named customer example to each use case so the model can attach proof to the claim.
7. Prompt a model with the situation of a real recent deal and see whether it pitches you the way your rep would.
8. Feed the gaps back into the content plan each quarter.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Leaving your best sales explanations trapped in decks, demo calls and email threads. The model can only sell from what it can read, so the site keeps producing generic recommendations while your reps have the good version.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish what its sales team says about which practice types fit, what Pabau replaces, and where it is not the right choice. Those pages become the material the model uses when a clinic owner asks for a recommendation.

**Apply anywhere:** Write down the questions your sales team answers on calls and publish the answers as content. The model can only pitch you using material it can read.

### 10. Treat the brand SERP as the leak point at the bottom of the funnel  `73.22`
*core · content insights · source 73*

Barnard's origin story for this work is a lost deal. He went into a meeting in 2012, knocked it out of the park, and was told the client would definitely work with him. He walked to the station, got on the train, and received an email saying they had decided against it. When he asked why, they said that when they searched his name it said Jason Barnard is a voiceover artist for the cartoon Boowa and Kwala - his previous career. They did not want to work with a cartoon maker. He puts the value at $100,000 and points out he only found out because they happened to tell him. His framing is that the search results for your name are your online business card, people look at it before they look at your website, and a strong knowledge panel produces an immediate 'they must be important' reaction. Leakage at the bottom of the funnel is the biggest enemy: you have done all the work to get them there and then you leak.

> "Leakage at the bottom of the funnel is your biggest enemy"

**Evidence:** Barnard lost a $100,000 engagement in 2012 after the client searched his name and found he was described as a voiceover artist for the cartoon Boowa and Kwala.

**How to do it**

1. Search your brand name and each key employee's name in an incognito window and read the first screen as a prospect would.
2. List anything that contradicts your current positioning: an old career, a previous company, a discontinued product, a wrong category.
3. Correct the source of each stale fact on the properties you control, since the SERP is assembled from those.
4. Add the current descriptor consistently across the profiles that rank on the brand SERP.
5. Ask lost prospects why they did not proceed, since the search-result objection is one they rarely volunteer.
6. Re-check the brand SERP monthly, particularly after any personnel or product change.
7. Treat the brand SERP as a bottom-of-funnel conversion asset with an owner, not as a vanity check.

**Pitfall:** Never learning this is happening. Barnard only found out because the prospect happened to explain, and he says he cannot imagine how many others made the same decision silently.

**Apply at Pabau:** Search 'Pabau' and the names of Pabau's founders and sales leads before any enterprise push, and fix anything on the first screen that suggests a different product or an older positioning.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat the search results for your brand and your key people as a bottom-of-funnel conversion asset. Audit the first screen for stale or contradicting descriptions, fix them at source, and re-check monthly.

### 11. Treat the podcast as the vehicle and the newsletter as the product  `68.10`
*core · best practices · source 68*

Cody's sharpest structural claim: if you are building media, the podcast exists to feed the email newsletter, and the newsletter is where the money is. He estimates the ad dollars available to sponsor email newsletters are roughly 100x those available for podcasts, and gives the reason: newsletters are quantifiable. Sponsors see open rates, click rates and clicks tracked directly to their site, so revenue is attributable. Podcast impressions have no comparable unit, which is why podcast sponsorship is hard to sell. The commercial structure he recommends is to sell the newsletter slot as the main product at a monthly rate and throw in the pre-roll read on the episode as a kicker. He extends the same logic to sales: the podcast is top of funnel entertainment, and the selling happens in the newsletter.

> "the podcast is a vehicle for the email newsletter"

**Evidence:** Cody estimates newsletter sponsorship budgets at roughly 100x podcast sponsorship budgets, attributing the gap to measurable opens, clicks and tracked site visits.

**How to do it**

1. Set up the email newsletter before or alongside the show, not after it.
2. Make every episode and every clip carry a call to action to join the newsletter.
3. Instrument newsletter links with UTM tags so click-to-conversion is traceable.
4. Report open rate, click rate and attributed conversions to prospective sponsors as your core numbers.
5. Price the newsletter slot as the main sponsorship product on a monthly rate.
6. Include the podcast pre-roll read as an added extra in the same package rather than selling it alone.
7. Do the actual selling of your own product in the newsletter, keeping the show educational and entertaining.

**Pitfall:** Building the show first and the list later means years of audience arriving with no way to reach them again, and no sellable inventory because podcast impressions cannot be priced.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content should route toward an owned email list, since that is the only channel where engagement is measurable and where the sales conversation can happen without competing for search visibility.

**Apply anywhere:** Build the newsletter as the asset and treat the podcast as source material for it. Sponsors pay far more for measurable email inventory than for unmeasurable podcast impressions, and the newsletter is where you sell.

### 12. Trigger the upsell from data the free product already sees  `170.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 170*

Campbell describes the mechanism that makes the wedge work. Because ProfitWell holds the customer's financial data, the team runs algorithms on top of it and can approach the customer with a specific diagnosis. In his words, they can see 'hey you have a problem with this, we have a product that helps with that problem, do you want to hook that up?' The offer is not a campaign, it is a detected condition. Retain addresses churn, Recognized addresses subscription-to-GAAP accounting, and each has a measurable signature in the data the free product already collects. The customer has effectively pre-qualified themselves before anyone reaches out.

> "we have a product that helps with that problem"

**Evidence:** Campbell runs 'different algorithms on top of ProfitWell' to detect which of Retain, Recognized or the Price Intelligently service each account needs.

**How to do it**

1. List each paid product and write down the single metric in the free product that indicates the customer needs it.
2. Set a numeric threshold on that metric that reliably separates who needs it from who does not.
3. Build a query or alert that flags accounts crossing the threshold.
4. Write the outreach so it opens with the customer's own number, not with a product pitch.
5. Include the size of the problem in their currency, so the price is compared to the loss and not to a competitor.
6. Track conversion rate by trigger metric and drop any trigger that converts below your baseline outreach.
7. Review thresholds quarterly as the customer base changes.

**Tools:** Stripe

**Pitfall:** Firing the offer on tenure or plan size instead of a real diagnostic. That turns a credible diagnosis into an ordinary upsell email and burns the trust the free product bought you.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau already holds no-show rates, rebooking rates and retention for every practice on the platform. David should trigger in-app content and Plus add-on offers on those thresholds, and link the relevant blog article by the same signal rather than by broadcast.

**Apply anywhere:** Use the data your free or core product already holds to detect which customers need which paid product. Set a numeric threshold per product, alert on it, and open outreach with the customer's own figure.

### 13. Turn Google Search Partners off and measure MQL rate before and after  `158.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 158*

Google Search Partners places your ads beyond Google search, including YouTube, directory pages and search boxes inside other websites. Advertisers like it because reach grows and CPC is usually much lower. Grow and Convert give two objections. First, intent is unreadable off Google: someone searching 'top magento developers' on Google is probably hiring, while the same phrase typed into a news journal or public forum search box tells you nothing. Second, Google, not you, decides how much of your spend goes to partners versus search. Their client's numbers are the evidence: with Search Partners on, 7.6% of leads became MQLs on roughly $9k a month in spend; with it off, 24% became MQLs on roughly $7k a month. Both figures are after junk leads were removed, and the client reported far fewer junk leads and better conversations with qualified prospects afterwards.

> "Google (not you) decides how much ad spend is spent"

**Evidence:** Client: 7.6% of leads became MQLs at ~$9k/month spend with Search Partners on, versus 24% at ~$7k/month with it off.

**How to do it**

1. Record the current baseline: 30 days of spend, leads, junk leads removed, and MQL conversion rate.
2. Open campaign settings in Google Ads and untick the Google Search Partners network option.
3. Leave every other setting, bid and keyword unchanged so the test is clean.
4. Run for at least 30 days and expect CPC and click volume to rise.
5. Recount leads with the same junk-lead filter applied as the baseline.
6. Compare MQL rate and cost per MQL, not CPC, across the two periods.
7. Ask the sales team whether inbound conversation quality changed, as a qualitative check.
8. Leave Search Partners off permanently unless MQL rate fell.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Turning Search Partners off raises CPC and cuts impressions, so anyone reading up-funnel metrics will call it a failure. Only the MQL rate comparison shows what happened.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau runs Search Partners, turn it off and compare qualified demo rate over 30 days. Ads appearing in unrelated site search boxes will pull consumers looking for treatments, not practice owners buying software.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn Google Search Partners off, hold every other setting constant for 30 days, and compare qualified-lead rate before and after. Expect CPC to rise and lead quality to rise with it.

### 14. Turn off Google Search Partners on every search campaign  `156.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 156*

Grow and Convert say they never use Google Search Partners. Search Partners is the network of third-party sites where your ads can also appear, and Google recommends enabling it during campaign setup because the CPCs are generally much lower than on Google itself. Their testing found it rarely, if ever, produces high quality leads. The mechanism they give is intent: someone typing your keyword into Google is in an active buying mindset, while someone on a partner site may be browsing a blog or news site with no comparable intent, and some partner sites are simply irrelevant to the query. Cheaper clicks therefore look good on cost per click while being, in their words, complete junk. It is a checkbox during setup and is easy to leave on by accident.

> "we never use Search Partners"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say their own testing showed Search Partners rarely if ever results in high-quality leads, despite lower CPCs than Google itself.

**How to do it**

1. Open each search campaign's settings and expand the networks section.
2. Uncheck 'Include Google search partners'.
3. Uncheck the Display Network too if the campaign is meant to be search only.
4. Do this on every new campaign at creation, since the setup flow enables it by default.
5. If a campaign already ran with partners on, segment performance by network to quantify the wasted spend before reporting.
6. Judge the change on lead quality and cost per qualified lead, not on the average CPC, which will rise.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Average CPC goes up after you switch partners off, which reads as a regression on a click-cost dashboard even though qualified leads improve. Report on lead quality instead.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should confirm Search Partners is off on every practice management search campaign, and segment any historical spend by network to see how much went to partner sites.

### 15. Use 1% as the conservative conversion rate, not the 0.5 to 5% range  `129.9`
*core · content insights · source 129*

Grow and Convert publish their observed conversion rates from client content and then tell you to plan with a different number. Across their work, a single article ranges from 0.5% to 5%, and the average across dozens of articles runs about 0.5% to 2%. For estimating whether SEO is worth it, they instruct using 1% as a safe conservative rate specifically for high-buying-intent keywords. This is a looser and more optimistic planning figure than the tighter 0.25% to 0.75% bottom-of-funnel band recorded elsewhere in this base from the same agency, so treat 1% as the go/no-go screening number and the narrower band as the number to defend results against. They also warn that conversion rate varies significantly by article and keyword, and that it depends heavily on how well the product is sold inside the article itself.

> "range from 0.5% to 5% for a single article and average about 0.5% to 2%"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert measure 0.5% to 5% conversion for a single article and 0.5% to 2% averaged across dozens; they recommend 1% as the conservative estimating figure for high-intent keywords.

**How to do it**

1. Use 1% as the conversion rate in any go/no-go SEO value estimate on high-buying-intent keywords.
2. Do not use the top of the observed range; a 5% article exists but is not a planning input.
3. Keep the 0.5% to 2% multi-article average as the band you expect once you have dozens of pages live.
4. After five to ten ranked pages, replace the assumed 1% with your own measured per-article rate.
5. Segment measured rates by keyword intent, since the spread between article types is larger than the spread within one type.
6. Audit low-converting articles for how the product is actually sold in the body before blaming the keyword.
7. Re-forecast the program with the measured rate rather than defending the original 1%.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Applying 1% to lower-intent or top-of-funnel content. The figure is specifically for high-buying-intent keywords, and top-funnel pages convert at a fraction of it, so a blended 1% assumption inflates the whole model.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should model 1% only on high-intent pages such as software comparisons and template downloads, and use a much lower figure for general blog how-tos. Track the real rate per page family in Analytics so the model stops being an assumption.

**Apply anywhere:** Plan with 1% conversion on high-buying-intent keywords, even though single articles range from 0.5% to 5% and multi-article averages sit at 0.5% to 2%. Never apply that 1% to top-of-funnel pages, and replace the assumption with your own measured per-article rate once five to ten pages rank.

### 16. Use Grow and Convert's six benchmark conversion rates to rank keyword types  `142.1`
*core · content insights · source 142*

Use it as the priority order when you build a bottom-funnel keyword roadmap and have to decide what to write first.

> "we pulled six months of data from 95 articles"

**Evidence:** 95 articles, six months, 123,000 organic pageviews, 4,687 product-related conversions across the six keyword types.

**How to do it**

1. Build one spreadsheet row per bottom-funnel keyword you are considering, with a column for keyword type.
2. Tag each keyword as alternatives/competitor, versus, main category, category plus specificity, JTBD, or side category.
3. Attach the benchmark rate to each tag: 8.43%, 5.45%, 4.85%, 2.96%, 2.44%, 1.94%.
4. Multiply each keyword's monthly volume by its benchmark rate to get an expected monthly conversion figure.
5. Sort by expected conversions, not by volume, and commission the top of that list first.
6. Track actual conversion per post after 90 days and replace the benchmark with your own site's rate per type.
7. Kill or repoint any post whose measured rate falls below a quarter of its type benchmark after six months.

**Pitfall:** Treating the averages as forecasts for a single post. The alternatives average of 8.43% is pulled up by a few outliers; most posts in that group converted under 4%.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should tag every keyword in Pabau's content roadmap with one of these six types and sort the backlog by expected conversions. Competitor-alternative pages against the aesthetic clinic software rivals should move ahead of general category posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Tag every keyword in your content roadmap with one of these six types, attach the matching benchmark rate, and sort the backlog by expected conversions rather than by search volume. Competitor-alternative pages usually belong ahead of general category posts.

### 17. Use phrase and exact match only, never broad match  `158.3`
*core · best practices · source 158*

Grow and Convert never recommend broad match, even though Google prompts advertisers with a message saying 'Your campaign is using Smart Bidding. Use broad match to get more conversions at a similar or better ROI.' On their client's account the keyword 'magento agency', which has strong buying intent, was matching search terms like 'magento web', 'bigcommerce' and 'online marketing'. The first two are people learning about a platform, and the third is only word-association related. Broad match survives audits because those cheap irrelevant terms pull average CPC down and lift up-funnel metrics. Phrase and exact match still capture wording variation without changing meaning: exact picked up 'magento development services' and 'adobe agency' because Adobe owns Magento, and phrase picked up 'top magento agencies' and 'how to choose a magento agency'.

> "However, we never recommend using broad match."

**Evidence:** 'magento agency' on broad match served ads on 'magento web', 'bigcommerce' and 'online marketing'.

**How to do it**

1. Filter the keyword list in Google Ads by match type and list every broad match keyword.
2. Open the Search Terms report for each and export the terms it actually matched over 90 days.
3. Mark any term whose intent differs from the keyword, such as a platform name alone or a generic category phrase.
4. Convert every broad keyword to phrase or exact match rather than trying to negative-list your way out.
5. Add the clearly wrong terms as negative keywords at campaign level anyway, to catch close variants.
6. Ignore the in-platform Google prompt recommending broad match with Smart Bidding.
7. Recheck the Search Terms report two weeks later and confirm average CPC rose while lead quality improved.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Switching off broad match makes CPC and click volume look worse, so the change gets reverted before qualified lead data arrives. Hold it for at least a full sales cycle before judging.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any Pabau paid search on terms like 'clinic booking software' should run phrase or exact only. Broad match on 'aesthetic clinic' will pull in treatment searches from consumers rather than practice owners, and those never become demos.

**Apply anywhere:** Run phrase and exact match only. Broad match lowers CPC by matching loosely related searches, which flatters your reporting while spending budget on people who cannot buy from you.

### 18. Validate a paid offer by emailing your 200 most engaged subscribers  `183.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 183*

Grow and Convert's first monetization test was an 8-week phone course at $750. They built no funnel and no design. They wrote a Google Doc landing page, linked it from one of their blog posts, then emailed the 200 most engaged subscribers on their list. Mailchimp's 5-star engagement rating was the segmentation input: they sorted the list by rating and took the top slice. They capped the offer at 10 early-adopter seats and filled all 10, which they report as a 5% conversion rate from outreach to close. The point is that a first revenue test needs a segment, a cap and a price, not a product. The engaged-subscriber segment is what makes 200 emails enough to close 10 sales.

> "sent emails to the 200 most engaged subscribers"

**Evidence:** 200 emails to the top-engagement segment filled all 10 seats at $750, a 5% outreach-to-close rate, from a Google Doc landing page.

**How to do it**

1. Sort your email list by engagement rating in your ESP (they used Mailchimp's 5-star subscriber rating) and take the top ~200 addresses.
2. Write the offer as a plain Google Doc landing page rather than building a designed page.
3. Link that doc from one existing blog post that already gets traffic from the same audience.
4. Set a specific price and a hard seat cap (they used $750 and 10 early-adopter seats) so the test has a defined ceiling.
5. Email only that engaged segment, not the whole list, so a weak result is not confounded by cold subscribers.
6. Track outreach-to-close conversion; treat roughly 5% from an engaged segment as the benchmark they hit.
7. If the cap fills, run post-purchase interviews before building the scaled version of the offer.
8. If under half the seats fill, rewrite the offer for the buyer's own outcome before rebuilding the product.

**Tools:** Mailchimp, Google Docs

**Pitfall:** Emailing the whole list instead of the engaged segment. That dilutes the conversion rate and makes a viable offer look dead, because unengaged subscribers never opened the post the offer sits in.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should test any paid Pabau offering the same way before building it. Segment the Pabau newsletter by engagement, write a one-page doc, link it from a high-traffic blog article, and cap the pilot at 10 practices. That gives a real price signal without building a page or a product.

**Apply anywhere:** Test a paid offer before building it. Sort your list by engagement, take the top 200, write a Google Doc landing page, link it from a relevant existing post, set a price and a seat cap of about 10, and email only that segment. Roughly 5% outreach-to-close is the benchmark to beat.

### 20. Value an AI recommendation as a confirmatory click, not an impression  `76.16`
*core · general insights · source 76*

The paper's argument for why the stakes are high is economic, not technical. When an AI system acts as a qualified intermediary, its recommendation carries unusually high value, because by the time the user clicks through to the brand's site the decision is substantially made. That click is not exploratory, it is confirmatory. The authors call this the Perfect Click, citing Barnard, and conclude that a single AI-mediated recommendation may be worth more in conversion terms than hundreds of unqualified organic impressions. The conclusion section reinforces it: AI-referred traffic converts at rates several multiples higher than traditional organic, and users arriving via AI recommendation are more qualified, more intent-driven and more commercially valuable. This reframes reporting. A drop in AI-era sessions alongside stable revenue is the expected shape, not a failure, and volume-based organic targets misprice the channel.

> "That click is not exploratory; it is confirmatory"

**Evidence:** Barnard's 'Perfect Click'; the paper claims one AI-mediated recommendation may outweigh hundreds of unqualified organic impressions, and that AI-referred traffic converts several multiples higher than organic.

**Pitfall:** Judging AI visibility on session volume. The channel is designed to send fewer, later-stage visits, so a volume metric reads a working channel as a failing one.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should report AI-referred sessions against demo bookings rather than against total organic sessions, and expect a small, high-converting stream rather than volume.

**Apply anywhere:** Report AI-referred sessions against conversions rather than against total organic sessions, and expect a small, high-converting stream rather than volume.

### 21. Watch session recordings of converters to decide what to fix on an article  `140.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 140*

Lewis added a step the Grow and Convert course does not teach. She filtered Hotjar recordings to visitors who read one of her articles and then requested a free audit, and watched what they did. She had a fixed question list: which CTA did they click, how much of the article did they read before skimming, did they click internal links to other articles, and were any page elements pulling focus. The answers map directly to fixes. Quick bounces mean rewrite the intro; scrolling past the CTAs means move them or make them more visible. What she found was that users spent one to three minutes on an article and then went straight to requesting the audit, and rarely navigated the wider site first. She reads that as confirmation that bottom-of-funnel keywords should be the first priority, because those visitors buy off the article itself.

> "the user would spend anywhere from 1-3 minutes on an article"

**Evidence:** Hotjar recordings for ADA Compliance Pros showed converting users spending one to three minutes on an article then requesting the free compliance audit, rarely browsing the rest of the site.

**How to do it**

1. Install Hotjar and set the free-audit or demo form submission as a recorded event.
2. Filter recordings to sessions that both viewed a target article and completed the conversion.
3. Watch at least ten such sessions end to end before changing anything.
4. For each session note which CTA was clicked, the scroll depth at which reading became skimming, and any internal link taken.
5. If sessions bounce inside 15 seconds, rewrite the introduction to answer the query in the first two sentences.
6. If sessions scroll past a CTA without pausing, move it higher or change its styling rather than adding more CTAs.
7. Note any element that repeatedly draws a cursor away from the article and remove it.
8. Record the median time on page before conversion; if it is one to three minutes, stop building long top-of-funnel pieces for that audience.

**Tools:** Hotjar

**Pitfall:** Watching random recordings instead of converter recordings tells you about people who were never going to buy. Filter to sessions that converted, or you will optimize for the wrong visitor.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should filter Pabau's session recordings to visitors who booked a demo after reading a template or comparison page. If they convert off one page without touching the rest of the site, the demo CTA belongs inside those pages rather than in the site navigation.

**Apply anywhere:** Filter your session recordings to visitors who read a specific article and then converted, and watch at least ten. Note the CTA clicked, the scroll depth where reading turns to skimming, and any distracting element. Fast bounces mean fix the intro; scroll-past means reposition the CTA. If converters decide inside a few minutes on one page, invest in bottom-of-funnel pages rather than long guides.

### 23. Write a different inline CTA for each article, tied to its feature  `144.4`
*core · best practices · source 144*

Grow and Convert recommend plain text contextualized CTAs through the body copy as the alternative to graphical CTAs, and they give one rule that is easy to skip: do not use the same exact CTA for every article. The CTA should make sense contextually inside that post. Their example is an article about invoicing on a software platform where invoicing is one of several features. The CTA on that post should pertain specifically to the invoicing feature, not to the broader platform. The mechanism is intent continuity: the reader arrived with a narrow problem and read a narrow argument, so an offer framed around that same narrow problem is a smaller step than an offer to evaluate the whole product. A single reusable site-wide CTA string breaks that continuity on every post it appears on.

> "Don't use the same exact CTA for every article."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's worked example: an invoicing article on a multi-feature software platform should carry an invoicing-specific CTA, not a platform-level one.

**How to do it**

1. List the features or services your product covers and map each published article to the one feature it argues for.
2. For each article, write a CTA sentence naming that specific feature and the outcome the reader wants from it.
3. Place that sentence as a plain-text link inside the prose, not as a button block.
4. Remove any global CTA snippet that inserts identical wording into every post.
5. Keep the wording consistent with the article's keyword, so the offer uses the same vocabulary the reader searched with.
6. Review new drafts for a feature-specific CTA as part of the editorial checklist, rejecting any that reuse a generic platform pitch.
7. Track conversions per article rather than site-wide so you can see which feature-framed CTAs work.

**Pitfall:** Templating the CTA to save production time. The tell is that every article's CTA reads like the homepage headline, which converts worst on the narrow, high-intent posts that should convert best.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's book-demo block is fixed by the block contract, but the inline text links around it are not. David should write the surrounding sentence to name the feature the article is about, for example a link framed around online booking on a booking article rather than a generic Pabau demo pitch.

**Apply anywhere:** If your CTA block is templated, the inline text around it is not. Write the surrounding sentence to name the specific feature the article is about, rather than repeating a generic product pitch on every post.

### 24. Write benefits to the person reading, not to their company  `159.3`
*core · best practices · source 159*

Grow and Convert call this the single biggest change they made. Their old sales page headline was 'Grow your site traffic and drive qualified leads from content marketing' and the opening sentence read 'This system is designed exclusively for B2B companies...'. Both were written to a company. They had done it deliberately and thought they were smart for it. Interviews showed marketers cared about becoming great content marketers, with lead generation just a tactic along the way. The replacement headline came verbatim from a customer call where someone said they wanted to become 'the Michael Jordan of content marketing', which became 'Become a Top 1% Content Marketer'. The rewritten page focuses on the individual, their career growth and their options. The caveat is that this applies where an individual chooses and champions the purchase.

> "It's all about benefits to a _company_, not a _person_"

**Evidence:** Conversion moved from 0.16% to roughly 1% of the email list on an unchanged product after the headline shifted from company benefits to 'Become a Top 1% Content Marketer'.

**How to do it**

1. Read your hero headline and first sentence aloud and mark every noun that names an organization rather than a human.
2. Identify the individual who actually champions the purchase inside the buying company.
3. Ask on customer calls what that person wants for their own career or workload, not what the company wants.
4. Rewrite the headline using the customer's own phrasing from those calls, keeping the subject a person.
5. Keep company-level outcomes such as revenue or lead volume, but move them below the fold as supporting proof.
6. Say the new headline to a past prospect on a call and keep it only if they react with visible enthusiasm.
7. Check the rest of the page for leftover company-voice sentences and rewrite them to match.

**Pitfall:** Company-voice copy makes readers skim rather than read, and skimming looks like a traffic problem in analytics rather than a copy problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog CTAs and demo pages should speak to the practice manager or clinic owner personally, naming the hours and admin they get back, with revenue framed as supporting proof lower down.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the hero to the individual who champions the purchase, naming what they personally get, and move company-level outcomes below the fold as proof.
