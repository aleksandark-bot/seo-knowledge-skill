### 1. Accept blog posts and side projects in place of a resume  `166.4`
*useful · best practices · source 166*

The author argues that resumes are a bad instrument for marketing hires. They do not show what someone actually did, they are often misleading, and they carry no signal about passion for the role, which he calls the greatest single indicator of success. Engineering hiring already accepts a portfolio of built things, and he sees no reason marketing has not adopted the same. He cites Ryan Hoover's argument that blogging is the new resume, and reports that his own last application was a Word document containing nothing but links to every marketing blog post he had written, which landed him an interview. He notes the honest caveat: that only worked because the company was progressive, and most employers would reject that format. Acceptable substitutes he names are a written blog post, a side project, a site the person built, a Facebook campaign they ran, or a case study.

> "Ryan Hoover made the case that blogging is the new resume"

**Evidence:** The author's own application, a Word document of links to his marketing posts, landed him an interview at a progressive company.

**How to do it**

1. Add a line to every marketing job post saying a portfolio may be submitted instead of a resume.
2. List the accepted formats explicitly: published articles, a site or product they built, a campaign they ran, or a written case study.
3. When a portfolio comes in, read one full piece rather than skimming the list of links.
4. Ask the candidate which parts of the portfolio piece they personally produced and which were edited by someone else.
5. For a side project, ask what it cost them, what it earned or attracted, and what they would change.
6. Keep the resume path open for candidates whose best work was under NDA, and use the challenge question to compensate.
7. Score portfolios against the same rubric as resumes so the two routes stay comparable.

**Pitfall:** Accepting a portfolio and then still ranking candidates by employer brand at the debrief. If the panel keeps referring to where someone worked rather than what they shipped, the portfolio route is decorative.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau hires writers or SEO staff, ask for published URLs and read one piece end to end. For healthcare and aesthetics content, check whether the sample cites external proof for its claims, since Pabau requires that on every article.

**Apply anywhere:** Let marketing candidates apply with published work, a side project or a case study instead of a resume, and read one piece in full rather than scanning the link list.

### 2. Ask your best marketers who else is good, like influencer outreach  `166.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 166*

The article's last hiring suggestion is a sourcing one: ask great marketers whether they know other great marketers. The reasoning is that marketers can tell the good ones from the bad ones, which hiring managers often cannot, and that they run in close circles with people at their own level. The author frames the mechanic as identical to influencer outreach, which is his point about how to run it: it is an outreach campaign with a list, a specific ask and follow-up, not a single Slack message to a friend. This is a cheap pre-filter that inherits the judgment problem the rest of the article is about, since the referrer has already applied the 5-Hows standard to the person they are naming.

> "ask great marketers if they know other great marketers to hire"

**How to do it**

1. List the 15 to 25 marketers whose work you already rate, including people you have hired, worked with and read.
2. Send each one a specific ask naming the role, the seniority and the channels involved, not a generic 'know anyone'.
3. Ask for one or two names rather than an open list, since a bounded ask gets answered.
4. Ask the referrer what specifically they saw the person do, and treat that as the first pass of the 5-Hows.
5. Approach the named person directly rather than asking them to apply through the job posting.
6. Follow up once after a week, exactly as you would in influencer outreach.
7. Log every name given, including ones you do not hire, as the seed list for the next role.

**Pitfall:** A vague 'let me know if you hear of anyone' gets no reply. The referral only works when you name the role, bound the ask to one or two names, and follow up.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content and SEO hires should start from a named referral list rather than a job board, and the same list doubles as an outreach list for guest contributors and expert quotes on pabau.com articles.

**Apply anywhere:** Run referrals as an outreach campaign: list the marketers whose work you rate, ask each for one or two specific names, ask what they saw that person do, and follow up once.

### 3. Benchmark an agency at 25 to 35 page-1 articles in twelve months  `180.7`
*useful · content insights · source 180*

Grow and Convert publish two timing numbers that let you compare an agency against an in-house hire. An in-house content marketer typically takes three to six months just to build a content plan and get production running, before any of that content starts producing results. Against that, they say their own engagements typically have 25 to 35 articles ranking on page 1 for their intended keywords within the first twelve months. Whatever you think of the source, the pair of figures gives you a usable benchmark to ask any agency for: how many articles on page 1, for their intended keywords, at the twelve-month mark. That is a much harder claim to fake than a traffic percentage, because you can check the URLs and the keywords yourself.

> "25 – 35 articles ranking on page 1"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state that in-house content marketers take 3 to 6 months to get running, while their engagements typically have 25 to 35 articles on page 1 of their intended keywords within the first 12 months.

**How to do it**

1. Ask every agency you evaluate how many articles rank on page 1 for their intended keyword at month 12, averaged across clients.
2. Ask for two client domains where you can verify that yourself.
3. Run those domains through a rank tracker and count page-1 URLs published in the last 12 months.
4. Compare against the 25 to 35 figure Grow and Convert claim, and treat a much lower number as the honest baseline.
5. When comparing to an in-house hire, add three to six months of ramp before the first article ships.
6. Set the same target internally: count articles ranking page 1 for their intended keyword, monthly.
7. Report that count instead of total sessions, so a single viral post cannot flatter the program.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Comparing an agency's month-12 results against an in-house hire's month-12 results without adding the hire's three-to-six-month ramp to build a plan and start production.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should track 'articles ranking page 1 for their intended keyword' as a monthly number alongside sessions. It is the metric that shows whether the /SEO pipeline is actually placing pages rather than accumulating impressions.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask any agency how many articles rank on page 1 for their intended keyword at month 12, and verify it on two client domains. Use the same count as your own internal metric instead of raw traffic.

### 4. Budget $50k to $120k+ for a content marketing manager by experience band  `165.4`
*useful · general insights · source 165*

Hyam gives three US salary bands for a content marketing manager based on what he has seen: one to two years of experience at $50k to $60k a year, three to five years at $60k to $90k, and six or more years at $90k to $120k or above. He flags the usual caveats about location, company and industry. The bands are useful less as a payroll number than as a budgeting sanity check before you open the role. A company hoping one $55k hire will own strategy, promotion, freelancer management, SEO and reporting is buying the one-to-two-year band and expecting the six-plus band. Either raise the budget or narrow the role, and plan the freelance writer budget on top, because the job description explicitly makes hiring writers part of the manager's duties.

> "Content Marketing Manager (1-2 years of experience): $50k-$60k per year"

**Evidence:** Hyam's observed US ranges: $50k-$60k at 1-2 years, $60k-$90k at 3-5 years, $90k-$120k+ at 6+ years.

**How to do it**

1. Decide which of the three bands the role's scope actually sits in before writing the ad.
2. Price a scope that includes strategy, promotion, freelancer management and reporting at the $90k+ band, not the entry band.
3. If the budget is $50k to $60k, cut the scope to execution and keep strategy with an existing manager.
4. Add a separate freelance writing budget on top of salary, since the manager is expected to hire writers.
5. Adjust for location and industry, but do not use that as cover for underpricing the full scope.
6. Model the content ROI target against total cost including writers, not salary alone.

**Pitfall:** Buying entry-band salary for senior-band scope. The signal is a first hire who publishes steadily but never runs customer interviews or promotion, because they were never senior enough to own them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau opens a content role, David should price the scope against these bands and add the freelance writer budget separately, so the plan is not an entry-level salary carrying an entire content program.

**Apply anywhere:** Price content marketing manager roles at roughly $50k-$60k for 1-2 years, $60k-$90k for 3-5, and $90k-$120k+ for 6 or more. Match the band to the scope and budget freelance writers on top of salary.

### 6. Build a business twin to route around org resistance to AI  `27.7`
*useful · general insights · source 27*

Getting AI into an established organization is described as far harder than for a solo practitioner, not because of bad faith but because of how large organizations are structured: each department controls its own data and access, so "somebody's job is to say absolutely not, you can't access that" when an AI system asks for information, producing an organizational "immune response" to AI. The workaround described is building "around the edge" — creating a standalone "business twin" system that independently performs a given department's role from outside the existing permission structure, then gradually rebuilding the surrounding systems and eroding that department's function over time, rather than trying to get the AI formally integrated and permissioned within the organization as it exists today. This is framed as a competitive risk for large incumbents specifically: if they can't adapt fast enough, smaller, fast-moving operators building lean agent networks can out-execute them.

> "getting AI into an organization means building around the edge"

**Evidence:** The speaker's direct experience with agency clients getting AI into their organizations, describing legacy-system data silos and departmental gatekeeping as the practical blocker, versus his own solo/small-team experience where hooking up AI to data is comparatively easy.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau or David's own team hits internal resistance getting an AI/SEO agent access to a data source it needs, such as a locked-down analytics or CRM system, consider building a narrower standalone tool that replicates just the needed function from outside that system first, rather than waiting for full internal data access and buy-in.

**Apply anywhere:** If your own team hits internal resistance getting an AI/SEO agent access to a data source it needs, such as a locked-down analytics or CRM system, consider building a narrower standalone tool that replicates just the needed function from outside that system first, rather than waiting for full internal data access and buy-in.

### 7. Choose growth marketing to learn every SEO discipline fastest  `52.8`
*useful · general insights · source 52*

Asked which specific SEO career path, product SEO, growth, or technical SEO, to choose starting out, Edward's recommendation is growth, on the reasoning that pursuing growth forces you to learn all the other disciplines rather than staying narrow: you need Google's spam and policy rules to avoid being penalized, technical SEO to ensure pages are crawled and updates are recognized, UX and quality awareness because pogo-sticking directly damages quality score and rankings over time, and copywriting and marketing skill to actually convert the traffic you earn. He adds that the resulting skill set transfers directly to other channels, social media marketing, email newsletters, and sales, because many other platforms' ranking or engagement algorithms are modeled in various ways on Google's own algorithm, so understanding SEO deeply makes you a measurably better marketer across the board.

> "growth is the best because it forces you to learn them"

**Evidence:** Edward's own first-person claim that he became a much better marketer generally from first understanding SEO and Google's algorithm, since other platforms' algorithms model themselves off of Google's algorithms in various ways, paired with his listed skill requirements for growth: spam rules, technical crawlability, pogo-sticking and quality-score awareness, and copywriting.

**Apply at Pabau:** When advising a junior team member at Pabau on career direction, or planning David's own skill development, growth-oriented SEO work spanning technical health, content, CRO, and distribution should be treated as the highest-leverage generalist path, since it builds transferable marketing judgment applicable beyond organic search, rather than treating narrow specialization, e.g., only technical SEO, as the faster route to broad competence.

**Apply anywhere:** When advising a junior team member on your team on career direction, or planning your own skill development, growth-oriented SEO work spanning technical health, content, CRO, and distribution should be treated as the highest-leverage generalist path, since it builds transferable marketing judgment applicable beyond organic search, rather than treating narrow specialization, e.g., only technical SEO, as the faster route to broad competence.

### 8. Discount degrees and weight self-taught execution in marketing hiring  `166.5`
*useful · best practices · source 166*

The article's position on education is that school is a weak predictor in marketing because the field changes faster than any university curriculum can. The author graduated in 2010, took no classes in online marketing at all, and found that almost every role companies were hiring for that year was an online marketing role. He got up to speed by attending conferences and webinars, reading, and learning from teammates. His stated preference order for candidates is real experience with results first, then a practical program such as TradeCraft, then someone who took online courses and actually tried to execute the tactics from them, and only then someone with an Ivy League degree alone. He singles out companies that filter for Ivy League graduates as the clearest version of the mistake.

> "It blows my mind when companies only want to hire Ivy League graduates"

**Evidence:** The author graduated in 2010 with no online marketing coursework, into a market where most open roles were online marketing roles, and had to self-teach through conferences, webinars and teammates.

**How to do it**

1. Remove any school-tier filter from the applicant screen and stop sorting by university.
2. Rank candidates on evidence in this order: results they drove, practical programs completed, courses they then executed on, degree alone.
3. For any course or bootcamp on a resume, ask what they built or ran afterwards with what they learned.
4. Ask every candidate how they keep up with the field now, and require specific named sources, not 'I read a lot'.
5. Treat a self-taught candidate with a shipped project as equal to or above a degreed candidate with none.
6. Record the reason for each advance decision so school bias does not creep back in at the debrief.

**Pitfall:** Screening on school makes the pipeline look prestigious and perform badly, because theory-only candidates need months of ramp on channels that did not exist when they studied.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should judge content and SEO applicants on published work and on what they can explain about aesthetics-practice buyers, not on their degree. Ask what they have shipped and what they read weekly.

**Apply anywhere:** Drop school-tier filters from marketing hiring and rank on demonstrated execution instead. Ask self-taught candidates what they built after the course, and ask everyone which specific sources keep them current.

### 10. Evaluate conflicting SEO advice against fixed first principles  `52.6`
*useful · best practices · source 52*

Edward's explanation for why SEO experts on his show sometimes contradict each other isn't that SEO itself is in constant flux — it's that some people are simply wrong, or unable to let go of previously held beliefs, echoing a quote he cites from Weblinker, moderator of the SEO subreddit, that critical thinking and doing your own research is the antidote, while finding a truism and locking yourself up is the failure mode. His own editorial practice reflects this: rather than publicly confronting a guest who states something inaccurate, he usually stays silent in the moment and addresses it later in a separate episode, reasoning that this keeps guests comfortable and willing to share more, which in turn gets him better guests and more information for the audience, reserving direct pushback specifically for cases where a guest seems to be promoting something spammy or exploitative to the audience.

> "Critical thinking and doing research is your friend"

**How to do it**

1. When you hear two credible SEO sources give contradictory advice, resist the assumption that both must be equally valid because SEO changes fast.
2. Instead, evaluate each claim against a fixed set of first principles, e.g., Edward's five-part formula of keywords, relevance, authority, reduce pogo-sticking, avoid spam.
3. Do your own supporting research or small-scale test on the specific claim rather than accepting either source's authority at face value.
4. Watch for guests or sources who seem unable to let go of a previously-held belief even when evidence contradicts it, and discount their claims accordingly.
5. Reserve direct, public pushback for claims that look actively spammy or exploitative toward an audience; for merely outdated or debatable claims, note the disagreement privately and address it separately rather than publicly confronting the source (inferred general practice, mirroring Edward's own editorial policy).
6. Periodically revisit your own held beliefs against new evidence, so you don't become the person who is locking themselves up in an outdated truism.

**Pitfall:** Treating every confident, contradictory claim as equally legitimate because "SEO is always changing" lets you get talked into bad advice — the fix is testing claims against fixed first principles rather than assuming the field has no stable ground truth.

### 11. Expect blame to land on the tool instead of on the approach  `175.4`
*useful · general insights · source 175*

The mechanism the article names is psychological rather than technical. When results do not come, it is far easier to blame a bad piece of software you bought than to admit the approach was wrong or that you are not yet good at the job. That is why people cycle through tools, and why they are genuinely confused when a tool that appears to work for others does nothing for them. The consequence the author draws is career-level: if you do not get better at strategy, you do not get better at your job, and no amount of tooling closes that gap. Strategy takes years of mentors, courses, reading, practice and failed tests to build, which is exactly why software feels like the shortcut.

> "it's a lot easier to blame something not working on a shitty tool"

**Pitfall:** Serial tool-switching. The signal is a team that has changed rank trackers or content platforms three times in two years while output and rankings stayed flat.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau SEO experiment fails, David should record the hypothesis and what the approach got wrong in the test log before considering whether tooling was a factor. Tooling goes last in the post-mortem, not first.

**Apply anywhere:** When an SEO experiment fails, record the hypothesis and what the approach got wrong before considering tooling. Put tooling last in the post-mortem, not first.

### 12. Expect creative ideas, not budget or headcount, to drive attention now  `88.7`
*useful · general insights · source 88*

The author predicts a shift he calls a marketing renaissance: the most creative ideas drive the most attention rather than the biggest budgets or the largest teams. He notes creative marketers have long been undervalued next to performance and data-driven marketers, because executing an interesting idea required a designer, a developer or several stakeholders, and the resource cost killed good ideas before they shipped. With AI removing that cost, the winners are the ones creating the most original work, not the ones publishing the most AI-generated volume. For search specifically this reinforces the existing doctrine that irreplaceable value, a tool, a transaction or a service, predicts surviving updates, while scaled generic output does not.

> "the most creative ideas drive the most attention"

**How to do it**

1. Reallocate part of the content budget from article count to one ambitious original asset per quarter.
2. Choose the asset on whether a practitioner would share it unprompted, not on search volume.
3. Prototype it with AI in days so the decision to build is cheap and reversible.
4. Set the success metric as links, mentions and branded search, not sessions.
5. Kill any content line whose only argument is that competitors publish it too.
6. Review quarterly whether budget is still concentrated in volume rather than originality.

**Pitfall:** Teams say they want original work but keep measuring publishing cadence, so the ambitious asset is always deprioritized for the next five articles. The signal is a content calendar with no line item that takes more than a week.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should protect one slot a quarter for an original asset such as an interactive tool or a proprietary data study drawn from aggregate practice usage, separate from the regular blog and template output.

**Apply anywhere:** Protect one slot a quarter for an original asset that no competitor could copy, and judge it on links, mentions and branded search rather than sessions.

### 13. GTM engineering has expanded well past cold outbound  `63.5`
*useful · general insights · source 63*

Useful context for the term. Cody notes that GTM engineering traditionally meant everything involved in cold email and outreach - and that Clay effectively owned the phrase for that role initially. But it has moved quickly past that into everything related to marketing: customer experience, product, paid ads, SEO - anything in the go-to-market process where you're using AI to do the engineering and the build-outs. His characterisation of the role change is the part worth keeping: it's all of the middle work you previously did with hands on a keyboard, handed to the agent, with you as the ideas and the polish at either end. His prediction is unhedged - this is going to affect every person doing any type of growth role, and thinking otherwise is 'getting high on your own supply'.

> "let's just talk about what GTM engineering actually is"

**How to do it**

1. Audit your own week and separate the ideas and judgement from the middle work.
2. List the middle work by tool - each one is a candidate for an API key in the environment file.
3. Start with the task you repeat most often rather than the most impressive one.
4. Keep the judgement calls and the final polish with a person.
5. Expect the scope to widen from outbound into paid, SEO, support and product over time.

**Pitfall:** Treating this as a tooling trend rather than a change in what the role is made of is the framing error - his point is that the ideas and the polish are the job, and the middle is not.

### 14. Hire a content marketer who built an operation from scratch  `160.4`
*useful · best practices · source 160*

Grow and Convert's hiring rule is specific: look for someone who has previously built a content marketing operation from scratch, not just someone who has scaled one. They argue the two skillsets differ. Building from zero means owning strategy, promotion and assembling a team of freelance writers, while scaling means improving a machine that already exists. They also separate the writer role from the marketer role. Writers without the content marketing skillset solve a short-term problem but cannot help you scale long-term, unless you have the resources to train that writer into a marketer. For an early company with one content hire, that argues for the marketer profile over the strongest writer.

> "who has previously built a content marketing operation from scratch"

**How to do it**

1. Write the job description around three duties: strategy, promotion, and building a freelance writer bench.
2. Screen resumes for a blog the candidate started at or near zero, not one they inherited with traffic.
3. Ask the candidate to describe the first ten articles they chose at that blog and why.
4. Ask how they sourced, tested and paid their freelance writers.
5. Ask what promotion they ran beyond publishing, and what it returned.
6. Reject candidates whose only evidence is growth on an established blog, unless you can fund training them into the marketer role.
7. If you can only hire a writer, budget separately for the strategy and promotion work that writer will not do.

**Pitfall:** Hiring a strong writer as the first content hire and expecting a program. You get articles and no strategy or distribution, and growth stalls once the obvious topics are used up.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau adds content headcount, David should weight from-scratch operators over big-brand scalers, and confirm the candidate has run promotion and managed freelancers, not only written posts. Pabau already has an established blog, so the gap the hire fills is strategy and distribution.

**Apply anywhere:** For a first content hire, prefer someone who has built a content operation from zero over someone who scaled an existing one. The from-scratch profile owns strategy, promotion and freelancer management; a writer alone leaves those gaps unfilled.

### 15. Hire one channel specialist and protect them from unrelated work  `177.11`
*useful · best practices · source 177*

The author's exception to the generalist instinct is specific. An early-stage B2B company that needs to dominate one strategy to hit a twelve-month revenue goal can be well served by a marketer with deep expertise in a single channel, and he uses AdWords as the example. That person may have zero visual, graphic or sales-material experience, and forcing that work on them is what he calls a massive waste of a company resource. His broader complaint is that requests like this stereotype every marketer as a competent product marketer, designer and sales assistant at once. The rule that falls out is to define the channel the company is betting on, hire depth in it, and then keep that person's calendar on that channel.

> "a marketer with deep adwords expertise could be a GREAT asset"

**How to do it**

1. Name the single channel that plausibly carries the next twelve months of revenue.
2. Hire for depth in that channel and write the job description around it, not around general marketing.
3. State in the offer what is out of scope: decks, design, rep emails, event support.
4. Give the specialist one measurable channel outcome and review against only that.
5. Route design and product-marketing requests to a contractor or a separate hire from day one.
6. Re-open the channel bet at twelve months rather than quietly widening the role.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Hiring a deep specialist and then handing them general marketing chores wastes the expertise you paid for, and the specialist usually leaves before anyone notices the channel never got built.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau hires for a specific search channel, the scope should say what is out of scope in writing. Otherwise the role drifts toward collateral requests and the channel bet never gets tested.

**Apply anywhere:** When one channel plausibly carries the next twelve months of revenue, hire depth in it and write the out-of-scope work into the offer. Route design and collateral requests elsewhere from day one.

### 16. Offer a referral bonus to marketers instead of paying a recruiter  `165.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 165*

Hyam's argument is simple arithmetic. Marketers know other marketers, so sending your job posting to talented marketers you already know and asking who would fit usually surfaces a few strong names. He suggests sweetening it with a referral bonus, and notes explicitly that the bonus will be far cheaper than a recruiter. That reframes referral bonuses as a recruiting channel rather than an internal HR perk: the bonus is offered to people outside the company as well. It also works alongside the outreach tactic of contacting people already in the role at content-strong companies, since the same message doubles as a referral request. The whole approach depends on you having a job description precise enough that a referrer can pattern-match against it.

> "the referral bonus will be a lot cheaper than hiring a recruiter"

**Evidence:** Hyam recommends a referral bonus as materially cheaper than recruiter fees.

**How to do it**

1. Write the job description first, tight enough that someone outside your company can screen against it.
2. List every marketer you know personally, inside and outside the company.
3. Send them the posting and ask directly whether they know anyone who fits.
4. Attach a named referral bonus, payable to external referrers as well as staff.
5. Set the bonus well below a recruiter fee, which is typically 15 to 25 percent of first-year salary.
6. Pay the bonus on a completed probation period rather than on start date.
7. Track which referrals convert, so the same people get the next role first.

**Pitfall:** A vague job ad makes referrals worthless, because referrers send whoever writes for a living. The signal is a stack of referred writers when you asked for a marketer.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should circulate any Pabau content role to marketing contacts with a stated referral bonus before considering a recruiter, and include the grow-skills scorecard so referrers screen correctly.

**Apply anywhere:** Send your job posting to marketers you know with a named referral bonus attached, open to external referrers. It costs a fraction of a recruiter fee, but only works if the description is specific.

### 17. Read a third-party review profile for completeness before scoring it  `178.12`
*useful · general insights · source 178*

Grow and Convert use Clutch ratings as a comparison axis across the agencies they list, and their handling of the data is the transferable part. They note their own 4.8 out of 5 average, several competitors with no reviews at all at the time of writing, one competitor at 4.9, and Seer Interactive with a single review carrying a half-star rating. On that last one they do not treat the rating as a verdict. They say it is unlikely the Clutch profile is a complete representation of the company. The rule is that review counts govern how much a rating means. A one-review profile and a fifty-review profile are not comparable, and an absent profile says nothing about quality, only about where that firm collects proof. Also worth noting: they disclose plainly that they have not worked with any of the listed agencies.

> "they only have one review with a half star rating"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert explicitly decline to read Seer Interactive's single half-star Clutch review as representative of the company.

**How to do it**

1. Look up each shortlisted agency on the same review platform so the comparison is like for like.
2. Record the review count next to every rating, and treat anything under about five reviews as noise.
3. Do not read an empty profile as a negative; check whether the firm collects proof elsewhere.
4. Read the text of the reviews for the work described, not the star number.

**Apply anywhere:** Always print the review count beside a rating and treat low-volume profiles as unscoreable. An absent profile is not evidence of poor quality, only of where that firm collects proof.

### 18. Reject 'write good content' as advice and hold advisors accountable  `74.21`
*useful · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid attacks the standard answer that SEO is about writing good content. He calls it subjective, noting 99% of sites are irrelevant to 99% of people, and asks whether the person saying it is just assuming everyone's content is bad. It does not stop spam either: spammers are not watching him, they know what they are doing, they have found an effective model with a line of sight from eyeballs to revenue, and that is Google's problem to solve. His arithmetic is the sharpest part. With hundreds of millions of pages in the index, writing good content will not move a page from zero. He also offers a test for the subjectivity claim: look in Search Console at your best non-branded page and check whether its click-through rate is 100%. If it is 1%, most searchers chose someone else. His recommendation is to stop listening to anyone whose answer to 'what is SEO' is write good content.

> "If you're listen if you ask someone what is SEO and they say write good content, move on"

**Evidence:** David's CTR test: if every searcher clicked your result and never searched again your CTR would be 100%, and at 1% it plainly is not the universally best answer.

**How to do it**

1. When you get content advice, ask what the mechanism is and what would change if you did it.
2. Reject any recommendation that cannot name the query it targets or the link it earns.
3. Open GSC, filter to non-branded queries, and look at the click-through rate on your best page.
4. Use that number to end internal arguments about whose content is objectively best.
5. Ask people who criticize your content to say specifically how they would improve it, and act only on the specific parts.
6. Keep a written log of advice taken and what it produced, so advisors can be judged on outcomes.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Rewriting a page again because someone said the content was not good enough. Without a named query, a named competitor and a named gap, the rewrite has no target and the page does not move.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau article underperforms, David should require a named diagnosis before commissioning a rewrite: intent mismatch, cannibalization, missing internal links or missing entities. 'Make it better' is not an actionable brief for the writer.

**Apply anywhere:** Require a named diagnosis, query and gap before commissioning any rewrite, and stop accepting 'write better content' as a recommendation.

### 19. Sequence the work sample: cheap challenge early, full plan late  `166.9`
*useful · best practices · source 166*

The article is explicit about ordering the two exercises it recommends, and the ordering is the actual advice. The short challenge question, such as the $5,000 spending prompt, goes out early in the process to everyone, because it is cheap for the candidate and cheap to score. The full plan, where the candidate outlines how they would approach the role, is reserved for candidates far along in the process. The author only reaches for exercises at all when the candidate has no portfolio to show: if they already have blog posts, a side project or a case study, that evidence substitutes for the exercise. > "But only do this for candidates that are"

**How to do it**

1. Decide at screening whether the candidate has usable published work; if they do, skip the early exercise and read the work instead.

4. Advance shortlisted candidates to interview and run the 5-Hows chain there.

**Apply at Pabau:** For a Pabau content hire, the late-stage plan should ask which existing pabau.com pages they would refresh first and why, since refreshing declining pages is the higher-return work.

**Apply anywhere:** Use two work samples at two stages: a short challenge everyone answers at screening, and a role plan only for finalists. Skip the early exercise for candidates who already have publishable work to show.

### 25. Treat team-level goals as the source of internal politics and kill them early  `177.10`
*useful · best practices · source 177*

The author defines company politics precisely: employees caring more about themselves, their team, or their power than about the success of the organization overall. He traces it to a structural cause rather than to personalities. At one employer, goals were set on team performance instead of company performance, and the predictable consequence was blame shuttling between sales and marketing until the company was hurt by it. His instruction to founders is unambiguous: if you see this happening, squash it immediately. The useful part for anyone running a function is the diagnosis order. Before treating a sales-marketing conflict as a relationship problem to be solved with more meetings, check whether the incentive structure is paying people to have it.

> "goals were based on team performance instead of company performance"

**Evidence:** The author reports that at a company with team-based goals, blame passed from sales to marketing and back, and the company was hurt by it.

**How to do it**

1. Write out what each team is actually measured and paid on, in one table.
2. Look for any case where one team can hit its number while the company misses; that is the fault line.
3. Change the incentive before adding meetings, because meetings do not outvote compensation.
4. Name the behavior out loud the first time you see blame-shifting, rather than letting it settle as culture.
5. Ask leadership to state publicly that no team goal counts unless the company goal is met.
6. Re-check the table each time a new function or target is added.

**Pitfall:** Treating the conflict as a personality clash and prescribing more joint meetings. The tell is that the same argument recurs each quarter with different people in it.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau ever measures content on published volume while sales is measured on closed revenue, the same blame loop appears. David should check the incentive table before proposing process fixes.

**Apply anywhere:** Before treating a sales and marketing conflict as a relationship problem, write out what each team is paid on. If one team can hit its number while the company misses, fix the incentive rather than adding meetings.
