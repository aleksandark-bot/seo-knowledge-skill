### 1. Accept 20-plus monthly searches in B2B, demand four figures in B2C  `129.3`
*core · best practices · source 129*

Grow and Convert give explicit volume floors that differ by business model. For B2B, they are comfortable if the keyword set shows terms with 20 or more monthly searches and at least a handful above 100. They acknowledge this looks low, and defend it: a keyword with a reported volume of 50 a month typically delivers at least 50 organic pageviews a month once you rank, often far more. For B2C or lower-priced ecommerce, where more traffic is needed to recoup the same SEO cost, they want many keywords with three- and even four-figure monthly volume, and note this is rarely a problem because consumer terms simply carry more volume than business terms. The floor also slides with price. A high-priced B2B SaaS with customers paying thousands a month for eighteen months may need only five or ten new customers a month to break even, so it can justify far thinner volume.

> "for B2B companies, we are comfortable if we see keywords with 20+"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert accept 20-plus monthly searches for B2B with a handful over 100, and say a 50-a-month keyword typically returns at least 50 pageviews a month; a high-ACV SaaS may need only 5 to 10 customers a month to break even.

**How to do it**

1. Decide which model you are before setting a floor: B2B with a high annual deal value, or B2C and lower-priced ecommerce.
2. For B2B, require terms at 20-plus monthly searches and at least a handful above 100 across the target list.
3. For B2C or ecommerce, require many terms in the three- and four-figure monthly range.
4. Compute how many customers a month you need to cover the SEO spend, using annual deal value and close rate.
5. Slide the volume floor down when that customer count is five or ten, and up when it is hundreds.
6. Do not discard a 20-a-month keyword with clear purchase intent — that band is where B2B revenue sits.
7. Re-derive the floor whenever pricing or the SEO budget changes.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Importing a consumer-scale volume floor into a B2B program. Filtering out everything under 100 or 500 searches a month deletes most of the keywords that actually convert, and leaves a list of high-volume terms whose searchers cannot buy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sells to practices, so use the B2B floor. Stop rejecting clinic-workflow and code-lookup keywords for low volume; a 30-a-month query typed by a clinic owner evaluating software is worth more than a 5,000-a-month consumer treatment query.

**Apply anywhere:** Set your volume floor from your business model, not a habit. In B2B, accept keywords at 20-plus monthly searches with a handful above 100. In B2C or low-priced ecommerce, insist on many three- and four-figure terms. Then slide the floor against how many customers a month you need to cover the spend.

### 2. Accept lower buying intent from JTBD for a much larger topic pool  `93.11`
*core · content insights · source 93*

Goolding places JTBD keywords mid-funnel, above category keywords and comparison or alternative keywords on buying intent but below both. The trade he names is explicit: JTBD terms do not have the extreme buying intent of the first two frameworks, but they unlock a much greater number of keywords and topics, and they still convert because the searcher has named a specific pain point you solve. His sequencing rule follows from that. Work the category and comparison terms first because they are the highest converting and the pool is small. Move to JTBD once those are mopped up, and to JTBD combined with competitors and alternatives after that. He says JTBD opens years of content ideas with high buying intent, which is what makes it the framework that keeps a pain-point program running after the obvious pages are built.

> "they don't have the extreme buying intent of the first two frameworks"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's funnel ordering: top of funnel, then JTBD, then category, then comparison and alternatives, running from low to high buying intent.

**Pitfall:** Starting with JTBD because the volume is bigger means the highest-converting category and alternatives pages get built last, and the program's conversion rate looks weak for the first year.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should finish the category and competitor-alternative pages for practice management before scaling how-to articles, then use JTBD as the ongoing topic engine once those exist.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your category and alternatives pages first, then use jobs-to-be-done keywords as the engine that keeps producing high-intent topics once the small high-intent pool runs out.

### 3. Accept that core site pages cap out at a handful of keywords  `163.12`
*core · best practices · source 163*

Grow and Convert build their whole hiring argument on one structural fact: the core pages of a marketing website, the homepage and the product or service pages, can only rank for a handful of keywords. To rank for more valuable terms you have to produce new, unique content. They combine this with the observation that most businesses have dozens of high-buying-intent keywords available, which no homepage or small set of landing pages can cover. The consequence for hiring is that any plan whose ranking story rests on optimizing existing pages is capped before it starts. The consequence for strategy is that blog content should not be relegated to top-of-funnel traffic building. Each buying-intent variation deserves its own dedicated page, built to rank and to convert.

> "can only rank for a handful of keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated position from years of agency work, backing their claim that most businesses have dozens of high-buying-intent keywords available.

**How to do it**

1. List every high-buying-intent keyword for your business, including comparison, alternative, pricing and use-case variations.
2. Map which of them your homepage and product pages currently rank for, using Search Console query data per URL.
3. Accept a cap of roughly one primary plus a few close variants per existing core page.
4. For every remaining keyword, plan a dedicated new page rather than another section on an existing one.
5. Write those pages to convert, not just to inform, since they carry buying intent.
6. Stop treating the blog as a top-of-funnel traffic bucket and put the commercial variations there.
7. Re-check the map quarterly and merge any two pages that ended up chasing the same query.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Stuffing more keyword variations into the homepage or one service page. The page dilutes, ranks for none of them well, and cannibalizes the dedicated pages you build later.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's buying-intent variations, such as software comparisons, alternatives and per-treatment use cases, each need their own page. Adding them as sections on existing product pages caps what pabau.com can rank for.

**Apply anywhere:** Give each buying-intent variation, such as comparisons, alternatives and per-use-case terms, its own page. Adding them as sections to existing product pages caps what you can rank for.

### 4. Add a capability qualifier to head terms your product only half fits  `135.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 135*

Grow and Convert's client sold text-based video editing, so the head term 'video editor' brought searchers expecting Final Cut Pro or Adobe Premiere Pro. Rather than abandon the category, they added a layer of specificity that named a capability the product genuinely had. 'Video editing software' became 'online video editor' and 'collaborative video editor'. 'Video text editing' was the closest term to the actual innovation. Each qualifier gave them something real to sell inside the post: cloud storage, sharing, team collaboration. This differs from adding a vertical or customer-segment qualifier. The qualifier here is a feature the product ships and competitors do not, so the post can pitch the differentiator without the searcher feeling misled. They also checked which of these terms competitors already ranked for, treating that as proof the term had commercial pull.

> "add a layer of specificity that ties back to our product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report the specific bottom-of-funnel keywords averaged a 2.7% conversion rate, with 'video logging software' at 3.7% and 'online video editor' at 1.85%.

**How to do it**

1. List the head category terms your product is adjacent to but does not fully match, such as 'video editing software'.
2. Write out every capability that separates your product from the category default: online, collaborative, no-experience-needed, text-based.
3. Combine each head term with each capability and check volume for the combinations in Ahrefs or Semrush.
4. Discard combinations naming a capability you do not actually ship, because the post will not convert.
5. Check which surviving terms your named competitors already rank for and prioritize those.
6. Rank the remainder by how directly the qualifier maps to the thing your product does better.
7. Give each surviving term its own post that sells that specific capability, not a general category overview.
8. Track conversion rate per term and keep expanding the qualifiers that convert above your site average.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google

**Pitfall:** Adding a qualifier the product does not really support. Traffic arrives expecting the feature, finds a general product page, and bounces; you see rankings with near-zero trial signups.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not fight for 'practice management software' alone. Build pages for the qualifiers Pabau actually ships: cloud-based practice management software, practice management software with online booking, HIPAA-compliant practice management software, and mobile practice management via Pabau GO, our iOS app.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not fight the bare category head term when your product is only a partial fit. Combine it with capabilities you genuinely ship, verify the combinations have volume and that competitors rank for them, and give each one a dedicated page.

### 5. Add a mini-volume tier under 20 searches to every keyword list  `95.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 95*

Matt Goolding of Grow and Convert defines mini-volume keywords as anything showing under 20 estimated monthly searches in Ahrefs, and argues they belong on the list rather than in the discard pile. Across two years and 17 clients, articles targeting sub-20 volume produced more than 1,600 conversions directly attributed to their content. He is explicit that these are not the first thing you write. They come into play once the higher-volume high-intent keywords are used up, or when the popular terms are impossibly competitive. The ideal combination stays high conversion intent plus high volume plus low competition. But once that pool is exhausted, mini-volume terms with real buying intent beat writing another top-of-funnel piece, and they are quicker to rank for because competitors ignore them.

> "defined as anything with less than 20 estimated searches per month"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: sub-20 volume articles generated more than 1,600 conversions across 17 clients over two years.

**How to do it**

1. Build the bottom-of-funnel keyword list first without applying any volume filter in Ahrefs.
2. Work through the terms with 20-plus volume and low competition first; those are still the priority.
3. Tag every remaining candidate under 20 estimated monthly searches as a mini-volume tier rather than deleting it.
4. Keep only mini-volume terms where the searcher is choosing a product: comparisons, alternatives, use cases, narrow how-tos.
5. Order the mini-volume tier by how exactly the term describes what your product does.
6. Assign one article per surviving term and publish them in order.
7. Record conversions per article by target keyword so the tier can be defended with your own numbers.
8. Revisit the tier every quarter, since terms that were sub-20 often show real volume a year later.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Teams treat mini-volume as a synonym for long tail and fill the tier with low-volume informational queries. A sub-20 term with no buying intent converts like any other top-of-funnel post, which is to say barely.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's keyword sheets should carry a sub-20 tier instead of cutting at 100 searches. Terms like 'aesthetic clinic software for injectables only' or specific competitor alternatives sit there, and they are exactly the pages a buyer reads before booking a demo.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep a tier for keywords under 20 monthly searches in your list instead of filtering them out. Work the higher-volume high-intent terms first, then publish against the sub-20 terms that describe a real buying decision.

### 6. Add a problem modifier to the product-type term: 'best X for Y'  `138.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 138*

After exhausting the 'best X tea' family, Cup & Leaf's next tier was 'best tea for X', where X is a problem the reader wants tea to solve: weight loss, sleep, upset stomach, a cough, a cold, stress. Nat Eliason calls these perfect examples of pain point SEO, someone with a specific problem looking for a product that solves it. He reports they are much more qualified than general searches like 'green tea benefits' or 'green tea diet', and that both click-throughs to the store and conversions were noticeably better. Notice the direction of travel: product type first, because those terms are easier to enumerate and prove the mechanism, then problem modifiers, which are more numerous and more qualified but harder to find. The problem list comes from customer language, not from a keyword tool's related terms.

> "These were issues people were having that they wanted to"

**Evidence:** Cup & Leaf's 'best tea for X' posts, covering weight loss, sleep, upset stomach, cough, cold and stress, showed much better store click-throughs and conversions than general terms like 'green tea benefits'.

**How to do it**

1. Write out every problem a customer might be trying to solve with your product category, sourced from support tickets, reviews and on-site search.
2. Turn each into the shape 'best [category] for [problem]', for example 'best tea for sleep'.
3. Search each term and confirm the SERP is dominated by roundups, not by medical or purely informational results.
4. Drop any variant where the searcher needs a professional rather than a product.
5. Rank the survivors by how well your catalogue actually addresses the problem, not by search volume.
6. Publish one post per surviving problem, and name the specific products that address it inside the post.
7. Compare click-through-to-store rate on these posts against your generic 'benefits' or 'diet' style posts to confirm the qualification gain.
8. Feed the problems that recur most often into product development, not just into content.

**Pitfall:** Sourcing the problem list from a keyword tool's related terms gives you the generic 'benefits' and 'diet' phrasings Eliason explicitly names as worse converting. The problems have to come from customer language.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's equivalent is 'best practice management software for [problem]': for no-show reduction, for consent form compliance, for multi-location scheduling. Build these after the vertical pages, and source the problem list from Pabau's support tickets.

**Apply anywhere:** After the product-type roundups, publish 'best [category] for [problem]' pages. Source the problems from support tickets and reviews rather than a keyword tool, because tool suggestions skew toward generic benefits phrasing that converts worse.

### 7. Add a vertical qualifier to every head BOFU term when your DA is under 40  `81.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 81*

Grow and Convert call this Underdog SEO. Constitution Lending had domain authority under 40 and was competing with lenders at 80+ DA with thousands of backlinks and decades of history. Rather than chase 'best hard money lenders', they prioritized keywords tied to a specific use case or vertical: 'best hard money lenders for first-time investors', 'DSCR lenders for multifamily homes', 'private money lenders for residential real estate', 'DSCR loans for construction', 'commercial loans for rental properties', 'DSCR loans for wholesalers and brokers'. Larger competitors do not target these, so the SERP is filled with broad pages that happen to rank. Because traditional rankings are what feed AI answers, the faster route onto an LLM's radar is the qualified long tail, not the head term. They kept the head terms on the list, just not first.

> "Underdog SEO focuses on prioritizing keywords tied to specific use cases"

**Evidence:** Constitution Lending, DA under 40, reached top three AI recommendations for 50+ bottom-of-funnel prompts using this prioritization.

**How to do it**

1. List the head bottom-of-funnel terms for your category first, then set them aside as phase two.
2. For each head term, generate qualifier variants across three axes: customer segment, asset or product sub-type, and geography.
3. Search each variant and read the top 10 titles to see whether any page is actually built for the qualifier.
4. Keep variants where at most one result is genuinely tailored and the rest are broad pages ranking by default.
5. Check the domain authority of the pages that do rank, and drop variants where every result is an 80+ DA incumbent with tailored content.
6. Order the surviving list by closeness to your differentiators rather than by search volume.
7. Publish one dedicated page per qualified variant, written only about that segment.
8. Once several qualified pages rank, revisit the head terms with the internal links and authority those pages built.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Teams add a qualifier to the title but write the same broad article underneath. The SERP already has broad pages ranking for that qualifier, so a broad page with a narrow title wins nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should stop leading with head terms like 'clinic management software' and build pages for qualified verticals: practice management software for medical spas, for dermatology clinics, for multi-location aesthetic groups, for injectable-only practices. Each gets its own page, not a section in a general article.

**Apply anywhere:** If your domain is weaker than the incumbents on a term, do not target the head term first. Add a customer-segment, product-sub-type or geographic qualifier, confirm from the SERP that no one has built a page for that qualifier, and publish a page written only about that segment.

### 8. Admit a top-funnel keyword only if it has a direct feature tie-in  `130.7`
*core · best practices · source 130*

Grow and Convert do eventually write top and mid-funnel pain point pieces on long engagements, especially once the highest-intent terms are used up. But they gate the choice with two tests. First, the keyword must have a direct feature tie-in, meaning the product has a specific feature that addresses the topic. Second, the piece must incorporate and sell the product throughout, not just at the end. They still do not prioritize these over bottom-of-funnel work, particularly early in an engagement. This is a stricter filter than the usual advice to build topical authority: a top-funnel topic with no feature to attach to is rejected outright, however good the volume looks.

> "ensure the keyword has a direct feature tie-in"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's top-funnel industry pricing guide ranked at position 1 and produced 150+ demo signups to date, because pricing tied directly to the client's pricing and invoicing features.

**How to do it**

1. Only open the top-funnel list once the bottom-funnel keywords for your priority verticals are written.
2. For each candidate top-funnel keyword, name the specific product feature that addresses the topic.
3. Reject any candidate where you cannot name one feature; volume does not rescue it.
4. Check that the pain point behind the topic is one your customers actually raise, from sales calls or support tickets.
5. Write the piece so the product appears in the body of the argument, not only in a closing CTA.
6. Include the feature's benefits and differentiators, not just a mention of the brand name.
7. Measure demo requests per top-funnel piece and stop the format if it underperforms the bottom-funnel average by a wide margin.

**Pitfall:** Approving top-funnel keywords on volume alone gives you traffic with no product hook, which is the low-to-zero attributable conversion pattern Grow and Convert name as flaw three of the standard approach.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's top-funnel blog ideas should each name the Pabau feature they attach to before the brief is approved. An article on, say, patient retention has to tie to the recall and marketing features, or it does not get written.

**Apply anywhere:** Before approving a top-funnel keyword, name the specific product feature it ties to. If you cannot, drop it regardless of volume. And write the product into the body of the piece rather than saving it for the closing CTA.

### 9. Ask every new email subscriber their single biggest challenge  `114.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 114*

Grow and Convert run an autoresponder that asks anyone who joins the email list what their number one marketing challenge is right now. Over three years they collected between 500 and 1,000 responses describing the problems people face in their roles. From two sample emails alone they pulled five content topics: how to generate more traffic from content marketing, how to get more leads from content, how to build a content marketing operation from scratch, how to build an audience from scratch, and how to generate awareness for a product. The load-bearing detail is that they reply personally to every single person who writes back. They say people are often surprised to get a reply because most blogs never interact with readers, and that reply is what keeps the response rate high enough for the loop to keep producing ideas. The cost is almost nothing and the input is continuous.

> "what their #1 marketing challenge is at this moment"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert collected 500 to 1,000-plus responses over three years and named five content topics from just two of them.

**How to do it**

1. Add a single-question autoresponder as the first email in your welcome sequence.
2. Ask one open question: what is your biggest challenge right now, in this moment.
3. Send it from a real person's address that accepts replies, not a no-reply address.
4. Reply personally to every response, however short, since the reply rate is what sustains the loop.
5. Paste each reply into one running document, tagged by role and company type.
6. Review the document monthly and cluster replies into recurring problems.
7. Turn each cluster with three or more independent mentions into a content idea.
8. Match each idea to a keyword and check the SERP intent before committing.
9. Keep the raw wording of the replies and reuse it in the article's opening lines.

**Pitfall:** Sending the question from a no-reply address, or collecting answers and never responding, kills the response rate within weeks. The signal is a survey autoresponder with a reply rate that decays toward nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should add this question to the newsletter and demo-request follow-up sequences, asking practice owners what their biggest operational challenge is this month. The replies become the topic queue for /blog/ and reveal which features to write pages about.

**Apply anywhere:** Put a one-question autoresponder at the front of your welcome sequence asking for the subscriber's biggest current challenge. Reply to everyone by hand, log the answers in one document, and mine it monthly for topics.

### 10. Ask in user research which other tools the buyer is evaluating  `136.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 136*

Grow and Convert say the two accidental pain point posts at Leadfeeder were not luck alone. Their research process always includes one question of customers: what other options, tools or companies are you evaluating or considering. The answers named specific competitors and specific product categories, and those answers turned directly into the comparison post and the category list post that outconverted everything else on the blog. They had tested the same question with an earlier client, seen the resulting posts convert unusually well, and repeated the test at Leadfeeder. The mechanism is simple: a customer telling you who else they considered is telling you the exact comparison query a buyer runs before purchase, including queries no keyword tool will show volume for.

> "What other options/tools/companies are you evaluating or considering?"

**Evidence:** This question produced the comparison and category-list posts that converted around 10x better than Leadfeeder's story content, and had already produced unusually high-converting posts for a previous Grow and Convert client.

**How to do it**

1. Add the question 'what other options, tools or companies are you evaluating or considering' to every customer and prospect interview script.
2. Ask it of recent buyers, of trial users who did not convert, and of sales-call recordings if interviews are slow to book.
3. Log every named company and every named category phrase verbatim in a sheet, with a count of how often each is mentioned.
4. Turn each named company into a 'competitor vs you' comparison post and an 'alternatives to competitor' post.
5. Turn each named category phrase into a category tools list post using the buyer's own wording.
6. Do not filter the list by search volume; publish for named competitors even at zero reported volume.
7. Feed the same list to the sales team so the pages answer the objections they hear.
8. Re-run the question quarterly, since the competitive set named by buyers shifts faster than the keyword tools update.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What other options/tools/companies are you evaluating or considering?
```

**Pitfall:** Interview scripts that only cover pain points and goals miss the evaluation set, so the highest-converting page types never get commissioned. If your comparison backlog was built from a keyword tool rather than from customer answers, you are targeting the competitors Google knows about, not the ones your buyers weigh.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should add this question to every onboarding and churn conversation with aesthetic and healthcare practices, then build one comparison page and one alternatives page per competitor practices actually name. Those names, not Ahrefs, should decide which competitor pages exist.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask customers which other tools they evaluated, log every name verbatim, and turn each into a comparison page and an alternatives page. Buyers name the competitive set more accurately than any keyword tool, including for queries with no reported volume.

### 11. Ask what customers would use instead to find the real competitor  `161.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 161*

Question three asks what the customer would use as an alternative if your product vanished. Khanal warns the answer is usually not who you think. When Sujan Patel ran growth at When I Work, an employee scheduling SaaS, the research found the biggest competitor was not another scheduling platform. It was an Excel spreadsheet. They built an onboarding program specifically to move people off Excel and into the product. Then they went further and used Excel scheduling templates as a lead magnet, collecting email addresses from people searching for the manual method they were already using. The lesson for content is that the real alternative names your best-performing keyword and your best-performing free asset at the same time.

> "the largest competition was actually using an Excel spreadsheet"

**Evidence:** When I Work found Excel spreadsheets were the biggest competitor, built an Excel-to-product onboarding program, and used Excel scheduling templates as an email lead magnet.

**How to do it**

1. Ask every customer what they would use instead if your product disappeared tomorrow.
2. Log answers verbatim and separate them into named software, manual methods and doing nothing.
3. Count how often the manual method appears; if it beats named software, it is your primary competitor.
4. Build a free template or tool that does the manual method, and gate it behind an email address.
5. Publish a page targeting the manual-method query, using the customer's own wording for it.
6. Add a section to that page explaining what breaks when the manual method scales.
7. Build an onboarding path that imports from the manual method, so switching costs drop to near zero.
8. Track how many leads come from the template page and how many convert, and compare against your competitor comparison pages.

**Tools:** Survey Monkey

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What would you likely use as an alternative to (Product or Company Name) if it were no longer available?
```

**Pitfall:** Marketers filter the answers down to recognized SaaS competitors because those are the ones a keyword tool has volume for. That throws away the manual-method answer, which is usually the largest and least contested segment.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's real competitor for many aesthetic practices is a paper diary, a Google Sheet or a WhatsApp group, not another software vendor. That argues for /templates/ pages built around those manual methods and an import path that moves a spreadsheet into Pabau in one step.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask what customers would use instead of you, and if the answer is a spreadsheet or a paper process, build the free template for it and an import path that replaces it.

### 12. Audit page-level GSC data to fix title/query mismatches  `21.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 21*

David demonstrates diagnosing an underperforming page by opening it individually in GSC over a 3-month window without a keyword filter: his 'PAA authority' page ranks occasionally but only ever for one unique phrase at a time, getting clicks from many one-off query variants that never repeat - a sign its title/slug doesn't line up with any real, coherent query bucket. The page was titled around a '5-minute hack' angle while the actual live queries clustered around 'PAA SEO' and 'what is PAA in SEO.' His fix is to use a keyword-difficulty tool to pick the correct query bucket, then rename the document/title/slug to match that bucket exactly so relevance approaches 100%, and republish while de-indexing the old version so authority consolidates instead of splitting.

> "there's something wrong with how it's titled, how it's targeted"

**How to do it**

1. Open each important page individually in GSC's Performance report (Pages filter) and view its query list over a 3-month window with no keyword filter applied.
2. Check the highest position the page has ever reached for any query - a ceiling that never breaks past roughly position 8-9 signals an authority problem rather than a content problem.
3. Check whether the page's clicks come from many unique, non-repeating query phrases rather than consistent volume on a coherent set of related terms - that pattern signals a title/targeting mismatch.
4. Compare the page's actual title and URL slug against the real query phrasing Google is testing it against.
5. Use a keyword-research tool's keyword-difficulty score (ideally personalized to your domain's authority) to identify which candidate query bucket is realistically winnable.
6. Rewrite the page's title, slug, and document name to match the chosen query bucket exactly so topical relevance approaches 100%.
7. Republish under the corrected title/slug and de-index or redirect the old version so authority consolidates onto the corrected page.
8. Recheck the page's average position and click-through rate for the target query a few weeks after republishing to confirm improvement. (inferred)

**Tools:** Google Search Console, SEMrush (keyword difficulty score)

**Pitfall:** A page can look healthy at the site level while quietly underperforming individually - scattered clicks from many unique, non-repeating query phrases (instead of consistent volume on one keyword bucket) signal the title/slug doesn't match any real query Google is trying to rank it for, and no amount of added domain authority will fix that until the targeting itself is corrected.

### 13. Audit your Ahrefs top rankings for product relevance, not volume  `110.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 110*

Grow and Convert audit Pipedrive as a worked example of a blog that ranks well and sells nothing. Pipedrive ranks #1 for 'sales tactics', 'sales presentation', 'challenger sales model' and 'sales meeting agenda'. None of those topics have anything to do with a sales CRM and pipeline management. Meanwhile Pipedrive does not rank for 'salesforce alternatives', 'pipedrive alternatives', 'automatic call logging' or 'call logging software', a feature they show in their own homepage video. Their competitors do rank for those. The audit they run is a two-part test on every ranking page: does the topic describe something the product actually does, and does searching it indicate the person needs the product now. If the answer is no, the ranking has no business value however high the volume.

> "The topic of the article itself is doing you no favors"

**Evidence:** Pipedrive ranks #1 for four top-of-funnel sales terms while ranking for none of 'salesforce alternatives', 'pipedrive alternatives', 'automatic call logging' or 'call logging software', and bids on many of those in Google Ads.

**How to do it**

1. Pull your site's top organic rankings in Ahrefs Site Explorer, sorted by traffic.
2. For each ranking keyword, write down the one product capability it relates to; if you cannot name one, mark the page as no-intent.
3. Ask the second question on each keyword: does searching this indicate the person needs the product right now?
4. List your product's core features from your homepage and pricing page, then search each feature name plus 'software' and record where you rank.
5. Add competitor-alternative terms: 'yourbrand alternatives' and each named competitor plus 'alternatives'.
6. Run the same Ahrefs check on two direct competitors and flag the high-intent terms they rank for and you do not.
7. Cross-check your Google Ads keyword list; terms you pay for but do not rank for organically go to the top of the content queue.
8. Rank the resulting gap list ahead of every top-of-funnel idea currently in the calendar.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Teams see a #1 ranking and stop auditing. The signal you have the Pipedrive problem is high blog traffic with a blog-to-signup rate under 0.1 percent, and paid search buying the exact terms your blog ignores.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this audit on pabau.com's ranking set. ### 24. Choose paid keywords on intent, never on volume or low CPC  `156.2`
*core · best practices · source 156*

Grow and Convert argue the deciding factor for adding a keyword to a paid account should be buying intent, not search volume and not a low cost per click. They list the three bad reasons they see: wanting higher volume keywords to spend the monthly budget, chasing low CPC keywords for 'better costs', and adding broad match because it is cheap. They are explicit that low CPC and high volume terms can stay in the account, they just cannot be the reason for adding them. 4. Pause the flagged keywords rather than lowering their bids.
5. Rebuild the shortfall in spend with longer, higher-intent terms even where CPC is higher.
6. Set a rule that new keywords need a one-line justification of the buyer intent before they are added.
7. Review cost per qualified lead, not cost per click, when judging whether the swap worked.

**Tools:** Google Ads

### 25. Compare page 1 for a qualified term against the head term before dismissing it  `95.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 95*

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: a 0 to 10 volume qualified keyword reached first place in a couple of days and converts at 6 percent.

**How to do it**

1. Take a head term you already target and list its realistic qualifiers: business size, industry, integration, geography.
2. Search the head term and each qualified variant, and record the top 10 URLs for each.
3. Calculate overlap between the head term's top 10 and the variant's top 10.
4. Where overlap is low, treat the variant as a separate intent that needs its own page, whatever the volume reads.
5. Where overlap is above roughly 70 percent, skip the variant and cover it as a section on the existing page.
6. Check whether any ranking result is actually built for the qualifier; if all are broad pages, expect a fast win.
7. Write the page about that qualified buyer only, not a broad article with a narrow title.
8. Track its conversion rate separately, since qualified pages convert well above blog average.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should run this SERP-overlap check before writing qualified variants such as software for solo practitioners versus multi-location groups. Where page 1 differs, build the separate page; where it matches, add a section to the existing article and avoid cannibalizing it.
