### 1. A navbar CTA is enough on a genuine bottom-funnel post  `113.11`
*core · content insights · source 113*

Grow and Convert take the opposite line to most CRO advice. Once a reader arrives from a high buying intent query and reads a page that explains how your product works and how it differs, they say most modern blog layouts already have plenty of call-to-action buttons to click. They show a collage of real client bottom-of-funnel blog posts across different industries, all with unmissable CTAs in the navbar, and note the logo linking home plus the other navigation links are reasonable routes for a reader to explore and request a demo. Their conclusion is that the reader has either been convinced by the content or has not, and if they have, the navigation is right there. This directly contradicts the CTA-count frameworks elsewhere in this base, such as requiring two CTAs above the fold and three at the page bottom.

> "have unmissable CTAs in the navbar"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show a collage of live client bottom-of-funnel blog layouts across several verticals, each with a prominent navbar CTA.

**Pitfall:** The claim only holds when the traffic is genuinely bottom-funnel. On top-funnel traffic the navbar CTA does nothing, which is how teams conclude they need more CTAs when they actually need better keywords.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before adding more CTA blocks to Pabau posts, David should check whether the post is genuinely bottom-funnel. On a comparison or alternatives page the navbar demo button plus the standard book-demo block is likely sufficient, and effort belongs in the product argument.

**Apply anywhere:** Before adding CTA blocks, check whether the post is genuinely bottom-funnel. If it is, a prominent navbar CTA plus the standard in-content CTA is usually enough, and the effort belongs in the product argument.

*core · general insights · source 26*

He frames this as evidence that being cited by AI systems (AEO/GEO) functions as 'a 24/7 sales machine' once you're the brand being surfaced, since the traffic that does convert is now higher-intent.

> "we're down about 30% on volume of traffic and clicks"

**Evidence:** "we're down about 30% on volume of traffic and clicks, the leads are about the same, but we're way up on sales. It's unbelievable."

**Apply at Pabau:** When reporting Pabau's own organic traffic decline to stakeholders, don't treat a falling click count alone as a failure signal — pair it with lead-quality and close-rate metrics from the CRM, since Dooley's data suggests users still clicking through post-AI-Overview convert at a meaningfully higher rate, changing how 'success' should be measured for pages losing clicks to AI Overviews.

**Apply anywhere:** When reporting your own organic traffic decline to stakeholders, don't treat a falling click count alone as a failure signal — pair it with lead-quality and close-rate metrics from the CRM, since Dooley's data suggests users still clicking through post-AI-Overview convert at a meaningfully higher rate, changing how 'success' should be measured for pages losing clicks to AI Overviews.

### 3. Align keyword, ad text and landing page as one three-tier match  `158.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 158*

Grow and Convert describe the fix as three-tier alignment: keyword to ad text to landing page. They rarely see it. The usual pattern is ad copy loosely related to the search term that never contains any part of it, and a landing page related even more indirectly. On their client's account an ad for 'magento web design agency' opened with 'Magento website design team', which is close, but the keyword appeared in neither the ad title nor any sitelink. The landing page behind it was titled 'UX Review and Design', which does not match the keyword at all and in their experience drives higher bounce rates. Their fix has three parts: put at least part of the search term in the ad copy, choose a landing page that satisfies the intent behind the query, and cut the number of keywords per ad group so both are possible.

> "This three tiered alignment (keyword → ad text → landing page)"

**Evidence:** Client ad for 'magento web design agency' pointed at a page titled 'UX Review and Design'.

**How to do it**

1. Pick one ad group and write its primary keyword at the top of a sheet.
2. Check whether that exact keyword appears in the ad headline, not just the description line.
3. Rewrite the headline so the keyword is in it, and put a variant into at least one sitelink.
4. Open the landing page the ad points to and check the page title contains the keyword.
5. If it does not, either rewrite the page around that keyword or build a new page for it.
6. Confirm the page answers the specific questions behind the query, not just the general category.
7. Repeat per ad group, and if you cannot do all of this for every keyword in the group, the group has too many keywords.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Teams satisfy this by adding the keyword to an existing page's title while leaving the body generic. The bounce rate does not move, because the page still does not answer the query.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's paid landing pages must be built per keyword cluster. A 'medspa software' ad should not land on the generic product page, and the landing page title should carry the same phrase the ad headline uses.

**Apply anywhere:** Make the keyword, the ad headline and the landing page title carry the same phrase, and make the page answer the specific question behind that phrase rather than the broad category.

### 4. Apply the 1-2-3 rule for calls-to-action on every page  `13.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 13*

Irwin's one-two-three framework, credited with a documented 48% conversion increase for one B2B service client, sets a minimum CTA count by scroll position: exactly one CTA type must be reachable at every point on the page (e.g. a sticky nav bar, sticky side widget, or sticky chatbot), a minimum of two CTAs must appear above the fold on arrival (top-right per F-shaped reading patterns, e.g. phone number or booking button, plus one in the header banner middle or beside the header text), and a minimum of three CTAs must be present by the bottom of the page (e.g. address, phone number, booking button, callback/SMS, AI chat). These are minimums, not maximums — he has audited pages with seven CTAs that still felt natural because they were placed as soft sell rather than aggressive buy-now messaging.

> "we created what I call the one-two-three framework"

**How to do it**

1. Open the target landing or conversion page and identify what is visible above the fold with zero scrolling.
2. Confirm at least 2 CTAs are visible above the fold: one top-right (phone number or booking button, per F-shaped reading pattern) and one in the header/banner middle area.
3. Confirm exactly one CTA type remains visible and reachable no matter how far the visitor scrolls, implemented via a sticky top nav bar, sticky side widget, or persistent chat launcher (inferred: use CSS position:sticky or an on-page widget script).
4. Scroll to the bottom of the page and confirm at least 3 CTAs are present there, e.g. address, phone number, and a booking/contact button, plus optional callback/SMS/chat options.
5. Count the total CTAs on the page; if it exceeds five to seven, review tone to ensure each reads as a soft, natural next step rather than a hard buy-now push, and reword rather than remove if in doubt.
6. Re-audit after launch by viewing the page as a first-time visitor and asking whether the CTAs feel like a natural offer or an aggressive sales wall; adjust copy and styling, not count, if it feels salesy.

**Pitfall:** Making CTAs feel salesy by stacking hard-sell language — Irwin's fix is not fewer CTAs, it is disguising a high CTA count (he cites a page with seven) inside natural, soft-sell phrasing so it does not feel aggressive.

### 5. Apply the two-second rule to every money-page hero section  `07.12`
*core · best practices · source 07*

The two-second rule is a hard usability check for every service/money page: within two seconds of landing, a visitor must be able to identify what the business does, where it operates (for local/service-area businesses), and what action to take next. The creator explicitly bans ambiguous or 'clever' hero copy at this stage — taglines are fine, but only after the first fold, never as the primary above-the-fold message. A passing example given is a plain H1 like 'Professional home EV charger installation in Austin' paired with a single clear CTA button ('Get a free quote'); a failing example is vague founder-story copy that never states the service. This check is framed as one of only four things that actually decide whether a money page performs, independent of word count or design polish.

> "Within two seconds of landing on this money page"

**How to do it**

1. Open the money/service page cold, as a first-time visitor would (or have a colleague unfamiliar with the page do it).
2. Time a strict 2-second glance and check whether 'what service is this' is immediately clear from the headline alone.
3. For local/service-area businesses, check whether the service location/area is visible within that same glance.
4. Check whether one unambiguous call-to-action is visible above the fold.
5. If any tagline, brand-voice line, or founder-story copy currently occupies the above-the-fold space, move it further down the page.
6. Rewrite the H1 in plain language stating the service and location if the current H1 fails the check.
7. Re-run the 2-second check after edits to confirm what/where/action are now all immediately clear.
8. Repeat this audit across every money/service page on the site, not only the homepage. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Leading with ambiguous, 'creative' copywriting above the fold instead of stating plainly what you do, where, and what to do next — the source's negative example is hero copy about a personal journey that never states the service being sold.

### 6. Audit the Google Ads search terms report before touching bids  `156.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 156*

Grow and Convert say the fastest way to see whether the keywords in a paid account carry real buying intent is the search terms report, which shows a sample of the actual queries that triggered your ads. Their example: you bid on 'web developers' and end up paying for 'what is the salary of a web developer in San Diego, CA'. That click can never become a web development client. They stress the problem in that case is the keyword, not the query, so the fix is to replace it with a term that only a buyer would type, such as 'web development agency for hire' or 'web developers that specialize in xyz'. They run this report as part of every new client audit, alongside SERP analysis and conversations with the client, before deciding which keywords stay.

> "take a look at your "search terms" report"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say the number one issue they find in client Google Ads accounts is wasted spend on keywords without buying intent, and that the search terms report is a surprise to many owners.

**How to do it**

1. Open Google Ads and pull the search terms report for the last 90 days across all search campaigns.
2. Sort by cost descending so the biggest waste surfaces first.
3. Tag every query as buyer, researcher, job seeker, or irrelevant.
4. For any keyword whose queries are mostly non-buyer, remove the keyword rather than only adding negatives.
5. Replace it with a longer term that only a buyer would type, such as 'X agency for hire' or 'X software for [segment]'.
6. Add the non-buyer patterns (salary, jobs, what is, free, DIY) as negative keywords at campaign level.
7. Cross-check the surviving terms against the live SERP to confirm the results are commercial, not informational.
8. Re-run the report monthly and repeat, because new queries keep appearing under phrase match.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Teams treat the report as a negative-keyword chore only. If the underlying keyword has no buying intent, negatives just slow the bleed; the signal you have hit this is a negative list that keeps growing while cost per lead stays flat.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should pull the search terms report for its practice management ads and check how much spend lands on 'clinic software free', 'medical software jobs' or student queries. Any keyword whose queries are mostly non-buyer should be cut, not just negated.

**Apply anywhere:** Pull your own search terms report, classify each query by whether a buyer would type it, and cut the keywords whose queries are mostly research or job seeking instead of only adding negatives.

### 7. Audit top 20 landing pages for mixed-intent queries  `36.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 36*

When one page tries to serve both informational intent (what is X) and commercial intent (buy X) at once, it converts neither audience well, so the fix is splitting the informational content from the commercial page and connecting them with contextual anchor text in the body copy rather than a generic call-to-action at the bottom. The specific audit to find these problem pages: pull the top 20 landing pages by traffic, look at the actual queries driving traffic to each, and flag any page where the top queries span both research-type and purchase-type intent — those mixed-intent pages are described as simultaneously the worst converters and the easiest wins to fix.

> "pull your top 20 landing pages, look at the queries driving traffic"

**How to do it**

1. Open Google Search Console (or Ahrefs/Semrush) and export the top 20 landing pages ranked by organic traffic or clicks.
2. For each of those 20 pages, pull the list of queries actually driving traffic to it via the GSC Performance report filtered by page.
3. Classify each query as informational/research intent (e.g., what is X, how does X work) or commercial/purchase intent (e.g., buy X, X pricing, best X).
4. Flag any page whose top queries span both intent categories at once as a mixed-intent page.
5. For each flagged page, split the content: keep or create a purely informational page or section for the research queries, and a separate, dedicated commercial page for the purchase queries.
6. Connect the two pages with contextual anchor text placed naturally within the informational page's body copy, not a generic click-here link or a single CTA button at the bottom.
7. Re-check each split page's conversion rate and query-intent mix after a few weeks to confirm the split resolved the mismatch. (inferred)

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Trying to fix a mixed-intent page's conversion rate with better copy or A/B testing alone — the conversion math is described as fixing itself once the informational and commercial intents are split into separate pages.

### 8. Benchmark blog-to-email at 3% under 100k uniques, 2% above  `147.2`
*core · best practices · source 147*

Grow and Convert give hard benchmark figures for reader-to-email conversion so you can tell whether your own number is broken or normal. For a blog with five figures of monthly unique visitors, 3% is achievable for most good blogs and should be the benchmark. Their own site averaged 4.6% across its top 10 landing pages in a 30-day window, with the best single post at 12%. Above 100,000 uniques a month it becomes hard to crack 2%, and they say the clients who beat that are special cases. They attribute the variation mainly to content quality and opt-in tactics, not to design. The general rule they state twice: as traffic rises, expect conversion rates to fall, because higher volume pulls in looser intent.

> "if a blog exceeds 100,000 uniques a month, then it becomes hard to crack 2%"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own top 10 landing pages: 4.6% average to email, 12% best post, over 30 days.

**How to do it**

1. Segment GA4 to blog URLs only and read unique users for the last 30 days.
2. Count email subscribers acquired from the blog in the same window.
3. Divide to get your reader-to-email rate.
4. Compare against 3% if you are in five-figure monthly traffic and 2% if you are above 100,000 uniques.
5. If you are materially below the benchmark, look at content quality and opt-in placement first, not popup design.
6. Break the number out per landing page, as they do for their top 10, so one outlier post does not hide a weak average.
7. Re-benchmark after any traffic step-change, since the target drops as volume rises.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Judging your rate against a site-wide or landing-page benchmark. These figures are specifically content pages, which convert far below dedicated landing pages.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before David invests in newsletter growth for pabau.com, measure the current blog-to-email rate against the 3% bar. If the blog is well under it, the fix is topic selection on the blog, not more popups.

**Apply anywhere:** Before investing in newsletter growth, measure your blog-to-email rate against the 3% bar for five-figure traffic. If you are well under it, fix topic selection before adding more opt-in forms.

### 9. Benchmark buying-intent posts at 25 times the conversion rate of top-funnel ones  `120.1`
*core · content insights · source 120*

Grow and Convert published the conversion rates of 65 of their own posts, split by whether the target keyword sat at the bottom or the top of the funnel. The bottom-of-funnel group converted at 4.78 percent. The top-of-funnel group converted at 0.19 percent. That is a 25x gap on the same sites, with the same writers and the same calls to action, so the only variable that moved is the intent behind the keyword. They say the pattern repeats across thousands of articles for clients in different industries and business models. The number matters because it sets the arithmetic for any traffic argument. A top-funnel page needs about 25 times the sessions of a buying-intent page to produce the same lead count, and most top-funnel terms do not carry that much more volume. They are explicit that the rule flips for ad-monetized media sites, where volume is the revenue.

> "posts had a conversion rate of 4.78%"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: 65 posts measured, 4.78 percent conversion on bottom-of-funnel keywords against 0.19 percent on top-of-funnel keywords.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Comparing conversion rate without comparing absolute conversions hides the case where a huge top-funnel page still wins on volume. Run both numbers before cutting a page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should tag every blog post with the funnel stage of its target keyword and report conversion rate by tag. If the split is anywhere near 25x, the publishing calendar should shift toward comparison, alternatives and per-problem pages rather than general aesthetics-industry explainers.

**Apply anywhere:** Tag each published post with the funnel stage of its target keyword, then report conversion rate by that tag. A gap on this scale is the strongest available argument for moving the publishing calendar toward comparison, alternatives and problem-specific pages.

### 10. Bottom-funnel keywords convert 10x to 25x better, and now the gap is wider  `86.5`
*core · content insights · source 86*

Grow & Convert have prioritized bottom-of-funnel content since 2018, well before AI search, and published the reasoning as Pain Point SEO. Their multiple case studies showed bottom-of-funnel keywords converting 10x to 25x better than top-of-funnel keywords. Their argument in this piece is that AI search widens that gap rather than changing it. With top-of-funnel content in an AI answer, the user gets the question answered inside the tool, so you lose not only the conversion but the visit that gave you a chance at one. With traditional search you at least got the click. So the funnel-stage decision that was already correct on conversion-rate grounds is now correct twice over, and any content strategy still built on high-volume informational keywords for traffic growth is optimizing for a metric that no longer produces business outcomes.

> "bottom of the funnel keywords convert 10x to 25x better"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert's Pain Point SEO case studies: 10x to 25x higher conversion from bottom-of-funnel versus top-of-funnel keywords, a position they have held since 2018.

**Pitfall:** Traffic-based reporting hides this. A top-of-funnel page can still show healthy impressions in GSC while sending fewer clicks and near-zero conversions, so the page survives audits it should fail.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David reviews Pabau's blog inventory, rank pages by conversion, not sessions. High-traffic definitional posts should be consolidated or repointed toward comparison, alternatives and template pages that name Pabau.

**Apply anywhere:** Rank your blog inventory by conversion, not sessions. Consolidate high-traffic definitional posts and shift the budget to comparison, alternatives and template pages that name your product.

### 11. Build '[Competitor] alternative' pages with comparison tables  `47.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 47*

Edward's closing tactic from the thread is building dedicated competitor alternative comparison pages, targeting the competitor's brand name plus the word 'alternative' as the primary keyword, with a side-by-side comparison table, your brand versus theirs, placed under its own H2 on the page. He explains the mechanism: if a competitor isn't publishing their own SEO content targeting their own brand name plus features or services terms, your alternative page can rank in that gap, effectively capturing search traffic and keyword territory the competitor left undefended. The page's job is explicitly bottom-of-funnel and persuasive, aimed at people already in active consideration of, or looking to switch away from, that competitor, converting them toward your brand instead. He adds that this content format is currently working really well with AI, with these comparison and alternative pages getting cited frequently by AI systems right now.

> "I get to rank for their keywords and snatch their traffic"

**How to do it**

1. List your top 3-5 direct competitors by brand name.
2. For each competitor, create a dedicated page targeting the keyword phrase '[Competitor Brand Name] alternative' as its primary target.
3. Structure the page with a clear H2 heading introducing a side-by-side comparison between your brand and the competitor's brand.
4. Build a literal comparison table under that H2, listing features, pricing, or services side by side for your brand versus the competitor's.
5. Write the surrounding copy to explicitly address people who are already considering the competitor or actively looking to switch away from them, framing your brand as the better choice.
6. Check whether the competitor has their own SEO content targeting their own brand name plus feature or service terms, and prioritize competitors with weak or absent SEO content first, since your page has the best chance of ranking in that gap (inferred).
7. Monitor whether these alternative and comparison pages get cited in AI Overview, ChatGPT, or other AI-search responses over time, since this content format is reported to be getting cited frequently right now (inferred).
8. Build out one alternative page per major competitor systematically, rather than just a single one-off page, to capture this keyword territory broadly.

### 12. Build a calculator that shows the cost of the problem you solve  `139.8`
*core · content insights · source 139*

Brandon reports that Keeper Tax's top converting pages were not articles but calculators. The pages showed freelancers how much tax they would owe with no write-offs applied. The conversion mechanism is specific: an independent contractor running the tool typically sees a large number, and the product is then positioned as the way to reduce it. The visitor arrives with an abstract worry, the calculator makes it a concrete figure, and the software is the fix for that exact figure. Brandon also made the calculator the destination of his internal linking effort, pointing many blog posts and pages at the '1099 tax calculator' page. So the calculator did double duty: highest conversion rate on the site, and the page that concentrated internal link value.

> "Our top converting pages were our calculator pages"

**Evidence:** Keeper Tax: calculator pages were the site's highest converting pages, and signups from content grew over 700% a month between May and September.

**How to do it**

1. Identify the number your customer is anxious about and that your product reduces.
2. Build a calculator that outputs that number for the visitor's own inputs, with no account required.
3. Keep the inputs to the fewest fields that still produce a credible figure.
4. Show the result as the bad case first, before any optimization your product would apply.
5. Place the signup or demo CTA directly beneath the result, framed as reducing that figure.
6. Target the calculator page at the query people type when looking for the number, not at a branded term.
7. Point internal links from every related blog post at the calculator page.
8. Track signups per session on the calculator against your best article and reallocate effort accordingly.

**Pitfall:** A calculator that outputs a neutral or reassuring number converts nothing. The output has to be the problem stated in the visitor's own figures, or there is nothing for the product to fix.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build calculators that price the problem for a practice, such as revenue lost to no-shows per month or staff hours spent on manual rebooking, and link every related blog article into them.

**Apply anywhere:** Build a calculator that puts a number on the problem your product removes, show the unoptimized figure first, put the signup CTA under the result, and link your related articles into it.

### 13. Build a dedicated landing page per keyword, not a keyword-sprinkled page  `158.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 158*

Grow and Convert call this the specificity strategy. Rather than adding a keyword to an existing page, the whole page is built to answer the questions behind that one keyword. For 'magento web design agency' the page they describe would carry examples of Magento sites the client had designed, a discussion of the nuances of designing a Magento site, and guidance on choosing the right agency for your website. They stress this is more involved than sprinkling keywords through an existing page, and that the asset can be either a new landing page or a blog post. The point is that the searcher's unanswered questions are what decide whether they convert, and a repurposed page built for a different purpose leaves most of them unanswered.

> "what we call the specificity strategy"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert attribute higher bounce rates specifically to landing pages whose title does not match the ad keyword.

**How to do it**

1. Take one high-intent keyword and write out every question a searcher typing it would want answered.
2. Group those questions into three or four sections that will become the page's headings.
3. Include proof specific to that keyword, such as examples of work in exactly that niche.
4. Include an explanation of the nuances of the thing being searched for, so the page shows expertise.
5. Include buying guidance, for example how to choose a provider for that specific need.
6. Put the keyword in the page title and the H1, not just scattered in the body.
7. Decide between a landing page and a blog post based on which format the ranking pages for that term use.
8. Point the matching ad group at this page only, and do not reuse it for adjacent keywords.

**Pitfall:** The shortcut of retitling an existing page to match the keyword leaves body copy written for a different intent, so bounce rate stays high and the spend is still wasted.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build a separate page per high-intent segment term rather than adding sections to the main product page. A 'medspa practice management software' page needs medspa screenshots, medspa workflow detail and medspa buying criteria.

**Apply anywhere:** Build one page per high-intent keyword, containing proof from that exact niche, an explanation of its nuances and guidance on choosing a provider. Do not retitle a general page and call it targeted.

### 14. Build a store collection per article and point the CTA at it  `138.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 138*

Nat Eliason calls this the real tipping point. Instead of linking blog readers to the store or to a single product, Cup & Leaf built topic-specific collections in their Shopify store, one matching each article, then tied the article into that collection with both an inline text link and a visual call to action. The best black tea article sends readers to the black tea collection. The 'best teas for weight loss' article sends them to a collection that did not exist before the article. He shows traffic to the black tea collection growing sharply after the change, and traffic arriving at a brand new weight-loss collection. The mechanism is that the collection is the store page that answers the article's question, so the reader crosses from reading to shopping without changing subject.

> "we started making topic-specific collections in our store"

**Evidence:** Cup & Leaf saw significant traffic growth to their black tea collection page after adding article CTAs, plus traffic to an entirely new 'best teas for weight loss' collection; sales from SEO rose over 300% in two months.

**How to do it**

1. List every converting or planned article and write the store collection that would answer its question exactly.
2. Create that collection in the store, curating only products that genuinely fit the article's topic or problem.
3. Place an inline text link to the collection immediately after the article's introductory paragraph.
4. Place a visual call-to-action block in the same position, so both link types are available at the point of highest attention.
5. Send both links to the collection URL, never to the store homepage or to a single product page.
6. Add further product callouts alongside the body sections they relate to, matched section by section.
7. Track sessions and revenue on each collection page separately, so you can see the article-to-collection flow working.
8. Create a new collection whenever an article's problem has no matching store page, rather than pointing it at the nearest existing one.

**Tools:** Shopify

**Pitfall:** Sending the reader to the store homepage or to one product resets their search. Eliason's point is that the collection is what makes the handoff continuous, and building it takes real work, which is why most sites default to a popup instead.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's analogue is a feature or use-case page per article rather than the generic /book-demo/ link. An article about no-shows should link to the reminders feature page, not the homepage, with the link placed straight after the intro.

**Apply anywhere:** Build one store collection or use-case landing page per article, curated to that article's exact topic, and point both an inline link and a visual CTA at it immediately after the introduction.

### 15. Build message match between the download topic and the trial offer  `148.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 148*

Shour credits 'Message Match' as the main lever on these pages. The employee engagement ideas post feeds a thank you page that argues healthy office snacks raise employee engagement, then offers a free sample box. That page converts 10.52% of visitors into leads. The same structure built around employee recognition converts only 7.59%, and Shour's own hypothesis is that the link between snacks and recognition is weaker, so prospects do not connect the dots. Devesh Khanal's fix is to add one sentence under the title spelling the connection out, such as saying office snacks can be used to show recognition in fun ways, and to expand it into bullets. The page has three parts in order: scent, an accelerate-your-results question, then the offer.

> "This Thank You page is converting 10.52% visitors into leads"

**Evidence:** 10.52% conversion on the employee engagement page versus 7.59% on the employee recognition page, same template and same offer.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the one-line argument connecting the article's topic to what your product does.
2. If that argument takes more than a sentence to defend, treat the match as weak and expect a lower rate.
3. Open the page by naming the downloaded asset so the visitor sees the same message they just acted on.
4. Add the bridge sentence directly under the title stating how the product serves that exact topic.
5. Expand the bridge into two or three concrete example bullets when the connection is not obvious.
6. Present the offer as solving the pain point named in the article, and make it risk-free.
7. Compare each page's rate against your strongest match page, and rewrite the bridge on any page a third lower.
8. Rebuild the weak page rather than A/B testing button copy on it.

**Tools:** Unbounce

**Pitfall:** A topic that only loosely relates to the product produces a page that reads as a non sequitur. Shour's employee recognition page fell to 7.59% against 10.52% for the tightly matched engagement page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau template pages cover very different jobs, from consent forms to treatment plans. The demo offer after each download should name that job, for example tying a consent form template to how Pabau stores signed forms against the client record.

**Apply anywhere:** Write a one-sentence bridge between the article topic and your product, put it under the offer page title, and expand it into examples where the link is not obvious. Weak bridges show up as a materially lower conversion rate.

### 16. Build one catch-all offer page for top-of-funnel posts  `148.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 148*

Building a message-matched page for every topic and every content upgrade does not scale, so Shour built a single 'Catch All' thank you page with no specific match to a free offer. He routes higher-in-the-funnel awareness posts to it, examples being a motivational videos roundup and a fun office activities list. It converts at 10.67%, higher than either of his message-matched pages, which he calls surprising. The catch is downstream: this page has produced a lower percentage of leads that convert into sales. More people hand over their details, but the lead quality is worse. His conclusion is the general rule that follows: do not stop at the top-of-funnel conversion rate, dig into whether those leads become sales.

> "is converting at 10.67%"

**Evidence:** SnackNation's catch-all thank you page converts at 10.67% versus 10.52% and 7.59% for message-matched pages, but produces a lower percentage of leads that turn into sales.

**How to do it**

1. List the blog posts that are awareness plays with no clean product connection.
2. Build one generic offer page with scent wording that works for any asset, plus the trial offer.
3. Route every one of those posts' opt-in forms to that single page.
4. Tag the leads it produces with the page source in your CRM.
5. Reserve bespoke message-matched pages for your highest-traffic, product-adjacent posts only.
6. After a quarter, compare lead-to-sale rate for catch-all leads against message-matched leads.
7. Promote any catch-all post whose leads close well to its own message-matched page.
8. Accept the lower quality on the rest as the cost of not building 50 pages.

**Tools:** Unbounce

**Pitfall:** The catch-all page's higher opt-in rate makes it look like the winner. Shour found its leads convert into sales at a lower rate, so a page-level conversion report alone will point you the wrong way.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build one generic post-download demo page for blog articles with no product-adjacent angle, and reserve bespoke pages for template articles. Tag both in the CRM so lead quality is comparable.

**Apply anywhere:** Build one generic offer page for your awareness-level posts instead of a bespoke page per topic, tag its leads, and check lead-to-sale rate before treating its higher opt-in rate as a win.

### 17. Cap each ad group at a few closely related keywords, not Google's 5-30  `157.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 157*

Google recommends 5 to 30 keywords per ad group. Grow and Convert say that is too many, and their reason is mechanical: you cannot write ad text relevant to 30 keywords at once. If someone searches for accounting software and the ad text talks about invoicing and payments, they are far less likely to click and much less likely to convert. Their rule is a handful of closely related keywords per ad group, all sharing the same intent, on phrase or exact match. Their example failure is a business management vendor with an ad group called 'project management' holding 'time tracking', 'task management', 'file sharing' and 'client portal'. Those are all features the vendor offers, but each carries different intent and so needs its own ad and landing page.

> "Google recommends including between 5-30 keywords per ad group"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contradict Google's own 5-30 keyword guidance based on client account audits.

**How to do it**

1. Export every ad group with its keyword count and sort descending.
2. Open any ad group with more than about five keywords and group its keywords by intent, not by feature ownership.
3. Split each intent cluster into its own ad group named after that intent.
4. Keep only closely related keywords in each new group, all on phrase or exact match.
5. Write one set of ad copy per new group containing that group's shared search term.
6. Assign each new group a landing page that answers its specific intent.
7. Delete or pause the original catch-all ad group rather than leaving it running alongside.
8. Re-check click-through rate per group after two weeks; a group still below account average is probably still mixing intents.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Grouping keywords by which product feature they map to. Features you own are not intents buyers have, which is how 'time tracking' and 'client portal' end up sharing one ad.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau builds campaigns, split by buyer intent such as online booking, clinical notes and inventory rather than by Pabau's feature list, and give each split its own destination page.

**Apply anywhere:** Cap ad groups at a few keywords sharing one intent, against Google's 5-30 recommendation. Split by the intent the searcher has, not by the feature you happen to own.

### 18. Cap each ad group at six keywords sharing one intent  `156.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 156*

Grow and Convert say the common advice of 5 to 30 keywords per ad group, once recommended by Google, is far too many, and that even 10 is usually too many. They use Google's own dessert example, where an ad group called 'desserts' holds cupcakes, pumpkin pie, apple pie and chocolate cake. Someone searching for birthday cupcakes will not click an ad about pumpkin pie. Their structure instead groups keywords that share one intent, such as buying cupcakes in Portland, and caps the group at six keywords, which in practice is three terms each in phrase and exact: 'cupcakes for sale', 'cupcakes in Portland', 'cupcake bakery near me'. They allow a range of roughly 2 to 8 depending on the intent, ad text and landing page, but six is the working ceiling.

> "the ad group should really only have up to six keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contrast Google's published 5 to 30 keyword guidance and its own dessert ad group example against their working cap of six.

**How to do it**

1. Export the account and count keywords per ad group.
2. Split any group holding more than six keywords, or any group whose keywords express more than one intent.
3. Write the single searcher intent each new group serves in one sentence.
4. Add three terms per group as phrase match and the same three as exact match, giving six keywords.
5. Write ad text that repeats the group's intent in the headline.
6. Point the group at one landing page that matches that intent exactly.
7. Allow the group to run 2 to 8 keywords only if all of them share that one intent.
8. Review click-through rate per group and split further whenever CTR is well below account average.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Splitting into single-keyword ad groups spreads conversion data too thin for the bidding to learn. Grow and Convert's ceiling is six, not one, and the test is whether all keywords share one intent.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should restructure paid ad groups around one intent each, for example 'aesthetic clinic booking software' separately from 'clinic EMR software', with a matching landing page for each rather than one generic demo page.

**Apply anywhere:** Cap each ad group at six keywords that all express one buying intent, run them as three phrase plus three exact, and give each group its own ad text and landing page.

### 19. Change the keyword before you A/B test the call to action  `142.8`
*core · best practices · source 142*

Grow and Convert take a direct position against standard conversion rate optimization. They describe how most marketers use the term, small tweaks to a site to lift a desired action, such as reducing page load time or bounce rate or A/B testing different calls to action. Their verdict from this dataset is that these small tweaks are not all that impactful, and that the number one thing dictating your conversion rate is the search intent of the person searching and how well the query aligns with what your product does. The evidence sits in the spread they report: 0.6% to 15.2% across posts on the same sites, with the same design, the same CTAs and the same brand. No button color test moves a page by that factor. This contradicts the usual CRO-first sequencing, where teams optimize existing pages before revisiting what those pages target.

> "these small tweaks aren't all that impactful"

**Evidence:** Conversion rates ranged from 0.6% to 15.2% across posts on the same client sites, with design and CTAs held constant.

**How to do it**

1. Before booking any CTA or layout test, pull the query set actually driving traffic to the page.
2. Ask whether the searcher behind those queries needs your product to finish what they came for.
3. If the answer is no, stop the test and change what the page targets instead.
4. Rewrite or repoint the page at a category, alternatives or require-a-tool JTBD keyword.
5. Only run CRO tests on pages whose queries already carry buying intent.
6. Hold every page to the type benchmark for its keyword group before crediting or blaming the design.
7. Record the keyword type alongside every CRO test result so a poor test is not misread as a design failure.

**Pitfall:** Running a CRO program on top-of-funnel pages. The ceiling is set by intent, so a well-executed test on a 0.6% page returns a rounding error while a keyword change on the same page can move it several points.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau invests in CTA testing on blog articles, David should check the query mix on each one. Articles pulling advice-shaped traffic need a new target keyword, not a new button.

**Apply anywhere:** Before investing in CTA testing on content pages, check the query mix on each one. A page pulling advice-shaped traffic needs a new target keyword, not a new button.

### 20. Choose paid keywords on buying intent, never on volume or cheap CPC  `157.3`
*core · best practices · source 157*

Grow and Convert say nobody deliberately targets top-of-funnel terms in paid search, yet accounts fill up with them for three reasons: chasing high volume to spend the monthly budget, chasing low CPC keywords because they look cost-efficient, and using broad match. They are explicit that low CPC and high volume terms are allowed in an account, but neither may be the deciding factor for adding a keyword. The deciding factor is search intent. Their point about cheap clicks is the sharp one: a low CPC keyword without buying intent still wastes the whole spend, because cheap clicks that never convert cost the same as expensive ones in outcome terms. They add that intent matters more in paid than in organic, because in paid every wrong query costs money.

> "The deciding factor should be search intent."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert audit client accounts and repeatedly find spend on low-intent terms added to hit volume or low-CPC goals.

**How to do it**

1. Take your current keyword list and add a column for buying intent, judged on whether the searcher is trying to buy the type of product you sell.
2. Mark each keyword high, medium or low intent; treat 'what is', 'how to' and definition terms as low.
3. Pause every low-intent keyword regardless of how cheap its CPC or how large its volume.
4. Do not add a keyword because it fills the budget; if spend is under target, widen into more high-intent long-tail terms instead.
5. Check the search terms report for the queries actually triggering each keyword and negate the low-intent ones.
6. Re-check the intent column monthly, since new search terms arrive on the same keywords.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Filling unused budget with high-volume terms at month end. It buys clicks that raise CPA and, worse, produce leads sales rejects.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau runs paid search, the keyword set should be buying-intent terms like practice management software and competitor alternatives, not the informational topics the blog covers. Those informational terms belong in organic only.

**Apply anywhere:** Select paid keywords on buying intent alone. Informational topics that work in organic should stay in organic, because in paid every low-intent click is paid for twice, in spend and in sales time.

### 21. Compare landing page and blog conversion rates on matched BOFU keywords  `146.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 146*

Grow and Convert settled an internal argument with data rather than opinion, and the method is repeatable. They picked three of the client's industry product landing pages and three of their own blog posts targeting closely comparable bottom-of-funnel keywords in the same industries, then calculated conversion rate from organic traffic only for each. Two of the blog posts targeted industry plus software plus a specific feature, so they noted the mismatch instead of hiding it. They deliberately ignored raw conversion volume, which favored the older, higher-volume landing pages, because the claim under test was about rate. Devesh, who runs a CRO agency, went in believing landing pages convert better and changed his view on the numbers.

> "We calculated the conversion rates from organic traffic for three product landing pages"

**Evidence:** The exercise produced ~0.4% for landing pages and 1.13% for blog posts across one SaaS client.

**How to do it**

1. List every product or solution landing page that targets a bottom-of-funnel keyword.
2. For each, find the closest comparable blog post on your own site targeting a keyword of the same intent and vertical.
3. Segment analytics to organic traffic only, so paid and email visits do not inflate the landing pages.
4. Compute conversion rate per URL as goal completions divided by organic sessions over the same date window.
5. Note where the keyword match is imperfect, for example head term versus head term plus feature, and record it beside the number.
6. Ignore raw conversion volume in this comparison, since older and higher-volume pages will always win on total.
7. Also record publish date per page, because a page live for years is not comparable on totals to a recent one.
8. Decide the format for the next bottom-of-funnel keyword from the rate table, not from received wisdom.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Comparing totals instead of rates. The landing pages in their data drove far more traffic and conversions purely from age and head-term volume, which would have reversed the conclusion.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this comparison once for Pabau, pairing each feature or solution page with the closest blog article on the same buying term, filtered to organic. The output is a standing rule for which format future Pabau bottom-of-funnel terms get.

**Apply anywhere:** Run this comparison once, pairing each product or solution page with the closest blog article on the same buying term, filtered to organic. The output is a standing rule for which format future bottom-of-funnel terms get.

### 22. Compare two posts on conversion rate before ranking them by traffic  `137.10`
*core · content insights · source 137*

Grow and Convert close the Cognitive FX case with a direct comparison. Their 'symptoms of a concussion' post generated 23,500 sessions and 23 conversions. Another post got one third of that traffic and still produced more conversions in the same timeframe, making its conversion rate more than three times higher. They stress that nothing about the page treatment differed: no popups, no special CTAs, no A/B tested elements. The whole difference is the conversion intent of the topic. Someone who knows enough to search post concussion syndrome already understands the value of a specialist clinic. They saw the same pattern with an earlier client where three pain-point posts produced 41, 33 and 51 conversions while the rest produced 5, 3, 0, 8, 0, 0 and 3.

> "the bottom post got only 1/3 of the traffic"

**Evidence:** 23,500 sessions and 23 conversions on one post versus a third of the traffic and more conversions on another; conversion rate over 3x higher. An earlier client showed 41, 33 and 51 conversions on pain-point posts against 5, 3, 0, 8, 0, 0 and 3 on the rest.

**How to do it**

1. Pull sessions and conversions per post for a fixed period from analytics.
2. Calculate conversion rate per post rather than sorting by sessions.
3. Identify the highest-traffic post and the highest-conversion-rate post and compare their target queries.
4. Check whether the higher-converting query requires prior knowledge that only a qualified buyer would have.
5. Write that knowledge requirement down as the pattern to repeat in the next batch of topics.
6. Resist adding popups or CTA experiments to the low-converting post; the topic, not the page furniture, is the constraint.
7. Retire or repurpose high-traffic, low-conversion-rate posts rather than optimizing them further.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Teams respond to a low-converting high-traffic post with conversion-rate optimization on the page. Grow and Convert found no page element explained the gap, so that work wastes months while the topic mix stays wrong.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should sort blog posts by demo-request conversion rate, not sessions, and expect that pages about specific practice problems beat high-traffic aesthetic explainers by several multiples.

**Apply anywhere:** Sort your posts by conversion rate, not traffic. When a low-traffic post converts three times better with identical page treatment, the difference is topic intent and no amount of CRO on the weaker page will close it.

*core · concrete actions · source 158*

Google recommends 5 to 30 keywords per ad group. Grow and Convert say that is too many, and show why with their client's real group: a single Magento ad group holding 23 keywords ranging from 'magento developers' to 'web design agency' to 'magento b2b agency'. No single ad text can be relevant to all of them, and if someone searching for Magento developers sees copy about BigCommerce developers they will not click, let alone convert. Because the intent behind all of them matches, one piece of targeted ad copy works for every searcher in the group.

> "in our experience this is too many keywords per ad group"

**Evidence:** Client group held 23 mixed-intent keywords; the rebuild used six closely related ones on phrase and exact match.

2. For any group over about six keywords, write the intent of each keyword in one phrase beside it.
3. Split the group so each new group contains only keywords sharing one intent phrase.
4. Set every keyword in the new groups to phrase or exact match.
5. Write one ad per group whose headline works verbatim for all its keywords.
6. Assign each new group a landing page built for that intent.
7. Name groups descriptively, for example 'Development Agency eCommerce US Search', so structure stays readable.

### 25. Diagnose non-converting traffic by testing cold-audience sales first  `160.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 160*

When a site gets the right traffic and still does not convert, Grow and Convert say the failure is usually product/market fit or positioning, not the content. Their diagnostic question is direct: have you proven the ability to sell your product or service to cold audiences? A company can build multiple millions in revenue on referrals and still have no proof it can close a stranger, because with referrals someone else does the vouching and value explanation. They describe a client in exactly that position whose cold site traffic converted at close to nothing, and the engagement ended. Test the cold channel cheaply through outbound or paid before blaming the blog, since outbound counts as a cold test for the same reason.

> "Have you proven the ability to sell your product or service to cold audiences?"

**Evidence:** A Grow and Convert client with multiple millions in referral revenue drove growing traffic with no paid signups; on inspection little if any of their cold site traffic had ever converted, and the relationship ended for lack of ROI.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm the traffic is the right audience before anything else, using on-site behavior and lead quality rather than volume.
2. Segment site conversions by source and isolate cold traffic from referral, direct and branded search.
3. If cold traffic converts far below warm traffic, stop optimizing articles and treat it as a positioning problem.
4. Run a cheap cold test outside content: a short outbound email or call sequence to strangers, or a small paid campaign to the same offer.
5. If strangers do not close on the same pitch that closes referrals, rewrite the site messaging to say out loud what a referrer normally says for you.
6. Re-test the rewritten pitch on cold traffic before adding more articles.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Assuming referral close rates carry over to cold audiences. Grow and Convert say this is not true in their experience, and the tell is a company with strong word-of-mouth revenue whose website converts almost nobody.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sells to practices through several channels, so before David judges a blog page as underperforming, check whether cold visitors to that page ever convert compared with demo requests from referrals and directories. If cold conversion is near zero site-wide, the fix is the demo page messaging, not more articles.

**Apply anywhere:** When the right traffic arrives and does not convert, check whether the business has ever closed a cold buyer. Test it with outbound or paid before rewriting more articles, because referral sales prove nothing about whether your site messaging closes strangers.
