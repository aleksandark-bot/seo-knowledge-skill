### 1. AI content rarely ranks because of pogo-sticking, not AI detection  `43.9`
*core · content insights · source 43*

Rand states that Google's own data shows AI-written content 'rarely ranks, and rarely ranks for long,' and he explicitly rejects the common explanation that this is because Google has built an accurate AI-content detector. Instead he attributes it to user behavioral signals, specifically pogo-sticking: a reader clicks through, immediately recognizes generic/gibberish-feeling AI content, and bounces straight back to the results, and Google's ranking systems pick up on that dissatisfaction signal rather than detecting the text's AI origin directly. He frames this as reassuring evidence that genuinely valuable, non-generic content still has a durable advantage, since the penalty is behavioral (reader reaction) rather than forensic (text-pattern detection).

> "it rarely ranks, not never, but rarely ranks"

**Evidence:** Rand's direct claim, sourced from Google ranking data he's observed, that AI-written content rarely ranks and rarely sustains rankings; his explanation ties this to pogo-sticking (users bouncing back to the results) rather than AI-detection technology.

**Apply at Pabau:** For any AI-assisted Pabau content, the real risk is not an AI-detector penalty but a reader immediately sensing generic, low-substance writing and bouncing — the quality bar to worry about is whether content reads as genuinely specific and useful, not whether it will be detected as AI-written.

**Apply anywhere:** For any AI-assisted your content, the real risk is not an AI-detector penalty but a reader immediately sensing generic, low-substance writing and bouncing — the quality bar to worry about is whether content reads as genuinely specific and useful, not whether it will be detected as AI-written.

### 2. AI content site rode a core update, then got crushed  `27.8`
*core · content insights · source 27*

The AI-generated financial-news site aiinvest.com is tracked across four points in time: about 270,000 clicks a month, then boosted by Google's June 2025 core algorithm update up to roughly 9.5 million clicks a month, then hit by the August 2025 spam update and dropped to about 900,000 clicks a month, and a year after that, down further to roughly 8,000 clicks a month. The speaker notes that after the spam update, some observers still called it a "win" because traffic remained above the original baseline — but the continued year-long decline to near-zero shows that read was premature. He adds that his own detailed analysis found the site was "actually doing a relatively good job with AI content" by conventional quality standards, yet it still lost nearly all of its traffic, which both speakers treat as evidence that even well-executed AI content at scale isn't safe from enforcement.

> "270,000 clicks a month, got boosted by the June 2025 core algorithm"

**Evidence:** Documented traffic trajectory for aiinvest.com: 270,000 to 9.5 million clicks/month (June 2025 core update), down to 900,000 (August 2025 spam update), down to about 8,000 clicks/month roughly a year later, despite the site being assessed as relatively well-executed AI content.

**Apply:** A partial recovery, or a traffic level that's still "up" after one Google update, should not be read as having survived enforcement — David should track sites like this over a full year-plus horizon before concluding an AI-content strategy is safe, and should not assume good execution alone protects a scaled AI-content site from further algorithmic action.

### 3. AI-generated content anchors you to a bad first draft  `46.6`
*core · content insights · source 46*

When you let AI generate a site's content and structure end-to-end from just a set of keywords, you inherit its content structure, good or bad, because editing from a flawed draft works like negotiation anchoring: 'you give a low price, and now you're arguing up from this low price.' Even careful post-editing, like stripping em dashes or tweaking phrasing, doesn't fix this, because the underlying structural decisions the AI made are still what you're revising against rather than the structure you'd have chosen if you'd thought it through first. Travis frames AI as a 'last-mile tool,' like scooters covering the last mile from a bus stop, that should only be applied after you've already worked out your own process and content structure, never as the starting point that defines your structure for you.

> "You are editing from what the AI anchored you in"

**Evidence:** Edward's framing: because AI writes both the keyword-driven content and the structure in one pass, the human editor only ever edits within that AI-chosen structure, comparable to price anchoring in negotiation, even after removing surface AI tells like em dashes.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau articles, always build the outline and content structure yourself, or via a human-reviewed outline stage, before any AI drafts prose — never let an AI tool generate structure and copy in one pass and then just edit the output, since you'll unconsciously stay anchored to its often searcher-misaligned structure.

**Apply anywhere:** For your articles, always build the outline and content structure yourself, or via a human-reviewed outline stage, before any AI drafts prose — never let an AI tool generate structure and copy in one pass and then just edit the output, since you'll unconsciously stay anchored to its often searcher-misaligned structure.

### 4. AI-written content is the worst content for winning AI recommendations  `86.7`
*core · content insights · source 86*

Grow & Convert make a counterintuitive argument with a clear mechanism. LLMs are trained to produce text resembling their training data, so AI-written content reflects the most average, most common way of saying a thing. It reads as articulate and correct while saying nothing original or differentiating. Publish that and, when ChatGPT comes to pitch your brand, it has only generic, undifferentiated material to pitch with. They connect this to a term they coined years earlier, Mirage Content: content that looks good on the surface but is high-level fluff regurgitating what everyone else already said. They previously applied that label to most freelance-written content and now call AI-generated content Mirage Content at scale. Note this is a different failure mode from the usual argument about AI content and rankings. The claim is not that Google detects it; it is that the model has nothing specific to repeat about you.

> "content written by AI is actually the worst kind of content"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert's Mirage Content concept, coined years before this article for freelance-written fluff, now applied to AI-generated content as 'Mirage Content at scale'.

**Pitfall:** The output passes every quality check a marketing team runs, because it is grammatical, on-brief and complete. The failure only shows up as absence from AI recommendation answers, which nobody is monitoring.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's AI-assisted drafting must not produce the article's substance. Draft structure and edits are fine, but the differentiating detail about Pabau's workflows and the aesthetic practice pain points has to come from people, or ChatGPT will describe Pabau exactly as it describes every competitor.

**Apply anywhere:** Don't let AI produce the substance of the article. Structure and editing are fine, but the differentiating detail about your product has to come from people, or the model will describe you exactly as it describes every competitor.

### 5. Abandon a structural framework once the facts stop supporting it  `72.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 72*

Khanal starts the article around a three-category framework: short-form generators, on-page SEO tools, and marketing writing tools. Editing it, he checks the actual products and finds every vendor is expanding into every category. Jasper covers GEO, SEO, personalization and campaigns; Copy.ai does SEO content; Wordtune does more than paraphrasing. He concludes out loud that there are no categories, deletes the section, and switches to the other intro he had written, which asks three questions about any tool regardless of category. His reasoning: the framework was going to invite a factual dispute he could not win, and the question-based intro sidesteps categorization entirely. He notes this is what real editing looks like and that anyone who writes knows it happens.

> "there are no categories"

**Evidence:** Khanal checked Jasper's solutions page live, found it covering GEO, SEO, personalization and campaigns, and deleted the three-category section he had already written the intro around.

**How to do it**

1. Before committing to a taxonomy in an article, test it against three or four of the actual products it sorts.
2. Check each vendor's solutions page for the categories they claim, not just their headline positioning.
3. If more than one vendor spans multiple buckets, treat the taxonomy as unsupportable.
4. Look for a replacement structure that does not require sorting, such as questions the reader asks of any option.
5. Delete the framework section outright rather than hedging it with qualifiers.
6. Re-map the body headings to the new structure and confirm each still has a section.
7. Keep a note of why the taxonomy failed, since it is often a genuine insight for the piece.

**Pitfall:** Hedging a broken taxonomy instead of dropping it. A reader can name one product that spans two of your buckets and the article's premise collapses.

**Apply at Pabau:** If a Pabau article's outline depends on sorting competitors into types, test that taxonomy against three competitor sites before the writing stage. Practice-software vendors bundle broadly, so category-based structures often fail the same way.

**Apply anywhere:** Test any category framework against the real products before you build an article on it, and drop the framework entirely when vendors span your buckets rather than hedging it.

### 6. Accept 7x less traffic to bottom-funnel pages and still get 3x the conversions  `132.3`
*core · content insights · source 132*

Grow and Convert address the standard objection head on: bottom-funnel keywords have low volume, so surely the traffic advantage of top-funnel content makes up the difference. In the Geekbot data it does not, and not by a small margin. Top-funnel content pulled 204,303 organic sessions against 28,190 for bottom-funnel, roughly 7x more traffic. But the 25x conversion rate advantage of the bottom-funnel pages more than absorbed that gap: 1,348 conversions from bottom-funnel against 397 from top-funnel. More than 3x the conversions from 7x less traffic. The arithmetic is the point. Any time someone proposes a high-volume top-funnel keyword against a low-volume buying-intent one, multiply volume by the observed conversion rate for that funnel stage on your own site rather than comparing traffic estimates.

> "more than 3X the amount of conversions despite 7X less organic traffic"

**Evidence:** Geekbot: top-funnel content drew 204,303 sessions and produced 397 conversions; bottom-funnel drew 28,190 sessions and produced 1,348 conversions.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Teams whose reporting stops at sessions will keep choosing the high-volume keyword forever, because in a traffic report the top-funnel page always wins. The signal is a blog with rising traffic and flat signups.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau compares a low-volume page like a specific competitor-alternatives article against a high-volume aesthetics guide, David should score both on expected demos rather than expected sessions. Apply each stage's own historical conversion rate from pabau.com to the volume estimate before choosing.

**Apply anywhere:** Score every keyword candidate on expected conversions, not expected sessions. Multiply the volume estimate by the conversion rate your own site has historically achieved at that funnel stage, so a low-volume buying-intent term can beat a high-volume informational one on the page.

### 7. Accept that genuinely good content aimed at the wrong reader is worthless  `122.7`
*core · content insights · source 122*

Grow and Convert's central claim is that content quality and content-customer fit are separate axes. They write that the failing companies have content, that the content may be really good, but that this is irrelevant, because it is not written for their customers. The examples are concrete. A firm selling highly technical development services to enterprise IT and engineering executives published posts titled 21 useful JavaScript tips and tricks, 7 things to stop doing with jQuery and 10 Bootstrap alternatives, when those executives have not written code in years. A UI/UX firm chasing $100,000-plus contracts published a website launch checklist. A B2B SaaS selling testimonial collection to enterprise CMOs published tips on why surveying customers is important, to buyers who already knew that.

> "Their content may be really good, but that's irrelevant"

**Evidence:** Three named client cases: a dev shop writing jQuery tips for IT executives, a UI/UX firm writing a launch checklist for $100,000-plus buyers, and a SaaS telling CMOs that testimonials matter.

**How to do it**

1. List your last 20 published posts with their titles in one column.
2. Next to each, write the single job title the piece is actually useful to.
3. Compare that column against the buyer title from your best-customer profile.
4. Mark every mismatch, however well written the piece is.
5. For mismatches with traffic, decide whether to re-aim the piece upward or leave it as top-funnel with no lead expectation.
6. Block the same failure at commissioning by requiring the brief to name the buyer title.

**Pitfall:** Judging content by editorial quality in review. A well-researched, well-written piece for the wrong seniority level passes every quality check and still generates nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's risk is the same one in aesthetics: publishing beginner explainers about what a treatment is when the buyer is an established practice owner who already knows. David should check whether each blog post assumes a level of knowledge below the clinic owner's.

**Apply anywhere:** Score every published piece on who it is useful to, not just on how good it is. Content written a level below your buyer's knowledge fails no matter how well it is written.

### 8. Add originality nuggets to every post, sized to the keyword  `100.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 100*

Grow and Convert define an originality nugget as a little bit of originality that makes a piece unique from others, and thus worth sharing or linking to. Their argument is that content can drive rankings, traffic and leads without being 10X, without being a taller skyscraper and without being specific, as long as it carries at least one nugget. The framework's value is that it scales with the keyword. A niche term like a vs-competitor comparison needs only light originality, not a six-month, 15,000-word study. A head term needs heavy originality that will look like a 10X piece anyway. They teach this to their agency's writers because sprinkling nuggets is less nerve-racking than trying to produce a game-changing piece every time.

> "Little bits of originality that make a piece of content unique"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite pieces ranking #2 for 'lead generation tools', #4 for 'hiring marketing', #2 for 'how to get a marketing job' and #1 for 'mobile checkout best practices', all built on nuggets rather than length.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the keyword first and judge its competitiveness before deciding effort level.
2. For low-volume, high-intent terms, budget days or weeks, not months.
3. Open the top 10 results and list what angle, format and information each one uses.
4. Write down what none of them has: an arrangement, a viewpoint, a dataset, a first-person account.
5. Choose more than one nugget per piece rather than betting on a single idea.
6. Confirm the nugget is genuinely absent from all 10 results before writing.
7. Sell the nugget in the introduction so a reader sees it in the first paragraph.
8. If you cannot name a nugget, do not write the piece.

**Pitfall:** Teams equate originality with length and produce 3,000-word posts that teach nothing new. The signal is that you can summarize your draft in one sentence that also describes every competitor on page one.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should add an explicit originality-nugget line to every Pabau brief, naming the nugget before drafting starts. On low-competition template and code-reference pages a light nugget is enough; on competitive aesthetic-practice guides plan for a dataset or a practitioner interview.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a named originality nugget to every brief before drafting, and size the effort to the keyword. Light terms get a fresh arrangement or a contrarian angle; competitive terms get original data or first-hand interviews.

### 9. Aggregate client campaign data into ranked vendor benchmark reports  `168.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 168*

CPC Strategy's second content pillar was original data drawn from its own book of business. With clients' permission, the agency indexed campaign performance across the entire roster, then published reports that ranked comparison-shopping engines on benchmarks like functionality and price. Nii Ahene says audiences valued the reports because they were both rare and unique, and that the specificity is what earned the agency industry authority. The strongest proof of value he gives is unusual: master's and PhD candidates contacted CPC Strategy asking for the raw data to use in their own research. That is the tell that a dataset has crossed from marketing asset into reference material, and reference material is what other people cite and link to.

> "because they were both rare and unique"

**Evidence:** Nii Ahene reports that people pursuing master's degrees and PhDs contacted CPC Strategy asking for its raw information to research the space.

**How to do it**

1. Get written permission from clients to use their performance data in aggregate and anonymized form, and put the clause in the standard contract.
2. Build one indexed store of campaign or account performance across every client so you can query it, rather than pulling numbers per project.
3. Choose two or three benchmark dimensions your readers argue about, such as price and functionality, and define exactly how you measure each.
4. Rank every vendor or option in your category on those dimensions and publish the ranking with the sample size and date range stated.
5. Publish the methodology in full so an academic or journalist can judge and reuse it.
6. Repeat the report on a fixed cadence, annual or quarterly, so the numbers become a time series people track.
7. Offer the raw or summarized dataset on request, and log every requester as a link and relationship prospect.

**Pitfall:** Publishing aggregate client data without explicit written permission, or with a sample small enough that individual clients are identifiable, turns a flagship asset into a contract breach.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sits on aggregated practice data: no-show rates, rebooking rates, average treatment prices by region, busiest booking hours. With consent and anonymization, an annual aesthetic practice benchmark report would be the most linkable asset on pabau.com and would feed every blog article with a citable original figure.

**Apply anywhere:** With written client permission, index performance data across your whole book of business and publish ranked vendor benchmarks on two or three dimensions your readers argue about. State the methodology and sample so journalists and researchers can reuse it.

### 10. Answer four differentiator questions on every bottom-of-funnel page  `81.4`
*core · best practices · source 81*

Grow and Convert argue that AI acts as a salesperson: when an LLM searches the web it consumes your content and uses it to communicate your value propositions to the user, so what you publish directly shapes how the model describes you. If the content lacks detail on how you beat the competition, the model has nothing to work with and defaults to the same generic language it applies to every other option. They name four questions the content must answer. How are we better than the competition. What problems do we solve. Who do we serve best. In which scenarios or use cases do we truly shine. The fourth is the one most pages skip, and it is the one that lets a model match you to a user's specific described situation.

> "In which scenarios or use cases do we truly shine?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show a ChatGPT answer describing Constitution Lending in nearly identical language to the company's own blog post passage.

**How to do it**

1. Add the four questions as required headings in your content brief template.
2. For 'better than the competition', name the rival category and the specific measurable difference, not an adjective.
3. For 'what problems do we solve', use the pain-point wording taken from customer interviews.
4. For 'who do we serve best', name the segment by size, type and situation, and also name who you are not for.
5. For 'which scenarios', write three to five concrete use-case paragraphs describing a customer situation and the outcome.
6. Reject any draft where a rival could paste their name over the copy without it becoming false.
7. Audit existing bottom-of-funnel pages against the four questions and rewrite the ones missing scenarios.
8. Re-prompt an LLM with each scenario a month later and check whether the returned description matches your wording.

**Pitfall:** Answering all four in vague superlatives. If the copy is generic the model still cites you but describes you identically to everyone else, so you win the mention and lose the recommendation.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's template and blog pages should each carry an explicit 'who this is for' and a scenarios section naming practice types and situations, for example single-room injectors versus multi-location clinics. Generic feature lists give the models nothing to differentiate Pabau from other practice management vendors.

**Apply anywhere:** Make every bottom-of-funnel page answer four questions explicitly: how you beat the competition, what problems you solve, who you serve best, and in which scenarios you shine. The scenarios section is what lets a model match you to a user's described situation.

### 11. Answer the reader's why questions after every claim you make  `99.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 99*

Khanal's worked example is the claim 'measuring conversions from content marketing is important.' He argues that stating it alone is poor writing because a reasonably smart reader immediately has questions the article leaves unanswered: should you measure all content or only bottom-of-funnel, using what tool, last click or first click and what is the difference, how do you define a conversion, and why worry about conversions at all when content is about traffic. The answers to those questions are the backup detail. His method is to write out the questions a knowledgeable reader would raise at each claim, then answer them in the draft. He shows three answers that each work: explaining how with Google Analytics steps, explaining why the claim is contrarian, and giving a client figure of 100,000 monthly pageviews producing only 30 signups.

> "a bunch of "why" questions that the article doesn't answer"

**Evidence:** Khanal's example rewrite: 'we worked with one client that had 100,000 monthly pageviews to their blog but were generating only 30 signups a month from it (a 0.03% conversion rate).'

**How to do it**

1. After writing a claim, stop and list five questions a knowledgeable reader would ask about it.
2. Include at least one definitional question (what counts as a conversion) and one tooling question (measured with what).
3. Include one scope question: does this apply everywhere or only to part of the reader's work.
4. Include one objection question that a skeptical reader would raise against the claim.
5. Answer each question in the draft, or delete the question if it is genuinely out of scope for the article.
6. Where an answer is a procedure, write the steps rather than promising to walk through them later.
7. Where an answer is a number, use one you actually have from a client or your own data.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Writers answer only the easy how question and skip the objection and definitional ones, which are the ones that prove expertise. If the answers all restate the benefit, the claim has still not been backed.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau articles, run the five-question list at every H2 claim. On a page about no-show reduction, that means naming the actual setting, the tool, what counts as a no-show and the objection that reminders annoy patients.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a five-question list at every major claim in your article. Name the actual setting, the actual tool, the definition you are using, and the objection a skeptical reader would raise.

### 12. Answer whether you have unique ideas before commissioning thought leadership  `125.1`
*core · best practices · source 125*

Grow and Convert argue that thought leadership content is by definition content built on unique or compelling ideas, so the first question is not a format question but an honesty question: does the company actually have such ideas? They say that for most companies the answer is no, because most are selling products and running services largely similar to everyone else, and they note there is nothing wrong with that. But demanding thought leadership content when no innovation is happening in the organization produces nothing. They then soften it: most companies have at least one detail their product improved on, or founders with provocative opinions that were never written down. The action is to run the audit before the brief, so the content team is not asked to invent ideas the business does not hold.

> "you have to have unique or interesting"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's experience across their agency clients: for most companies asking for thought leadership, the honest answer to whether they hold unique ideas is no.

**How to do it**

1. Before approving a thought leadership program, write down every idea in the business that competitors do not also hold.
2. List the one detail the product does differently from every rival, even if it is small.
3. Ask founders and leadership for opinions they hold that are not written down anywhere yet.
4. If the list is empty, cancel the thought leadership brief and run a normal SEO content plan instead.
5. If the list has one item, plan a single deep piece around it rather than a content series.
6. Assign each surviving idea to the person who actually holds it, not to a writer.
7. Re-run the audit each quarter as the product ships new differentiation.

**Pitfall:** Teams skip the audit, brief an agency on thought leadership anyway, and get beautifully designed me-too content. The signal is a finished ebook nobody in the company can summarize in one non-obvious sentence.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before David plans any Pabau thought leadership piece, list what Pabau does that competing practice management vendors do not, in the words of the product and support teams. If the list is thin for a topic, publish a standard intent-matched article instead of a manifesto.

**Apply anywhere:** Before commissioning thought leadership, list the ideas your business genuinely holds that competitors do not. If that list is empty, publish ordinary intent-matched content instead and stop asking writers to invent a point of view.

### 13. Ask four pain-point questions before writing anything for the profile  `122.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 122*

Grow and Convert's last research step is learning everything about the narrowed list of ideal customers. They ask four questions: what problems the company faced before working with you, how those problems were solved once they started, what other problems and challenges these people face in their roles beyond what you solve, and what publications they read and who they look up to in the industry. They note the company usually cannot answer these, particularly the last one, so it requires ongoing research on the agency's side: emails, surveys, reading customer service chats and talking to more people at the account. Their stated attitude when this feels hard is that you are a marketer and this is your job. The output replaces a marketer's hunch with real data.

> "What publications do these people read?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say answering these usually requires ongoing customer research: emails, surveys, customer service chats and more interviews at the account.

**How to do it**

1. Take only the narrowed ideal-customer list into this step, not the full customer base.
2. Email or call those accounts and ask what problems they had before buying.
3. Ask how those problems were solved after they started, in their words.
4. Ask what other challenges they face in their role that you do not solve.
5. Ask which publications they read and who they look up to in the industry.
6. Mine customer service chat logs and support tickets for the same answers where interviews are not possible.
7. Run a survey to the same segment for the questions interviews did not cover.
8. Write every distinct pain point into a list, each with a verbatim customer phrase attached.

**Pitfall:** Assuming the client or your own team can answer these from memory. Grow and Convert say the company usually does not know, especially about which publications the buyer reads, so a workshop answer here is a guess dressed as data.

**Apply at Pabau:** The question about problems Pabau does not solve is the highest-value one for the blog. Those adjacent clinic problems, staffing, marketing, compliance, are legitimate article topics that reach the right owner without stretching the product claim.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview the narrowed best-customer list on four things: the problem before buying, how it resolved, adjacent problems you do not solve, and what they read. Use chats and surveys where interviews fail.

### 14. Ask the clinician how patients describe the symptom, then open with it  `117.3`
*core · content insights · source 117*

The most valuable material out of the Cognitive FX interview was not clinical. Dr. Jaycie Loewen, a clinical neuroscientist who talks to patients daily, could supply the exact words patients use for their headaches: a tight band around the head, the head blowing up like a balloon, constant pressure, being pulled apart between the eyes. Grow and Convert opened the article's first paragraph with those phrases. One reader commented that she almost cried when she started reading it. The mechanism is recognition: a searcher who cannot name their symptom sees their own experience written down and trusts the page immediately. No amount of self-research surfaces this, because the phrasing lives in consultations, not in published literature.

> "the exact words and phrases that her patients use to describe"

**Evidence:** A reader named Myrna commented "I almost cried when I started to read this article." The post went on to produce leads for a $13,000 treatment program.

**Pitfall:** Writers paraphrase patient language into clinical register and lose the recognition effect entirely.

**Apply at Pabau:** Ask Pabau's clinic customers what their patients actually say when they ask about a treatment, and open the relevant blog articles with those verbatim phrases rather than a definition.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask your customer-facing experts for the literal phrases customers use to describe the problem, then open the article with those phrases instead of a definition.

### 15. Ask what makes this piece better than the first page, per article  `128.12`
*core · best practices · source 128*

Grow and Convert make one explicit effort per article: to make it better than every other result on the first page for the target keyword. Devesh says having that mindset is what produces their rankings. The mechanism is a question asked for every single piece, not once for the site: what would make this article unique or better than the rest of the articles in the search results. They name the answers they typically land on, which makes the question answerable rather than aspirational. Making the article easier to navigate if it is a list. Finding a better subject-matter expert if it is an opinion or how-to piece. Adding an originality nugget. The rule that follows is that the answer is chosen before drafting, matched to the format, and it is a specific edge over the specific pages currently ranking.

> "what would make the article unique/better than the rest"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert ask for every article what would make it unique or better than the current first-page results, and credit that habit for their rankings.

**How to do it**

1. Before drafting, open the first page for the target keyword and note the format of each ranking result.
2. Write one sentence stating what will make your piece better than those specific pages.
3. If it is a list, make navigability the edge: jump links, a comparison table, a consistent per-item structure.
4. If it is an opinion or how-to piece, make the edge a better-qualified subject-matter expert than the ranking pages used.
5. If neither applies, make the edge an originality nugget, meaning data, a case or a tactic that does not appear on page one.
6. Put that stated edge in the brief and hold the draft against it at review.
7. Do not publish until the reviewer can name the edge without being told it.
8. Revisit the edge if the SERP changes format after publication.

**Pitfall:** Answering the question at site level instead of per article. A general commitment to quality produces no concrete edge over the pages actually ranking, and the piece publishes interchangeable.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau brief should carry one sentence naming the edge over the specific pages ranking today, and the fact-check reviewer should be able to identify that edge from the article alone.

**Apply anywhere:** For every article, name in one sentence what will make it better than the specific pages currently ranking. Use navigability for lists, a better-qualified expert for opinion and how-to pieces, and an original data point or case otherwise. Put it in the brief and hold the draft against it.

### 16. Attack keywords at the most specific angle available, not with an overview  `128.9`
*core · best practices · source 128*

Grow and Convert call this the Specificity Strategy and treat it as the first decision, made when picking the topic, keyword or angle rather than during writing. Their position is that specificity fundamentally leads to higher-quality content, and they contrast it with what they see most marketers still doing: prioritizing general overview pieces titled "Beginner's guide to" or "Overview of". The strategy pairs with their pain point SEO approach, which prioritizes medium to long-tail keywords, and those are more specific by nature than head terms. The practical consequence is that the specificity decision is upstream of the brief. You cannot rescue an overview angle with better writing, because the angle itself is what makes the piece interchangeable with the rest of the SERP.

> "we make sure to get as specific as possible"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name the Specificity Strategy as the first of three content principles and tie it to pain point SEO's preference for medium and long-tail keywords.

**How to do it**

1. Before writing a brief, restate the keyword as the narrowest real situation a reader could be in when searching it.
2. Reject any working title starting with "Beginner's guide to", "Overview of", "Ultimate guide" or "What is" unless the SERP is genuinely all definitions.
3. Prefer medium and long-tail keywords over head terms, since the tail forces specificity.
4. Name the specific reader, specific setting and specific decision the piece serves, and put those in the brief.
5. Check the top ten for the angle: if three or more are general overviews, take a specific angle rather than a better overview.
6. Make the specificity visible in the title and H1, not just in the body.
7. Kill the piece at brief stage if you cannot state an angle narrower than the SERP's.

**Pitfall:** Choosing the specific angle at drafting time instead of at topic selection. By then the keyword and outline already commit you to an overview, and better prose will not make an interchangeable piece rank.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should state the exact practice type, treatment and decision behind each keyword before an outline exists, and generic "guide to" angles should be rejected at the brief stage rather than fixed in editing.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide the angle's specificity at topic selection, not at drafting. Restate the keyword as the narrowest real situation a searcher could be in, prefer medium and long-tail terms, and reject generic guide or overview angles before an outline exists.

### 17. Audit a legacy keyword library for passages that survive chunking  `76.21`
*core · concrete actions · source 76*

The paper explains why big established content libraries underperform in answer engines. Large brands' legacy SEO libraries, optimized for high-volume keywords, often produce few passages that survive chunking and pairwise comparison for specific long-tail queries, because that content covers topics without addressing the precise attribute intersections agentic queries target. This is a different diagnosis from thin content or stale content. The pages can rank, be well written and still contribute almost nothing to retrieval, because no individual chunk of them resolves a specific sub-question. The paper's remedy follows from its three retrieval principles: each surviving chunk needs self-containment, dense factual encoding and a header naming the sub-query it answers. Auditing for that is a chunk-level exercise, not a page-level one, so page-level content scores will not surface the problem.

> "often produce few passages that survive chunking and pairwise comparison"

**Evidence:** The paper attributes large brands' AEO weakness to legacy libraries whose content covers topics without addressing the precise attribute intersections that agentic queries target.

**How to do it**

1. Take your highest-traffic legacy guides and split each into its H2 and H3 sections as separate text blocks.
2. For each block, write the one question it answers; if you cannot, mark it for deletion or rewrite.
3. Mark any block that fails self-containment because it back-references earlier sections.
4. Mark any block under 100 words of substance or with no checkable fact as failing density.
5. Count the surviving blocks per page; a long guide yielding two or three is the problem case.
6. Rewrite the failed blocks around the specific attribute intersections buyers ask about, one block per intersection.
7. Re-test by asking an engine those specific questions and checking whether your page is now retrieved.

**Pitfall:** Running a page-level content audit and passing. The unit of retrieval is the passage, so a page can score well overall while nearly every chunk in it fails.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's older long-form guides should be audited chunk by chunk, counting how many sections answer a specific clinic-owner question on their own, then rewriting the ones that answer none.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit older long-form guides chunk by chunk, counting how many sections answer a specific buyer question on their own, then rewrite the ones that answer none.

### 18. Audit each draft claim for a backup detail before publishing  `99.1`
*core · best practices · source 99*

Devesh Khanal of Grow and Convert says every blog article is a series of claims, and what separates good posts from bad is not the claims but the details that prove, back up or add color to them. Bad posts list claim after claim with no explanation, which reads to the reader as the same thesis restated over and over. He names six kinds of backup detail: explaining why, showing how, giving an example or case study, providing data, showing the claim is contrarian, and showing the claim is unique. His editing pass is mechanical. Mark every claim in the draft, then confirm each carries at least one of those six. He warns the business consequence is commercial, not just stylistic: an unbacked article reduces trust and the likelihood a reader converts.

> "Good blog posts have unique details to back up claims"

**Evidence:** Khanal derived the pattern from reading freelance portfolio pieces, test outlines and test drafts across a hiring round plus client edits.

**How to do it**

1. Read the draft once and underline every sentence that asserts something, including sub-claims inside a how-to step.
2. For each underlined claim, tag which of the six backup types follows it: why, how, example or case study, data, contrarian, unique.
3. Flag any claim whose next sentence only restates it in different words and delete that restatement.
4. For claims with no backup type at all, write the missing detail or cut the claim.
5. Prefer original details you own (client numbers, your own process) over borrowed ones.
6. Re-read the section and check the claim-to-detail ratio has moved toward detail, not toward more claims.
7. Repeat on the intro, where unbacked claims cluster most.

**Pitfall:** Writers pad an unbacked claim with a rephrasing of itself, often a rhetorical question or a cutesy line. Khanal says he sees this all the time and it clutters the writing without adding anything; the signal is two consecutive sentences that a reader could delete one of with no loss.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a claim-backup pass to the Pabau editorial checklist, run after the draft and before the block contract pass. On every blog and template article, each claim about aesthetic-practice workflows needs a why, a how, a named example or a figure attached to it.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a claim-backup pass to your editorial checklist, run after the draft and before formatting. Every claim in the piece needs a why, a how, a named example or a figure sitting right next to it.

### 19. Audit each post against the best-customer profile line by line  `122.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 122*

Grow and Convert demonstrate the audit with FundersClub, a VC firm that had funded over 200 startups including Instacart and Coinbase. The best-customer exercise identified serial founders who had been through top incubators such as Y Combinator, 500 Startups or Techstars, had already raised a seed or angel round, and showed defined traction metrics. FundersClub were publishing a Series A fundraising checklist. On the surface it looked like ideal content for early-stage companies. Scrolling the bullets showed the mismatch: the piece explained that Series A rounds usually involve institutional venture investors, that founders need to present financials and practice their presentation. A serial founder out of a known incubator knows all of that already.

> "which we argued is mirage content"

**Evidence:** FundersClub's Series A checklist passed the title test and failed the bullet-level test against a best-customer profile of serial, incubator-backed founders.

**How to do it**

1. Write the best-customer profile on one page and keep it visible during the audit.
2. Open a candidate post and read the actual bullet points and subheads, not the title.
3. For each bullet, ask whether the profiled buyer already knows this.
4. Mark the post as mirage content if the majority of bullets are things the buyer already knows.
5. Do not judge on the title alone; the FundersClub title looked correct and the body did not.
6. Sort the audit output into keep, raise the level, and retire.
7. Feed the failures back into the brief template so the same level mismatch is not commissioned again.

**Pitfall:** Auditing by headline. A title can match the topic and the audience perfectly while the body is pitched several levels below the buyer, which is exactly what happened with the Series A checklist.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this bullet-level audit on Pabau's treatment and compliance explainers. If the H2s tell a clinic owner things they learned in their first month of practice, the piece needs raising rather than refreshing.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit content by reading the bullets against a written best-customer profile and asking whether that buyer already knows each point. Titles pass this test far more often than bodies do.

### 20. Audit product copy against the four value-prop mistakes before publishing  `92.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 92*

Grow and Convert reviewed portfolio samples from thousands of writer applicants and found one pattern separating bad writing from good: the writer gets the value propositions wrong. They break it into four failure modes, in escalating subtlety. Selling the wrong value props, where the writer picks features that do not matter. Selling non-existent value props, which they call fluff. Missing important value props entirely. And selling a good value prop the wrong way, meaning the feature is right but the positioning undersells it. All four trace to the same root cause: the writer does not understand customer pain points, so guesses. They call the fix Pain Point Copywriting, the copy-level twin of their Pain Point SEO keyword framework. Run all four checks as a named editing pass, not as a vague quality read.

> "we've noticed 4 patterns in messing up value props"

**Evidence:** Pattern identified across portfolio samples from thousands of writer applicants and test projects at Grow and Convert's content agency.

**How to do it**

1. Open the draft and list every value prop it claims, one per line, with the product it belongs to.
2. For each, mark whether a customer-facing employee has actually named it as a buying reason; delete or demote the ones nobody named.
3. Flag any adjective or phrase that is true of every product in the category (cloud-based, full-fledged, multilingual) as fluff and cut it or defend it with evidence.
4. List the pain points you know from interviews and check each one appears in the draft; add a section for any that is missing.
5. For each correct value prop, read the sentence that introduces it and ask whether it is positioned as a core reason to buy or as an optional perk.
6. Rewrite any perk-framed sentence so it names the pain the feature removes.
7. Re-read the first sentence of every product description specifically, because that is where fluff concentrates.
8. Do not sign off until each of the four checks has been run separately.

**Pitfall:** Teams run this as one general quality read and catch only the obvious fluff. The subtle failure, a correct value prop positioned casually, survives every time because the sentence is technically accurate.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a four-check value-prop pass to Pabau's editorial stage for every listicle and comparison article. For each competitor and for Pabau itself, the reviewer confirms the named benefits are ones the sales and support teams actually hear, not ones lifted from the vendor's own site.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a four-check value-prop pass to your editorial stage for every listicle and comparison article. For each product covered, confirm the named benefits are ones your customer-facing staff actually hear, not ones lifted from the vendor's own website.

### 21. Audit the existing inventory in the first 30 days for page-two BOTF posts  `107.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 107*

Grow and Convert say nine times out of ten you need an entirely new piece to rank for a specific target keyword, and always for originality-led topics. But they flag a separate question for sites with dozens or hundreds of older posts: keep producing or go back and update. Their answer is do both, prioritized by current performance and by whether the conversion potential is real. Posts already ranking on page two or the bottom of page one are usually faster to lift into the top three than starting from scratch, especially when they target a high-buying-intent keyword. They say they audit a client's existing content in the first 30 days of an engagement to find the posts that are one or two updates away from meaningful gains. They also warn against the refresh-everything reflex, since optimizing a top-of-funnel post that was never going to convert does not fix the underlying strategy problem.

> "one or two updates away from meaningful ranking gains"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run this audit inside the first 30 days of every client engagement.

**How to do it**

1. Export every existing blog URL with its best ranking position and its conversions from GSC and analytics.
2. Filter to posts ranking between roughly position 5 and 20.
3. From that set, keep only the posts whose target keyword shows real buying intent.
4. Rank the survivors by conversion potential and pick the top ten for updating.
5. For each, read the current top three results and list what your post is missing.
6. Ship the updates before commissioning any new posts on adjacent topics.
7. Leave low-intent top-of-funnel posts alone rather than refreshing them out of habit.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** The refresh-everything reflex burns the quarter on top-of-funnel posts that were never going to convert, so rankings improve and pipeline does not.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog has a long tail of older posts. David should run this audit across the English blog, keep only page-two-or-lower posts on evaluation-intent keywords, and update those before commissioning new pieces.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your existing posts early, isolate the ones ranking on page two or the bottom of page one that target buying-intent keywords, and update those first rather than refreshing everything.

### 22. Audit your blog for mirage content before commissioning anything new  `97.1`
*core · best practices · source 97*

Grow and Convert define mirage content as content that looks good on the surface but is high-level fluff once you read it. The article's diagnostic is that three complaints all trace back to the same cause: nobody links to our pieces, our content looks fine but gets no traffic, and we get traffic but no leads. They say it is extremely common, found in deep-dive reviews of dozens of company blogs, and that it stems from two correlated causes: a writer without real experience in the subject, and a lack of specificity. The practical move is to audit what you already publish against those two causes before adding more, because publishing more of the same fluff makes the trust problem worse rather than better.

> "content that looks good on the surface"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report finding mirage content in deep-dive reviews of dozens of company blogs over a few months.

**How to do it**

1. List every post published in the last 12 months in a sheet with columns for author, author's hands-on experience with the topic, and whether the post contains a named real example.
2. Read the first 300 words of each and mark any that open with a definition or a fact your target reader already knows.
3. Mark any post whose advice items could be written by someone who had never done the task, such as 'optimize your website for search engines' or 'update your content'.
4. Count the concrete artifacts per post: named companies, real numbers, screenshots, dated results. Flag anything with zero.
5. Sort the flagged posts by traffic and rewrite the highest-traffic ones first, since those are doing the most reputational damage.
6. Add the same three checks (experience, specificity, real example) as gates on every new brief so the backlog stops growing.

**Pitfall:** Teams audit for word count, keyword coverage and formatting, all of which mirage content passes. The signal you have hit it is a post that scores well on every SEO checklist and still produces no links and no leads.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this audit across pabau.com/blog/, flagging any article whose advice a practice manager already knows, such as generic 'keep good records' guidance. Those are the pages to rewrite first, ahead of publishing new ones.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your existing blog for surface-level fluff before commissioning new posts, flagging any article whose advice your reader already knows and rewriting the highest-traffic offenders first.

### 23. Back pain points with a scenario and a consequence, not a restatement  `99.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 99*

Khanal separates backing up claims from backing up pain points, which he says earns a different benefit: emotional connection and relatability. A poor article states the pain point and moves on, or restates it as a rhetorical flourish like 'If only digital marketing were easy!' A strong article paints the detail behind it in two specific moves. First a detailed example of the pain point in action: a reader sees a blog post, then two ads, then a webinar, then converts, so which channel gets credit. Second the consequence of the pain point: you end up without clear numbers for leadership and they devalue content marketing as a channel. He says these are not cutesy rephrasings but real-life scenarios and consequences that prove the author knows the details behind the pain, and that this is what earns the reader's attention.

> "they actually describe real life scenarios and consequences"

**Evidence:** Khanal's paired example: the attribution scenario (blog post, two ads, webinar, conversion) plus the consequence (no clear numbers for leadership, content marketing devalued).

**How to do it**

1. Write the pain point as a plain one-line statement first.
2. Under it, write a concrete sequence of events showing the pain happening to a specific person doing a specific job.
3. Use real objects in that sequence: the tool they open, the report they cannot produce, the meeting they have to sit in.
4. Then write the downstream consequence: what the reader loses, who else notices, and what decision gets made because of it.
5. Delete any sentence between those two that only rephrases the pain point.
6. Check the passage names things only someone in that role would know about.
7. Repeat for each pain point the article introduces, not just the headline one.

**Pitfall:** Rhetorical questions and clever phrasing pass as pain-point writing in review because they sound sympathetic. The test is whether the passage could have been written by someone who has never done the reader's job.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog intros should replace generic 'admin is time-consuming' lines with the sequence a clinic manager actually lives: the phone rings during a treatment, the paper diary is in another room, the double-booking surfaces at 4pm. Then the consequence, such as a refunded appointment.

**Apply anywhere:** Replace generic pain statements in your intros with the sequence your reader actually lives through, using the tools and moments specific to their job, followed by the consequence it produces for them.
