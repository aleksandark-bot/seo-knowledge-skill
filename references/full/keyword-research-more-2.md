### 1. Group only near-identical SERP-and-intent keywords on one page  `41.6`
*useful · best practices · source 41*

Edward's stated exception to never targeting multiple keywords with one page is when a group of keywords share both very similar SERPs, meaning the same set of competing pages and result types show up, and very similar underlying search intent. In that specific case, and only that case, it's fine to target the whole group of similar keywords with a single dedicated page rather than needing a separate page per keyword. Even in this exception, the rule is still to use a dedicated page for the keyword group, never the homepage itself. He adds a link-building nuance on top: only invest in building backlinks directly to that grouped page if the combined keyword group is genuinely competitive, otherwise route backlinks to a hub page that houses multiple such SEO landing pages, so authority flows down through internal links instead.

> "the search engine results pages for all of these keywords is really"

**How to do it**

1. Before creating a separate page for every individual keyword variation, check whether a cluster of related keywords returns near-identical search engine results pages, meaning the same competing pages and result types appear for each.
2. Also confirm the underlying search intent is genuinely the same across that keyword cluster, not just similar-looking keyword text.
3. If both SERP overlap and intent match closely, consolidate that keyword cluster onto a single dedicated page rather than the homepage and rather than splitting it across multiple thin pages.
4. Assess whether the combined keyword cluster is competitive enough to justify direct link-building investment; if yes, build backlinks straight to that consolidated page.
5. If the cluster is not especially competitive, instead route backlink-building effort to a hub page that houses this and other similar SEO landing pages, letting internal links pass authority down.
6. Re-verify periodically that the keyword cluster's SERPs haven't diverged over time, since if they split apart, splitting the content back into separate dedicated pages may become the better structure again (inferred).

**Pitfall:** Don't consolidate keywords onto one page just because they look similar as text - confirming both the SERPs and the search intent are genuinely similar is required first; keywords that read similarly but pull different SERP result-types or serve different intents still need their own dedicated pages.

### 2. Harvest new competitor names from the comparison keyword rows  `118.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 118*

Grow and Convert flag a side benefit of the comparison and alternative bucket that most teams miss. When you pull every 'alternatives', 'competitors' and 'vs' row out of a gap report, you get a list of brand names, and some of them will be companies you did not know you competed with, either in the market or only in the search results. The rows also surface your competitors' competitors, which tells you who they consider a threat. Their example is the help desk space, where 'Zendesk alternatives' and 'Zendesk vs' rows expose the full set of brands buyers weigh against each other. The output is a refreshed competitor list, which then feeds back into the next gap run and into which alternatives pages you build.

> "because you can discover new brands"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's help desk example: 'Zendesk alternatives' and 'Zendesk vs' rows expose the brands buyers compare in that market.

**How to do it**

1. Filter the gap report for 'alternatives', 'competitors', 'vs' and 'versus' and extract every brand name in the matching rows.
2. Deduplicate the names against your existing competitor list and mark the ones that are new to you.
3. For each new name, check whether it is a real market competitor, a search-only competitor, or an unrelated product sharing vocabulary.
4. Note which brands appear most often in your competitors' comparison rows, since those are who they treat as the threat.
5. Add the confirmed market competitors to the next content gap run as additional inputs.
6. Build a '[brand] alternatives' page for each confirmed competitor, and consider inserting yourself into their 'X vs Y' comparisons.
7. Re-run the harvest quarterly, since new entrants show up in comparison keywords before they show up in category ones.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Adding every discovered brand to the gap run bloats the report with rows from products you do not compete with. Confirm each name is a real alternative for your buyer before it becomes an input.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should harvest brand names from the alternatives and versus rows each quarter, confirm which are genuinely pitched against Pabau in aesthetic practices, and add an alternatives page for each confirmed one.

**Apply anywhere:** Extract every brand name from the alternatives and versus rows of a gap report, confirm which are real competitors, then feed them into the next gap run and build an alternatives page for each.

### 3. Head keywords like IBM's "AI" ranking only come after enough authority  `23.9`
*useful · content insights · source 23*

Edward marks going after the highest-volume, most lucrative 'head' keywords as something that only becomes viable once a site has accumulated enough authority, using IBM's ranking for broad, massive-volume terms like 'AI,' 'AI agent,' and 'what is AI' as the concrete proof point — single-word or near-single-word categories with enormous competition and search volume that only an extremely high-authority domain could realistically contest. He references a dedicated episode of his own podcast as the deeper breakdown of the tactics IBM used to take over these keywords.

> "the highest volume, most lucrative head keywords that require the most"

**Evidence:** IBM ranking for extremely broad, high-volume head terms including 'AI,' 'AI agent,' and 'what is AI,' cited as the benchmark example of what pursuing head keywords looks like once a site has enough authority.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should treat head-term keyword targets (broad, single-concept, massive-volume queries relevant to practice management software) as a later-stage goal to plan for as Pabau's domain authority grows, rather than a near-term target, and should study the referenced IBM approach (topical breadth plus authority-building) as a model for how an established B2B brand can eventually contest these terms.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat head-term keyword targets (broad, single-concept, massive-volume queries in your category) as a later-stage goal to plan for as your domain authority grows, rather than a near-term target, and study the referenced IBM approach (topical breadth plus authority-building) as a model for how an established B2B brand can eventually contest these terms.

### 4. Ignore volume thresholds on competitor and alternatives keywords  `142.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 142*

Grow and Convert note that many competitor and alternatives keywords carry very low organic search volume, and point to their own mini-volume keywords argument as the justification for targeting them regardless. The math is straightforward. At an 8.43% average conversion rate, a term getting 50 searches a month that you rank first for still produces meaningful demo requests, while a 5,000-search informational term converting at 0.6% produces fewer product conversions from a hundred times the traffic. This runs directly against the common filter of dropping anything under a volume floor of a few hundred searches. Grow and Convert also add that the spread within this group is wide, and that direct competitors beat loosely related ones, so the selection criterion is competitor proximity rather than volume.

> "conversion rates make them very worthwhile to target"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report an 8.43% average across 23 competitor and alternatives keywords despite many having very low organic search volume.

**How to do it**

1. Remove any minimum search volume filter from your competitor and alternatives keyword list.
2. Rank the candidates by how directly the named competitor substitutes for your product.
3. For each, estimate monthly conversions as volume times a 4% floor rather than the 8.43% average.
4. Compare that figure against your informational candidates at a 0.6% to 1% rate.
5. Commission in that order, accepting terms in the tens of searches per month.
6. Defend the choice internally by reporting conversions per post, never sessions per post.

**Pitfall:** A volume floor of 100 or 500 searches a month deletes the highest-converting group on the site before anyone reads it. The signal you have hit this is a keyword list with no competitor names on it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's keyword research should keep competitor-named terms even at very low volume, and David should report those pages on demo requests rather than sessions so they survive traffic-based audits.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep competitor-named terms in your research even at very low volume, and report those pages on sales actions rather than sessions so they survive traffic-based audits.

### 5. Interview your sales team when customer interviews are not possible  `90.2`
*useful · concrete actions · source 90*

Grow and Convert name a shortcut for teams that cannot get customers on a call: ask the same five questions to the sales team and anyone else in a customer-facing role. They say this is in fact what they do with all of their clients, so it is the default rather than the fallback. Sales staff hear the problem statement in the buyer's own words dozens of times a week, know which competitors come up in deals, and know the objections that decide whether a deal closes. The output is the same list of pain points, competitor names and phrasings, gathered in one meeting instead of over weeks of scheduling. The caveat is that sales hears the pre-purchase story and not the post-purchase value, so questions four and five still benefit from at least a handful of real customer responses.

> "a shortcut to talking directly with your customers is to talk to your sales team"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report this is what they do with all of their clients, not just those without customer access.

**How to do it**

1. Book one 45-minute session with two or three sales reps and one support or onboarding rep.
2. Ask the same five customer questions, phrased as 'what do buyers tell you', so answers stay in the buyer's language.
3. Ask specifically which competitor names come up on calls and in what order of frequency.
4. Write down verbatim phrasings rather than paraphrases; the exact words become keyword candidates.
5. Tally which problems and competitors were named by more than one rep and drop the singletons.
6. Run the surviving phrases through your keyword tool, but keep the ones with no reported volume as candidates anyway.
7. Follow up with five to ten real customers on the benefits and search-terms questions, since sales cannot answer those from experience.

**Pitfall:** Sales reps describe the product the way they pitch it, not the way buyers describe it. If the answers come back in your own marketing vocabulary, push for the exact sentence the buyer used on the last call.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's sales and onboarding teams are the fastest source of competitor names and pain-point phrasing for the blog. David should schedule one session and use the output to prioritize alternatives pages and jobs-to-be-done articles.

**Apply anywhere:** When customer interviews are hard to arrange, run the same five questions with your sales and support staff instead, capturing verbatim buyer phrasing and the competitor names that come up most often.

### 6. Jobs-to-be-done queries carry buying intent because the problem is live  `113.14`
*useful · content insights · source 113*

Grow and Convert defend jobs-to-be-done keywords as bottom-funnel on a timing argument rather than a phrasing one. These are queries where the searcher has a problem your product solves but is not searching your category or your competitors: how to manage sales leads, how to keep track of staff holidays, how to plan the shortest route for multiple destinations. They also include variants such as ways to manage multiple projects, can I do my own taxes, and should I hire an interior designer. The distinction they draw is between learning and having a problem right now. Someone searching project management tips is learning. Someone searching how to manage multiple construction projects at once has a real problem and may be evaluating whether to solve it manually or with software. Some fraction of that second group is open to buying, which is why the bucket converts.

> "don't need to be led down a buyer"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contrast project management tips, a learning query, with how to manage multiple construction projects at once, a live problem query.

**Pitfall:** Treating every how-to as bottom-funnel. The test is whether the query describes a problem happening now rather than a subject being studied, and tips-style phrasing usually fails it.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, how to track client consent forms or how to reduce no-shows at a clinic are live problems worth building. How to start an aesthetics career is a learning query and should not be commissioned.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge a how-to query by whether it describes a problem happening now or a subject being studied. Live-problem queries carry buying intent; tips-style learning queries do not.

### 7. Judge domain rating relative to the SERP you are entering, not as a number  `133.7`
*useful · best practices · source 133*

Grow and Convert started with Circuit at an Ahrefs domain rating of 18, against a normal client range of 30 to 60. Their framing avoids the usual mistake of treating DR as a threshold. They quote Tim Soulo that there is no hard and fast rule about needing a certain domain rating to rank, because DR only measures backlink profile strength. The workable rule they give is comparative: the lower your score is relative to the other sites in the SERPs for the keywords you are going after, the harder ranking will be relative to them. They also say the same reasoning holds with Moz or Semrush equivalents, since all of these are 0 to 100 proxies for backlink strength. So DR is an input to keyword selection, page by page, rather than a gate on whether to do SEO at all.

> "the lower the score is relative to other sites in the SERPs"

**Evidence:** Circuit began at Ahrefs DR 18 against Grow and Convert's usual 30 to 60 client range, and still reached 39 keywords in positions 1 to 3 and 92 on page one.

**How to do it**

1. Pull your own domain rating in Ahrefs and record it as the baseline.
2. For each candidate keyword, pull the top 10 ranking domains and their domain ratings.
3. Compare your rating against the median of that top 10, not against an absolute threshold.
4. Prioritize keywords where several ranking pages sit at or below your own rating.
5. Deprioritize, but do not delete, keywords where every ranking page is far above you; those become phase two once authority builds.
6. Repeat the comparison per keyword rather than applying one site-wide difficulty cutoff.
7. Re-run the comparison every few months, since your own rating and the SERP both move.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Moz

**Pitfall:** Setting a blanket keyword difficulty cap for the whole site. Difficulty is relative, so one cap either blocks winnable niche SERPs or waves through head terms defended by 70+ DR incumbents.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau picks targets, compare pabau.com's domain rating against the actual ranking domains for each aesthetic and practice management query rather than applying one difficulty ceiling across the whole keyword sheet.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat domain rating as a comparison, not a threshold. For each candidate keyword, check the ratings of the sites already ranking and prioritize the SERPs where some of them are at or below yours.

### 8. Judge each keyword by three questions about the searcher  `118.12`
*useful · best practices · source 118*

Grow and Convert give a short qualification test to apply to every row in a gap report, framed around the searcher rather than the metrics. For each keyword, work out the questions the searcher has, the problems they want to solve or the tasks they want to accomplish, and the goal of their research. Then answer three follow-ups: is this searcher looking for a product or service, do they have a problem and are seeking a solution, and do you actually have a good solution for this person. Only a keyword that passes all three is a pain point keyword worth a page. The test is deliberately manual and cannot be replaced by a difficulty or volume filter, because it depends on your product, not on the market.

> "The problems they want to solve, or tasks they want to accomplish"

**How to do it**

1. Take each surviving keyword and write one sentence stating what the searcher is trying to accomplish.
2. Write the specific question the searcher would ask a knowledgeable friend using that phrasing.
3. Answer question one: is this searcher looking for a product or service right now, or only for information.
4. Answer question two: do they have a live problem and are they seeking a solution for it.
5. Answer question three: do you have a genuinely good solution for this exact person, not an adjacent one.
6. Keep the keyword only if all three answers are yes, and delete it otherwise rather than parking it.
7. Carry the three answers into the brief, since they define the angle and the point at which the product is introduced.

**Pitfall:** Answering the third question generously. If the honest answer is that your product partly fits, the article ends up hedged, and hedged buying-intent pages convert worse than no page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should carry these three answers at the top: what the practice owner is trying to do, whether they are actively looking for software, and which Pabau module genuinely solves it.

**Apply anywhere:** Qualify each keyword by writing what the searcher wants, then confirm they are seeking a solution, have a live problem, and that you genuinely solve it.

### 9. Keep a how-to keyword only when the job needs software  `106.19`
*useful · content insights · source 106*

Grow and Convert give a filter for jobs-to-be-done keywords that separates the ones with implied buying-intent from the ones that only want tips. Ask whether the job can be completed without a tool. 'How to paint a deck' does not imply the reader wants to buy painting supplies, because they may only want technique. 'How to automate payroll' cannot be done without software, so the need for a tool is implied in the query itself. For most SaaS categories this filter passes a lot of keywords, which is why they call jobs to be done the largest set of buying-intent keywords. It also predicts what the ranking content has to be: the manual method explained honestly, then the tool as the better route.

> "can't be done without software, so the need for a tool is implied"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's paired examples: 'how to paint a deck' fails the test, 'how to automate payroll' passes it.

**Pitfall:** Treating any 'how to' query about your domain as bottom-funnel. If a competent reader could finish the job with the article alone, the page will get traffic and no signups.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should apply this filter to clinic how-to topics. 'How to write a consultation form' can be done manually and converts poorly; 'how to automate appointment reminders' or 'how to manage recurring treatment plans' imply software.

**Apply anywhere:** Filter every how-to keyword by asking whether the job can be finished without a tool. If it can, the reader wants tips, not a product; if it cannot, buying-intent is built into the query.

### 10. Match interview pain points to bottom-of-funnel keywords opportunistically  `78.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 78*

Grow and Convert do not start GEO work from a keyword tool, but they do check afterwards. Once the interviews surface real customer pain points, they test whether any of those pain points map to a known Google search term. Where one does, they target it with the same piece of content, so the asset earns traditional search and AI search in one go. They say most businesses have plenty of these product-recommendation keywords, and name the patterns: 'best [category] software', '[competitor] alternatives', and 'how to [solve specific problem]'. The order matters and reverses normal practice: pain points first, keyword validation second, never a keyword list that then dictates which pain points get written about.

> "We target Google keywords if and when it makes sense"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say most businesses have plenty of bottom-of-funnel product recommendation keywords on Google matching interview-sourced pain points.

**How to do it**

1. Finish the customer-facing interviews and list every distinct pain point before opening any keyword tool.
2. Take each pain point and search its natural phrasing to see whether a query exists for it.
3. Check the three bottom-of-funnel patterns explicitly: best [category] software, [competitor] alternatives, how to [solve problem].
4. Where a pain point maps to a real query, set that query as the page's target and keep the pain-point framing in the body.
5. Where no query exists, publish the page anyway and treat AI visibility as its only job.
6. Do not add a pain point to the plan just because a keyword has volume.
7. Report the two channels separately so a page with zero search volume is not judged a failure.

**Pitfall:** Letting keyword volume filter the topic map. Pain points with no search volume are often the ones LLMs match hardest, and dropping them removes exactly the specificity the strategy depends on.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, pain points from support calls should be written up regardless of volume, then checked against queries like 'best med spa software' or 'Aesthetic Record alternatives' and targeted where a match exists.

**Apply anywhere:** Source pain points from customer-facing staff first, then check each against Google for a matching bottom-of-funnel query. Target the query when one exists, publish anyway when it does not, and never let volume decide the topic list.

### 11. Match the specificity modifier to your market positioning, not to volume  `142.11`
*useful · best practices · source 142*

Grow and Convert's clearest mechanism in this study is alignment. Their digital asset management client is positioned mainly as an enterprise tool, and had extra features built for marketers and creative professionals. Targeting 'marketing asset management software' returned 15.2%. Enterprise-framed keywords for that client converted around 6.5%, while other industry-specific keywords for the same client converted around 2%. They put it plainly: the more a keyword and article align with your product's natural strengths, the higher the conversion rate. The lesson is that the modifier you attach to a category keyword is a positioning decision, not a keyword research decision. Picking modifiers by search volume produces the 2% outcomes; picking them by where the product is genuinely strongest produces the 6% and 15% outcomes.

> "the more a keyword and article align with your product's natural strengths"

**Evidence:** Same DAM client: 15.2% for 'marketing asset management software', around 6.5% for enterprise-framed terms, around 2% for other industry-specific terms.

**How to do it**

1. Write one sentence naming the segment your product serves better than any rival.
2. List the features that only exist because of that segment.
3. Turn each of those features and that segment into a category-plus-modifier keyword.
4. Rank the resulting keywords by strength of alignment, not by monthly volume.
5. Publish the top-aligned term first and measure its conversion rate against the 4.85% category benchmark.
6. Drop any modifier pointing at a segment where you have no distinct feature advantage.
7. Revisit the list whenever positioning or the product roadmap changes.

**Pitfall:** Choosing modifiers from a keyword tool's suggestion list. Volume-led modifiers point at segments where the product has no particular advantage, and those posts land near 2%.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's specificity modifiers should follow where the product is strongest, not where volume is highest. If Pabau is strongest for multi-location aesthetic clinics, that modifier gets the first article, not the largest generic vertical.

**Apply anywhere:** Pick specificity modifiers from where your product is genuinely strongest, not from where the volume is highest. The segment you serve best gets the first article.

### 12. Mine Reddit for national seed topics, then test for local conversion  `11.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 11*

Gotch's Reddit ideation technique: because Reddit rarely surfaces posts that already combine a service keyword with a specific city, search Reddit at the broad, national level first (his example: searching a general topic like 'AEO' and browsing recent posts), find genuine longtail questions people are actually asking, such as 'Is AI replacing traditional digital marketing agencies or just making them smarter?', strip filler wording down to the core question, then test whether adding a location naturally fits before converting it into a localized target keyword.

> "I look at national-based keywords and then convert those into local keywords"

**How to do it**

1. Go to Reddit and search a broad, national-level seed term related to your niche, not combined with a location, since local-plus-niche combos rarely appear as native posts.
2. Sort by most recent posts and scan for genuine questions being asked by real users, not just top or hot posts.
3. Copy any longtail question that reflects real demand (inferred: prioritize ones with replies or upvotes as an engagement signal) into your keyword tracking sheet.
4. Strip filler words from the question to isolate the core query, e.g. remove a trailing clause like 'or just making them smarter?'.
5. Test whether a location modifier fits naturally by checking if the answer to the question would actually differ by city or region; only localize if it does.
6. Add the localized version of the query to the tracking sheet under the relevant cluster, tagged with Reddit as the Source.

**Tools:** Reddit

### 13. Mine autocomplete, People also ask and related searches in that order  `104.3`
*useful · concrete actions · source 104*

Grow and Convert name three free Google features and give each a distinct job. Autocomplete tells you which layers of specificity searchers most often add to your main keyword, and also surfaces sibling framings of the same query, their example being 'compliance strategy' versus 'compliance guide'. People also ask gives you the exact words people use to describe a problem, and it exposes adjacent problems; if your product also solves one of those, it opens a new bucket of long-tail terms. Related searches is the variation finder, and they call out one specific use for it: when the terms you invented show no volume in a tool, related searches surfaces phrasings of the same idea that do have volume. Their demonstration queries are 'compliance strategy for', 'online payment software' and 'it businesses in NYC'.

> "autocomplete, People also ask, and related searches"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use related searches specifically to find phrasings with volume when their own candidate terms report none.

**How to do it**

1. Type your category keyword into Google with a trailing 'for' and record every autocomplete suggestion.
2. Repeat with each letter of the alphabet appended to catch suggestions the base query hides.
3. Note where autocomplete offers a different noun for the same idea, such as guide versus strategy, and treat each as a separate candidate.
4. Open the People also ask box on your main commercial query and expand two levels deep, copying the wording verbatim.
5. Flag any PAA question describing an adjacent problem your product also solves, and start a new keyword bucket for it.
6. Scroll to Related searches at the foot of the SERP and harvest every variant.
7. When your invented phrasing shows zero volume in Ahrefs, run it through Related searches and check the volume on the phrasings Google returns instead.
8. Filter the whole harvest by buying intent before anything goes on the calendar.

**Tools:** Google Search, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Harvesting all three features indiscriminately produces a large pile of informational questions. Most PAA and autocomplete output is low intent, so an unfiltered harvest quietly turns a bottom-funnel plan into a blog of how-to posts.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the People also ask boxes on queries like 'clinic booking software' hold the phrasing practitioners actually use. David should harvest that wording into template and blog briefs rather than relying on tool exports alone.

**Apply anywhere:** Work autocomplete for specificity layers, People also ask for the customer's own wording and adjacent problems, and related searches for variants with volume when your own phrasings report none. Filter everything by buying intent afterwards.

### 14. Mine competitor JTBD keywords as customer-pain research, not just targets  `118.10`
*useful · best practices · source 118*

Grow and Convert point out a second use for the jobs-to-be-done rows in a competitor gap report. Beyond being keywords to target, they are evidence of pains your audience has that you may not have heard in customer conversations. Their help desk examples are 'how to organize customer support emails', 'how to manage a team inbox' and 'how to create and send automated support emails'. Each names a task in the customer's own words, and a competitor investing in ranking for it has usually validated that the pain is real and common. The output is not only a content plan but an input to positioning: the phrasing tells you how to present the solution, and a cluster of related JTBD terms points at a pain worth naming on the product pages too.

> "you can learn more about your target audiences' pains"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's help desk JTBD examples: 'how to organize customer support emails', 'how to manage a team inbox', 'how to create and send automated support emails'.

**How to do it**

1. Pull every 'how to' and task-shaped row from the gap report into its own tab.
2. Rewrite each as the underlying pain in one sentence, in the searcher's vocabulary rather than yours.
3. Group the pains into clusters and count the keywords behind each cluster as a rough proxy for how common the pain is.
4. Compare the clusters against the pains your sales and support teams actually report hearing.
5. Flag any cluster with real volume that nobody internally has mentioned, and take it to a customer call to verify.
6. Feed the verified new pains into product page copy and demo scripts, not just into blog briefs.
7. Reuse the searcher's exact phrasing in headings and opening sentences on the resulting pages.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Treating a high-volume JTBD term as proof of a pain your buyers have. The searchers may be a different segment entirely, which is why the cluster needs checking against a real customer conversation before it reshapes positioning.

**Apply at Pabau:** The JTBD rows from a Pabau gap report should be summarized as a list of clinic pains and shared with sales, so recurring ones like managing consultation forms or chasing no-shows shape product page copy as well as blog briefs.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat the jobs-to-be-done rows in a gap report as a record of customer pains, cluster them, verify the unfamiliar ones on a customer call, and feed them into positioning as well as content.

### 15. Mine sales calls, autocomplete and related searches for category variants  `106.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 106*

Grow and Convert list four named sources for finding category-keyword variants you would not invent at a desk. Ask customers directly, or ask your salespeople what customers actually say, which surfaces the words buyers use rather than the words the product team uses. Find synonyms for each word in the keyword. Type the seed slowly into the Google search bar and read the dropdown suggestions, their example being 'accounting software for'. And read Google's related searches at the foot of the results page, their example being the block under 'CRM software for law firms'. The first source is the one most teams skip, and it is the only one that is not derived from a tool or from Google itself, so it is where genuinely new variants come from.

> "Type slowly in the search bar and see what suggestions Google makes"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show the 'accounting software for' autocomplete dropdown and the related searches block for 'CRM software for law firms' as their two screenshots for this step.

**How to do it**

1. Ask two or three salespeople to list the exact phrases prospects use to describe the product on calls.
2. Ask recent customers what they typed into Google before they found you.
3. Take each word in your seed keyword and list synonyms for it.
4. Type the seed plus a trailing 'for' slowly into the Google search bar and record every dropdown suggestion.
5. Search the seed and copy the whole related searches block at the foot of the page.
6. Repeat the dropdown and related-searches pass on the two or three most promising variants you just collected.
7. Deduplicate the collected phrases into the category tab of the keyword sheet.
8. SERP-check each new variant for buying-intent before adding volume data.

**Tools:** Google

**Pitfall:** Only mining Google's own suggestions gives you the same list every competitor has. The sales-call phrasing is what produces variants nobody is targeting.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should ask its sales and onboarding teams what clinic owners say when they describe the problem, then run autocomplete and related searches on 'clinic software for' and 'aesthetic practice software'. Those phrases belong in the keyword sheet before any tool export.

**Apply anywhere:** Collect keyword variants from four sources: what customers and salespeople actually say, word-by-word synonyms, Google autocomplete typed slowly, and the related searches block. The sales-call phrasing is the part competitors do not have.

### 16. Mine secondary keywords that never cracked the top 10 for new articles  `116.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 116*

Alongside recovering lost rankings, Grow and Convert flag a second source of article ideas hiding in the same data. When you pull a post's full organic keyword list, some secondary keywords will have been ranking, but never inside the top 10. Those are not recovery candidates because there is nothing to recover. They are new-article candidates: the page proves the domain has some relevance to that query, but not enough to rank a page that was never built for it. Combined with their buying-intent filter, this turns a single converting post into a small content plan. The same SERP comparison test applies before committing, since a keyword whose SERP closely matches the existing post will cannibalize rather than add.

> "Secondary keywords that were never ranking in the top 10 spots"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name never-top-10 secondary keywords as viable options for new articles, applying the same SERP comparison test they use for recovery decisions.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the full organic keyword list for a proven converting post in Ahrefs.
2. Filter to keywords where the page's best-ever position sits outside the top 10.
3. Drop anything without buying intent, keeping category, comparison and jobs-to-be-done phrasings.
4. Run each survivor's SERP against the existing post's target keyword in a SERP comparison tool.
5. Discard candidates whose SERP is highly similar, because a new page would cannibalize.
6. Commission a dedicated article for the survivors, written only about that query.
7. Internally link the new article to and from the original converting post.
8. Track the new page's position and conversions against the parent post's, watching for cannibalization.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Keyword Insights

**Pitfall:** Building a page for a near-duplicate keyword. If the two SERPs are highly similar, the new article competes with a page that already converts and you can end up worse off than before.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's converting comparison and category pages are a source of new-article briefs. Pull the keywords they rank for outside the top 10, keep the commercial ones, and check the SERP overlap before commissioning to avoid cannibalizing the page that already converts.

**Apply anywhere:** Your converting pages are a source of new-article briefs. Pull the keywords they rank for outside the top 10, keep the commercial ones, and check SERP overlap before commissioning.

### 17. Mine six named internal sources for the jobs customers say out loud  `93.5`
*useful · concrete actions · source 93*

Goolding says there is no shortcut to JTBD keywords: you need the pain points your product solves and the exact language customers use for them. He names six sources. Customer enquiries across email, webforms and social messages. Product demo recordings. Interviews with internal experts. Customer service emails, calls and surveys. Forums, chat rooms and social platforms. And SEO software, which he calls imperfect but useful for surfacing interrelated topics. He puts SEO tools last on purpose, because the tool only shows phrasings someone already searched enough to register, while demo calls and support tickets show the job in the customer's own words before it has been translated into a keyword. The output of this mining is a phrasing list, which then gets run through the four query shapes.

> "Customer enquiries (emails, webforms, social messages)"

**How to do it**

1. Export the last 6 months of customer service emails and webform enquiries into one document.
2. Pull transcripts of the most recent 20 to 30 sales demo recordings.
3. Interview two or three internal experts and ask what customers say they are trying to get done.
4. Search relevant forums and subreddits for the same jobs and copy the phrasing verbatim.
5. Tag every recurring job with the exact wording customers used, not the wording your product marketing uses.
6. Group the wordings into jobs, keeping every synonym as a separate row.
7. Run each wording through Ahrefs or your keyword tool last, only to find volume and related terms.
8. Keep zero-volume wordings on the list if they appeared repeatedly in support or demo calls.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Teams start at the SEO tool, so the list inherits the tool's vocabulary and misses the phrasings customers actually use. The signal is a keyword list that reads like your product page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should mine demo recordings and support tickets from aesthetic and dermatology practices for the jobs staff describe, then check those exact phrasings for volume before briefing any how-to article.

**Apply anywhere:** Mine support tickets, demo recordings and expert interviews for the jobs customers describe in their own words, and only take that list to a keyword tool at the end.

### 18. Mine the odd words patients use for the mechanism, not the condition  `109.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 109*

Beyond the wrong name for a condition, Grow and Convert found a second seam at Cognitive FX: strange descriptions of the treatment itself. An article targeting 'neuroplasticity treatment' has consistently brought in conversions. No clinician at the practice would ever use that phrase to describe what they do. It is one of the ways target patients search when they are looking for help and reaching for a mechanism word they half-remember. The general rule the author draws is that with any complex product or service, technical, medical or financial, many people searching for it and around it will use terms that are wrong, ambiguous or non-ideal, and those terms are worth deliberate effort to uncover rather than something to avoid.

> "It's not a term their clinicians would ever use"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX's 'neuroplasticity treatment' article has consistently brought in conversions despite no clinician using the phrase.

**How to do it**

1. List the mechanisms, ingredients and technologies behind your service, in the words a layperson might half-remember.
2. Add the word treatment, therapy, software or service to each mechanism word and check search volume.
3. Read the SERP for each: if results are academic or definitional, a practical page has room.
4. Check Reddit and patient forums for how people phrase the mechanism when asking for help.
5. Keep terms where the searcher is clearly looking for help rather than for a definition.
6. Write the page as help for a person with the problem, not as an explanation of the science.
7. Connect the mechanism word to your actual service early so the reader knows they are in the right place.
8. Track conversions per mechanism-word article separately, since these behave differently from condition terms.

**Pitfall:** Mechanism words attract students and researchers as well as buyers. If the page converts nothing after three months despite steady traffic, the mix is academic and not worth expanding.

**Apply at Pabau:** Aesthetic patients and clinic owners search device and ingredient names rather than treatment names. Pabau's blog and procedure-code pages can capture those mechanism-word searches and route them to the relevant treatment and software content.

**Apply anywhere:** Search volume also sits on the mechanism behind your service, phrased as patients half-remember it. Pair each mechanism word with treatment, therapy or software, keep the ones whose SERP shows help-seeking rather than definitions, and write the page as help.

### 19. Mix high-volume and low-KD keywords in the first publishing batch  `106.12`
*useful · best practices · source 106*

Grow and Convert accept that the ideal of high volume plus low KD is rare, so most decisions are a trade between higher volume with higher KD and lower volume with lower KD. They do not pick one. High-volume, high-KD terms take longer to rank but drive more conversions once they land. Low-volume, low-KD terms rank faster and deliver early conversions while the harder pages mature, and they still convert, so they belong in the strategy either way. Their working recommendation is a starting batch that spans all three keyword categories plus both ends of the volume and difficulty trade, on the grounds that diversification raises the chance of early wins that are also sustainable rather than betting the quarter on one type.

> "which increases the chances of early, sustainable success"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state they start every engagement with a mix across all three keyword categories, high-volume terms and low-KD terms.

**How to do it**

1. Split the intent-verified list into high-volume high-KD and low-volume low-KD buckets.
2. Confirm each of the three keyword categories, category, comparison and JTBD, appears in both buckets.
3. Build the first publishing batch by drawing from every category and both buckets.
4. Publish the high-KD pages early so they have time to mature while the easy pages deliver.
5. Set different review windows: judge low-KD pages at three months, high-KD pages at nine to twelve.
6. Track conversions per page in both buckets rather than aggregate traffic.
7. Rebalance the next batch toward whichever bucket produced the most conversions on your own data.

**Pitfall:** Filling the first batch entirely with easy low-volume terms produces early wins and then a plateau, because nothing hard was started early enough to mature.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content queue should always have both running: hard category terms like 'practice management software' alongside quick comparison and template pages that convert within a quarter.

**Apply anywhere:** Start with a mixed batch: some hard high-volume terms that need a year to mature, some easy low-volume terms that convert within a quarter, and all three keyword categories represented in both.

### 20. Monitor about 20 niche Facebook groups for repeated buyer questions  `114.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 114*

Grow and Convert treat communities as an ideation source rather than only a traffic source. The author belongs to around 20 Facebook groups and monitors the feeds for questions or challenges worth writing about. In one morning's scan he found two usable posts. One asked whether to blog on Medium or use WordPress, which became the idea 'Medium vs WordPress for a company blog' and checked out in Google as a common question. Another asked at what phase of the business lifecycle members started their blog, which produced 'when should a startup start blogging' and a content marketing ROI angle. He uses the engagement count on a question as the demand signal: heavy engagement means a hot topic worth a piece. He also names Reddit, Discord and Quora, where you can run a keyword search, sort by top threads and read for recurring questions.

> "I'm a member of around 20 different Facebook groups"

**Evidence:** A single morning's scan of two Facebook groups produced two publishable topics, one validated by Google search results for Medium vs WordPress.

**How to do it**

1. Join around 20 groups where your buyers actually post, not groups full of your own peers.
2. Scan the feeds on a fixed schedule, once each morning, rather than browsing ad hoc.
3. Copy any post phrased as a question or a challenge into an ideas document with a link.
4. Use the reply and reaction count as the demand signal and prioritize the heavily engaged questions.
5. Search each candidate question in Google to confirm other people ask it and to see what already ranks.
6. Repeat the sweep on Reddit, Discord and Quora using a keyword search sorted by top threads.
7. Apply the filter of who is asking before committing: name the company size and role behind the question.
8. Discard questions your target buyer is long past asking, such as basic setup questions when you sell to large brands.

**Pitfall:** Ideas get taken from whoever posts most, not from your buyer. Grow and Convert warn that a Reddit question from someone starting an ecommerce store is worthless if you sell to 50 million dollar brands who moved past it years ago.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should monitor aesthetic practice owner and injector groups on Facebook plus the relevant subreddits, and apply the who-is-asking filter hard. Questions from solo injectors starting out map to different pages than questions from multi-location clinic managers.

**Apply anywhere:** Join around 20 groups your buyers post in, sweep them daily, and log every question with its engagement count. Validate the promising ones in Google, then screen each by who is actually asking before it enters the calendar.

### 21. Multiply category keywords across the six product-noun suffixes  `93.16`
*useful · concrete actions · source 93*

Goolding defines category keywords as terms where someone is simply looking for your exact product type, and gives a mechanical expansion rule most teams skip. For Shopify these are ecommerce software and ecommerce platform, plus all the variations on those terms including solution, app, service, services, tool and tools. The point is that each suffix is a separate SERP with its own set of ranking pages and its own volume, and a single page targeting 'software' does not automatically pick up 'platform' or 'app'. This is a cheap expansion because the seed list is short: take the category noun and cycle the suffix. It runs before any JTBD work, since category keywords sit at the highest buying intent alongside comparisons.

> "including solution, app, service(s), tool(s), etc"

**Evidence:** Goolding's Shopify example: ecommerce software and ecommerce platform plus solution, app, service(s) and tool(s) variants.

**How to do it**

1. Write your product category as a bare noun phrase, for example 'practice management'.
2. Append each suffix in turn: software, platform, solution, app, service, services, tool, tools, system.
3. Check volume for every combination in Ahrefs rather than assuming the software variant dominates.
4. Search each variant and compare the top 10 against the software variant's top 10.
5. Where the ranking pages differ materially, treat it as a separate target rather than a synonym.
6. Where the SERPs are near-identical, fold the variant into the existing page as a secondary term.
7. Repeat the whole cycle with each vertical qualifier you serve.
8. Order the resulting list by intent closeness, not volume.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Building a separate page per suffix when the SERPs are identical creates cannibalization. Compare the top 10 for each variant before deciding it deserves its own page.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should check the suffix variants for its categories, such as practice management software, platform, system and app for clinics, and confirm from the SERPs which need their own page.

**Apply anywhere:** Cycle every product-noun suffix through your category term, then compare the top 10 for each variant to decide which need their own page.

### 22. Own every synonym people use for your product category, homepage included  `90.7`
*useful · best practices · source 90*

Grow and Convert call it borderline inexcusable not to have pages deliberately aimed at your category keywords, and say the homepage can be one of those pages. Category keywords are the terms that describe what you sell, such as 'best crm software', 'shopify development agency' or "women's hiking boots". Searchers using them need no buyer's journey and no problem-aware stage; they are already in the market and the only job is to appear and convince. The part teams miss is coverage of synonyms. Grow and Convert give themselves as the example: some people call them an SEO agency, others a content marketing agency, others a SaaS content marketing agency. Each is a separate query with its own SERP, and the customer interview question about how customers describe you to a friend is what surfaces the ones you would not have guessed.

> "It's borderline inexcusable not to very intentionally have"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite their own category variants: SEO agency, content marketing agency, SaaS content marketing agency, each of which they try to rank for.

**How to do it**

1. Write down every phrase you use internally for your product category.
2. Add every phrase customers used in answer to 'how would you describe our product to a friend who knew nothing about us'.
3. Add the category names your competitors use in their own titles and meta descriptions.
4. Search each phrase and check whether the SERP is genuinely the same set of results; different results mean it needs its own page.
5. Assign one page per distinct SERP, using the homepage for the single most central term.
6. Confirm each assigned page front-loads that exact phrase in the title, H1 and first sentence.
7. Check Search Console for which of these terms the site already gets impressions on and fix the ones ranking with the wrong page.
8. Revisit the synonym list every time a new customer interview round is run.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Targeting only the term the company uses internally. If sales and marketing say 'practice management software' while buyers say 'clinic software', the highest-intent query in the category has no page pointed at it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should confirm a dedicated page exists for each category synonym buyers use, including clinic software, medspa software and salon management software, rather than relying on the homepage to cover them all.

**Apply anywhere:** Map every synonym customers use for your product category to a distinct page, use the homepage for the most central term, and get the synonyms from customer interviews rather than internal vocabulary.

### 23. Pair every phrase match keyword with a negative keyword list  `156.12`
*useful · best practices · source 156*

Grow and Convert allow phrase match alongside exact match once broad match is gone, but attach a condition to it. Phrase match on 'accounting software' can still serve on 'what is the best affordable accounting software', which is looser than exact match would allow. Because short keywords have a wide tail of possible queries, they say you should also include a negative keyword strategy if you are using phrase match. Exact match, by contrast, serves only the term itself or a very close variant such as 'accounting programs', so it needs far less protection. The practical reading is that phrase match buys reach at the cost of ongoing maintenance, and the negative list is that maintenance cost rather than an optional extra.

> "include a negative keyword strategy if you're using phrase match"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contrast phrase match serving 'what is the best affordable accounting software' with exact match serving only the term or a close variant like 'accounting programs'.

**How to do it**

1. Create a shared negative keyword list at account level before launching phrase match keywords.
2. Seed it with the standard non-buyer modifiers: free, jobs, salary, template, DIY, meaning, definition, reddit.
3. Add competitor names you do not want to serve against, and adjacent verticals you do not sell to.
4. Apply the shared list to every search campaign.
5. Read the search terms report weekly for the first month, then monthly, adding new patterns as they appear.
6. Use exact match alone for the highest-value terms where you want no tail at all.
7. Track the ratio of matched to excluded queries per phrase keyword, and pause any keyword where most of its tail is junk.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Adding negatives only inside one campaign leaves the same junk queries running in the others. Build the list at account level as a shared list from the start.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build a shared negative list covering free, jobs, salary and non-target verticals before running phrase match on practice management terms.

**Apply anywhere:** Run phrase match only with an account-level shared negative list, seeded with non-buyer modifiers and off-target verticals, and reviewed weekly for the first month.

### 24. Pick the mini-volume term that anchors a cluster of low-to-mid variations  `95.16`
*useful · concrete actions · source 95*

Matt Goolding shows how one mini-volume target can collect rankings across an entire variation cluster. Geekbot's article targeting 'daily standup app' at 10 reported volume picked up rankings for related low-to-mid terms including 'daily standup tools' at 30 and 'standup software' at 40, and has produced 37 signups since publication in December 2021. Their article on 'free Slack standup bot' at 20 volume generated 177 organic conversions in the 7 months after publication, over 25 a month from a keyword tools price at 20 searches. His fourth explanation for the underestimate is exactly this: a 20-volume target with 10 variations each adding 5 to 10 searches sums to a healthy total. So the selection rule is to pick the mini-volume phrasing that sits at the center of the variation set, not any low-volume phrase.

> "daily standup tools" (30 vol) and "standup software"

**Evidence:** Geekbot's 'daily standup app' page at 10 reported volume ranks for 'daily standup tools' (30) and 'standup software' (40), producing 37 signups. Its 'free Slack standup bot' page at 20 volume produced 177 conversions in 7 months.

**How to do it**

1. For each mini-volume candidate, run Ahrefs Matching Terms and Related Terms to pull every variation of the phrase.
2. Sum the reported volume of all variations that share the same intent, and record that total next to the candidate.
3. Check the SERP for two or three of the variations and confirm the same type of page ranks for each.
4. Where the same pages rank across variations, choose the single phrasing that appears most often in those page titles as the target.
5. Write one article for the whole cluster rather than one per variation, to avoid cannibalization.
6. Work each variation naturally into a subheading or a sentence rather than repeating the head phrasing.
7. After 3 months, pull the page's ranking keywords and confirm it is collecting the cluster, not just the target.
8. If variations are ranking on page 2 or 3 while the target ranks on page 1, add a section addressing that variation specifically.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Building a separate page per variation splits the cluster and leaves several weak pages competing with each other, when one page would have taken the lot.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should target the central phrasing of each mini-volume cluster and cover the variations inside one article, rather than spinning up near-duplicate pages for every wording of the same software query. That also keeps Pabau's cannibalization risk down.

**Apply anywhere:** Choose the mini-volume phrasing that sits at the center of a variation cluster and cover the whole cluster in one article. Summed variation volume, not the target's own reading, is what the page will actually earn.

### 25. Pick topics from customer pain points because volume favors beginner keywords  `99.13`
*useful · best practices · source 99*

Khanal restates Grow and Convert's topic-selection position as the setup for the detail principle. Chasing search volume for blog post ideas is the wrong method, because search volume tends to favor beginner-level keywords, and beginner-level topics fail against an advanced audience regardless of how well they are written. His example is selling IT services to CTOs: '9 Tips for IT professionals in 2019' or a free guide about their own job will not grab a CTO, because wanting nine tips about your own job is not one of their pain points. The alternative he prescribes is thorough customer research first, then topics chosen from the pain points that research surfaces, with search volume demoted. He points to their Pain Point SEO post as the method. The connection to the rest of the article is that topic level and writing detail are separate controls, and volume-led topic selection breaks the first one.

> "which tends to favor beginner level keywords"

**Evidence:** Khanal's CTO example and Grow and Convert's Pain Point SEO method, referenced as the alternative to volume-led ideation.

**How to do it**

1. Run customer research interviews before building the topic list, not after.
2. Extract the pain points those interviews surface, in the customer's own wording.
3. Map each pain point to a topic the customer would search when that pain is active.
4. Check each candidate topic against the question: would a veteran in this role search this.
5. Cut topic formats that only exist because of volume, such as generic tips roundups and free beginner guides.
6. Use search volume to choose between two viable pain-point topics, not to generate them.
7. Keep a note of pain points with no search volume and serve them with sales or nurture content instead.

**Pitfall:** Volume-led lists look productive and fill a calendar, but they aim the blog at people who are not the buyer. The signal is traffic rising while demo requests stay flat.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's topic list should start from what clinic owners and practice managers actually raise with sales and support, then be checked against volume. High-volume beginner aesthetics terms will not reach the buyer.

**Apply anywhere:** Start your topic list from what buyers actually raise with sales and support, then check volume. High-volume beginner terms in your category usually miss the buyer.
