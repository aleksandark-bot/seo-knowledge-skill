### 1. Keep the chain intact: keyword to query to ad to landing page  `156.5`
*core · best practices · source 156*

Grow and Convert reduce paid account structure to one chain: the high-intent keyword should match the query, the query should match the ad, and the ad should match the landing page. Break any link and the money leaks. If the keyword and ad line up but the searcher who typed 'Shopify development agency' lands on a page about Squarespace themes, there are no leads. They admit the logic is not groundbreaking, and say the reason so many accounts fail it is structural: oversized ad groups force generic ad text, which forces a generic landing page. They also give a diagnostic order. If intent is right and ad text matches and click-through rate is strong but bounce rate is high, the landing page is the broken link.

> "the ad should match the landing page"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite the Shopify-query-to-Squarespace-page mismatch as the standard failure they find in mismanaged accounts.

**How to do it**

1. Pick a keyword and read its top search queries from the search terms report.
2. Read the ad that serves for those queries and check the headline names the same thing.
3. Click the ad and check the landing page headline names the same thing again.
4. Mark the first point in the chain where the wording changes.
5. If click-through rate is low, fix the ad text; if click-through rate is fine but bounce rate is high, fix the landing page.
6. Give each ad group a single landing page that matches its one intent.
7. Repeat the walk for every ad group in the account, starting with the highest spend.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Sending several ad groups to one general product page. Click-through rate looks healthy, bounce rate stays high, and teams blame the keyword instead of the destination.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should map every paid ad group to a dedicated landing page rather than sending all paid clicks to the generic demo page, so an ad about online booking lands on a booking page.

**Apply anywhere:** Walk each ad group from keyword to query to ad to landing page and fix the first place the wording changes, using bounce rate against click-through rate to tell which link is broken.

### 2. Kill any offer whose lifetime value equals its one-time price  `183.6`
*core · best practices · source 183*

Grow and Convert's in-person workshop worked on every measure except the one that mattered. Clients got a full day, the founders charged $1500 to $3000, feedback was strong, and requests arrived about once a month without any promotion. They still shut it down. The reason was structural: demand was not high enough to offset the fact that it was one-off revenue. They spent heavy sales effort to land a single client, delivered one day, and collected a few thousand dollars with nothing behind it. As they put it, if you charge $3000 the LTV is $3000, with no back-end product, no software and no recurring service. Their extra filter was market size: how many companies have just hired a content marketing person and will pay to train them. Not enough, from what they could tell.

> "the demand just wasn't high enough to offset the fact that it was one off revenue"

**Evidence:** Workshops at $1500-$3000 drew about one inbound request a month with no promotion, and were still abandoned for a recurring-revenue agency model.

**How to do it**

1. For each offer, write the price, the sales hours needed to land one client, and the delivery hours.
2. Set LTV equal to the price unless a named back-end product, retainer or subscription follows it.
3. Divide expected annual revenue by the sales hours and compare it against your other offers.
4. Estimate how many buyers exist in a year by describing the exact trigger that makes someone buy, then judging how often that trigger fires.
5. Kill or reprice any offer where LTV equals price and inbound is roughly one request a month.
6. Before killing it, test whether a recurring version of the same value exists, since that is the version worth scaling.
7. Redirect the sales effort into the offer with recurring revenue, even if its first-sale price is lower.

**Pitfall:** Judging an offer by client satisfaction and steady inbound alone. Both were strong here, and the business still did not work, because one-off revenue with a heavy sales cycle cannot compound.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's subscription model already solves this, so the lesson applies to side offers. If David scopes one-off services such as paid setup, migration or a content package, he should check they either feed the subscription or repeat, not sit as isolated one-time revenue.

**Apply anywhere:** Score every offer by lifetime value, not by satisfaction. If the price is the LTV, there is no back-end product, and inbound is about one request a month, either attach recurring revenue to it or stop selling it.

### 3. Let the first-versus-last shape pick your funnel stage  `151.7`
*core · content insights · source 151*

Grow and Convert show three real datasets and draw a different content decision from each. For their own email list, first and last click numbers were almost identical, because people decide to join a blog's list immediately rather than think it over. For their high-value B2B service form, several posts had far more first-click than last-click conversions, since a big service purchase takes discussion and time. For one agency client, the pattern reversed: multiple posts had more last-click than first-click conversions, meaning readers signed up in the same session but had first landed somewhere else. Devesh is blunt that this is not true for every agency, which is why you must run the analysis on your own data before copying anyone's content strategy.

> "But this is not true for every agency!"

**Evidence:** Three Grow and Convert datasets: near-identical first and last for email opt-ins, first well above last for their own B2B service form, and last above first on several posts for an agency client.

**How to do it**

1. Run first versus last click per post for your single most important conversion goal.
2. Classify the blog as immediate-converting if first and last are close across most posts.
3. Classify it as delayed-converting if first click is consistently well above last click.
4. Classify it as same-session-closing if last click exceeds first click on several posts.
5. For immediate-converting blogs, build content upgrades and inline opt-in CTAs and do not rely on return visits.
6. For delayed-converting blogs, invest in email capture, retargeting and a 90 day nurture path.
7. For same-session-closing blogs, write more bottom-of-funnel posts that qualify hot prospects and put the contact form in them.
8. Redo the classification annually or whenever pricing changes, since the shape moves with deal size.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Copying another company's content mix because it worked for them ignores that the first-versus-last shape differs even between two agencies selling similar services.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sells a considered purchase to practice owners, so David should expect a delayed shape and fund email capture and nurture rather than expecting demo bookings in the first session.

**Apply anywhere:** Run the first-versus-last comparison on your own data and let its shape decide whether to build immediate CTAs, nurture sequences or bottom-of-funnel posts.

### 4. Make the prospect price the upside before you name your fee  `173.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 173*

KlientBoost's close hinges on one question asked at the end of the proposal call, before price has ever been mentioned: 'If we were able to improve performance by 50%, what would that mean in terms of money being made for you?' Dane calls it 'their death sentence, in a good way.' The prospect states the value in their own numbers, so the fee is then judged against a figure they produced rather than against competitors' rates. His worked example: the prospect says they would easily make $10,000 more a month, KlientBoost proposes $4,000, and paying $4,000 to earn $10,000 is an easy decision. The order matters. Price named first becomes a cost to negotiate down; price named after the prospect's own upside number becomes a ratio.

> "what would that mean in terms of money being made for you?"

**Evidence:** Worked example given: prospect says $10k more per month, KlientBoost proposes $4k. KlientBoost reached $300,000 monthly recurring revenue with Dane running this process himself.

**How to do it**

1. Keep price out of every document and every call until the ideas have been presented in full.
2. Present the audit and the proposal of ideas, answering all questions without holding back method.
3. At the end of that call ask what a specific percentage improvement would be worth to them in revenue.
4. Stay silent and let them do the arithmetic out loud; write down the figure they say.
5. Repeat their figure back to confirm it before moving on.
6. Set your fee at a fraction of that figure, roughly 30 to 40% in Dane's example, so the ratio is obvious.
7. Present the fee explicitly against their number, not against market rates.
8. If they cannot produce a figure, treat that as a qualification failure rather than pushing to a price.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
If we were able to improve performance by 50%, what would that mean in terms of money being made for you?
```

**Pitfall:** Asking the question too early, before the prospect has seen the ideas, makes it read as a manipulative sales script. It only lands once you have already given away real value on the same call.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau demos should end with the practice quantifying what fewer no-shows or a fuller diary is worth per month before pricing appears. David can also turn that arithmetic into a calculator or ROI section on the pricing and template pages, so the ratio is visible before a call happens.

**Apply anywhere:** Withhold price until you have delivered your ideas, then ask the buyer what a defined performance improvement would be worth in revenue. Quote a clear fraction of the number they say, and present your fee as a ratio against it.

### 5. Make transaction pages completable by a person or an agent  `66.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 66*

The framework's highest click-resilience type, and it now has two audiences. This content lets a person or agent complete a high-value action on the site: buy, book, apply, download, request, cancel, contact or resolve an account task. Its role is the conversion, so it should show the current price or value, eligibility, safety constraints, required inputs and next step. It spans pricing, sign-up, checkout, booking, download, application, quote, claim, contact, account and support-resolution pages, and the quality bar is complete, structured action requirements. It scores High on click resilience, citation potential, business value and proprietary advantage at Medium effort. The 'or agent' is the new part: an AI agent acting for a user needs the same five things a human does, stated explicitly on the page rather than implied by the interface, or it cannot complete or recommend the action.

> "This content lets a person or agent complete a high-value action on the site"

**How to do it**

1. List every high-value action the site supports — buy, book, apply, download, request, cancel, contact, resolve — and confirm each has a page that completes it.
2. On each, state the current price or value in text, not in an image or a script-rendered widget, so both a user and an agent can read it.
3. State eligibility explicitly: who can take this action, in which markets, under which plan or account state, and what disqualifies them.
4. State safety and legal constraints where they apply — regulatory limits, age or licensing requirements, data handling — rather than burying them in terms.
5. List the required inputs before the flow starts, so the user or agent knows what to have ready: documents, account details, measurements, payment method.
6. Name the single next step in unambiguous language, and make it one action rather than a choice of three.
7. Mark the action up in structured data (Product/Offer with price and availability, Service, Event, or the relevant action type) and keep the markup in sync with the visible values.
8. Test the page as an agent would: strip the JavaScript and check whether price, eligibility, inputs and next step are still all readable.
9. Remove anything that competes with completion — unrelated explanatory copy, cross-sells above the action, or a definition that belongs in a hub.

**Tools:** Schema.org, Google Search Console, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Rendering the price, availability or eligibility only in client-side script or an image. A human browser resolves it; an AI agent evaluating whether to recommend or complete the action does not, and the page's whole advantage — being the only place the action can be finished — goes unused.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's pricing, book-a-demo and template download pages are this type. Put the plan pricing, what each subscription includes, which markets Pabau serves and what onboarding involves in readable text on the page — and remember Pabau has no free trial, so the next step is a demo, stated as one action.

**Apply anywhere:** Pricing, demo-request and download pages are this type. Put the plan pricing, what each plan includes, which markets you serve and what onboarding involves in readable text on the page — and make the next step a single, unambiguous action rather than a choice of three.

### 6. Match CTA wording to the keyword bucket, three fixed pairings  `113.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 113*

Grow and Convert give a direct mapping from keyword bucket to CTA copy. Category keywords such as best project management software get Start free trial or See pricing, because the searcher is comparing products and ready to evaluate. Comparison keywords such as Asana vs Monday get Compare features or Try your product free. Jobs-to-be-done keywords such as how to manage multiple projects get See how the product does this, or a softer CTA like Learn more, because the searcher has a problem but has not committed to buying software. They pair this with a tone rule: readers are evaluating your product, so do not interrupt that evaluation with aggressive conversion tactics that hurt user experience. Sticky navigation bars work well. Pop-ups that block the entire content do not.

> "match your CTA to the keyword intent"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish the three pairings as their standing mapping across SaaS and B2B client work.

**How to do it**

1. Tag every converting page with its keyword bucket: category, comparison, or jobs-to-be-done.
2. On category pages, set the primary CTA to Start free trial or See pricing.
3. On comparison pages, set it to Compare features or Try [product] free.
4. On jobs-to-be-done pages, set it to See how [product] does this, or a softer Learn more.
5. Make the navigation bar sticky so the CTA stays reachable through a long evaluation read.
6. Remove any pop-up that covers the content, since it interrupts the comparison the reader came to complete.
7. Once the page ranks, A/B test placement, in-content against sidebar, and copy specificity, Start free trial against Try free.
8. Do not run those tests before the page ranks for a high-intent keyword, because they cannot fix keyword targeting or weak product selling.

**Pitfall:** Putting a hard trial CTA on a jobs-to-be-done page. The searcher has a problem but has not decided software is the answer, so the mismatch reads as an interruption.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has no free trial, so the mapping becomes: category pages get See pricing or Book a demo, comparison pages get Compare features then Book a demo, and jobs-to-be-done pages get See how Pabau does this. The book-demo block copy should differ by page type rather than being identical everywhere.

**Apply anywhere:** Map CTA copy to the keyword bucket. Category pages get a trial or pricing CTA, comparison pages get a compare-features CTA, jobs-to-be-done pages get a softer see-how-it-works CTA. Use a sticky nav, never a content-blocking pop-up.

### 7. Multiply annual deal value by close rate to get lead value  `150.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 150*

Grow and Convert's breakeven math for lead-based businesses needs three numbers: the annual value of a closed deal, the lead-to-close rate, and the monthly cost of content marketing including the team and its tools. Multiply the first two to get the average value of a lead, then divide monthly content cost by that lead value to get leads per month needed to break even. Their worked example: a $20,000 annual deal value at a 2% lead-to-close rate gives a $400 average lead value. At $10,000 a month, which is what their agency charges, the business needs 25 leads a month to break even. The whole calculation is deliberately back-of-envelope, and the point is that it converts a content budget into a single countable target that can sit on a chart.

> "the business would need 25 leads per month to break even"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's worked example: $20,000 annual deal value x 2% close rate = $400 per lead; $10,000 monthly cost / $400 = 25 leads a month to break even.

**How to do it**

1. Get the average annual value of a closed deal from finance, covering the first year only.
2. Get the lead-to-close rate as a percentage from the CRM, using the same lead definition your goal tracks.
3. Multiply the two to get the average value of one lead.
4. Total your monthly content cost: agency or salaries, plus tools, plus management time.
5. Divide the monthly content cost by the average lead value to get leads per month to break even.
6. Write that number at the top of the ROI sheet before the next article is commissioned.
7. Recompute whenever contract value, close rate or spend moves materially.
8. For ecommerce, substitute average order value for the first two numbers and divide spend by AOV instead.

**Tools:** Google Sheets

**Pitfall:** Using a lead definition looser than the one your goal tracks inflates the close rate and understates the breakeven target, so the program looks closer to profitable than it is.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should run this with its own numbers: average first-year subscription value times the demo-to-customer rate gives the value of a booked demo, and monthly content cost divided by that figure is the demo target the blog and /templates/ pages are working toward.

**Apply anywhere:** Multiply your average annual deal value by your lead-to-close rate to get the value of a lead, then divide your monthly content spend by it. That quotient is the leads per month you need to break even, and it belongs at the top of the reporting sheet.

### 8. Multiply your two email-funnel rates before choosing a blog CTA  `147.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 147*

Grow and Convert settle the email-list-versus-direct-signup argument with arithmetic instead of opinion. The nurture path has two conversion rates in series: blog reader to email subscriber, then email subscriber to lead. The direct path has one. So you multiply the two nurture rates and compare the product against your single direct-to-lead rate, and whichever is higher decides what the blog CTA asks for. Their worked example uses a hypothetical accounting SaaS, account.ly, with 30,000 monthly uniques: 3% to email, 8% of subscribers eventually starting a trial, versus 0.5% direct to trial. The nurture path yields 0.24%, so direct wins even though email opt-ins are captured at six times the rate of direct leads. They note these numbers are extremely typical in their client work.

> "simply a matter of measuring these conversion rates and multiplying them"

**Evidence:** account.ly model: 3% x 8% = 0.24% versus 0.5% direct, on 30,000 monthly blog uniques.

**How to do it**

1. Pull unique visitors to the blog for the last 30 days from GA4, restricted to blog URLs only, not the whole site.
2. Pull email subscribers acquired from the blog in the same 30 days and divide by those uniques to get line 4, the reader-to-email rate.
3. Pull leads (trial starts, demo requests, sales conversations) attributed to blog sessions in the same window and divide by uniques to get line 5, the direct-to-lead rate.
4. Pull leads that originated from email subscribers and divide by total email subscribers to get line 7, the email-to-lead rate.
5. Multiply line 4 by line 7 to get the effective nurture conversion rate.
6. Compare that product against line 5 and point every blog CTA at whichever path wins.
7. If the two are close, rerun the comparison per post rather than blog-wide, since bottom-funnel posts skew direct.
8. Re-measure quarterly, because both rates drift as traffic volume changes.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Comparing the raw opt-in rate to the raw lead rate and concluding email wins. The 3% versus 0.5% gap looks decisive until the second-stage 8% is applied and the nurture path collapses to 0.24%.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this multiplication on pabau.com's blog before adding any more newsletter capture. Compare blog-to-newsletter times newsletter-to-demo against the direct blog-to-demo rate, and if direct wins, replace list-building CTAs on blog and template pages with demo requests.

**Apply anywhere:** Run this multiplication on your own blog before adding more newsletter capture. Compare blog-to-list times list-to-lead against your direct blog-to-lead rate, and point the CTAs at whichever path actually produces more leads.

### 9. Offer a lead magnet for research intent, a demo CTA for buy intent  `36.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 36*

SEO and CRO should not be treated as separate workstreams, because a page that gives the visitor the wrong ask loses them regardless of ranking. The rule is to match the specific call-to-action to the searcher's intent: someone searching a general or informational question is not ready to buy, so offer them something free (a lead magnet) in exchange for their email instead of a hard sell; someone searching a bottom-of-funnel comparison query is much closer to purchasing, and that is the moment to place a direct, high-commitment CTA such as a demo-booking button in front of them.

> "Offer them something free in exchange for their email"

**How to do it**

1. Classify each page's primary target query by funnel stage: general/informational question versus a comparison or purchase-ready query.
2. On informational or top-of-funnel pages, replace or avoid hard-sell CTAs and instead offer a free resource (guide, template, tool) in exchange for the visitor's email address.
3. On bottom-of-funnel or commercial pages, place a direct, high-commitment CTA such as a demo-booking button rather than a soft lead-magnet offer.
4. Audit existing pages for mismatches, such as an informational article that only offers a demo CTA, or a bottom-of-funnel page that only offers a newsletter signup, and swap in the funnel-appropriate CTA. (inferred)
5. Track conversion rate separately for each CTA type by page-intent category to confirm the matched CTAs outperform the mismatched baseline. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Treating SEO and CRO as two separate projects — a page can rank perfectly and still fail commercially if its call-to-action does not match the visitor's actual readiness to buy.

### 10. Open cold emails with a specific critique of the prospect's ad funnel  `173.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 173*

Johnathan Dane started KlientBoost with no clients and no traffic, and landed his first three accounts purely on cold email. His formula was narrow and repeatable: use SpyFu to find companies already spending heavily on ads, actually walk their ad-to-landing-page-to-signup flow, then write a short email naming specific improvements they could implement without hiring anyone. He wrote and sent every email by hand to local companies, and added personality on purpose, closing with an animated gif of a cowboy riding a slice of pizza. The point was that the email had to be immediately useful before it asked for anything. Grow and Convert report 100 emails produced 3 deals at roughly $2,500 a month each, a 3% deal rate that Dane credits entirely to the custom advice inside the message.

> "Find a company that was spending a lot on ads (using SpyFu)"

**Evidence:** About 100 custom cold emails produced 3 clients in the first month at roughly $2,500/month each, $7,500 MRR within three to four weeks.

**How to do it**

1. Build a prospect list in SpyFu filtered to companies with meaningful, sustained paid search spend in your service area.
2. Click the prospect's live ad and walk the whole flow: ad copy, landing page, form, signup confirmation.
3. Write down three to five specific, implementable fixes you saw, naming the page and the element.
4. Draft an email under 200 words that leads with the fixes, not with your company or credentials.
5. Say plainly that they can implement the ideas themselves with no obligation.
6. Add one piece of personality at the end so the email does not read as a template.
7. Send it manually, one at a time, to a named person rather than a generic inbox.
8. Track sends against replies and deals so you can see whether your critique quality is carrying the response rate.

**Tools:** SpyFu

**Pitfall:** Sending the same critique structure at volume without doing the funnel walk turns it into generic outreach and the reply rate collapses. The signal you have hit this is replies that ask what you actually do rather than reacting to your suggestions.

**Apply at Pabau:** David can run this against aesthetic and med-spa practices running Google Ads for treatments Pabau supports. Pull spenders in SpyFu, audit their booking flow from ad to confirmed appointment, and send the specific fixes; that same audit doubles as raw material for a booking-funnel blog article.

**Apply anywhere:** If you sell a service that improves someone's paid or booking funnel, prospect on ad spend rather than firmographics, then earn the reply by giving away a specific, implementable critique of their live funnel before you mention your own offer.

### 11. Open the offer page by naming the asset to build scent  `148.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 148*

The first job of Shour's thank you page is scent: keeping the same message as the page the visitor just came from. He calls out the exact thing they opted in for by name and tells them how they will receive access to it, before any product pitch appears. Only after that does the page ask the accelerate question and present the offer. The reason is that the visitor's attention is still on the download; if the page opens with a product pitch, the visitor reads it as a bait and switch and leaves to look for the file. Naming the asset and stating delivery also cuts the support load from people who think the download failed. Scent is the first of the three components he breaks the page into.

> "build "scent" from the previous page"

**Evidence:** Shour lists scent as the first of three components on a page converting 10.52% into sales-qualified leads.

**How to do it**

1. Write the headline of the offer page using the exact title of the downloaded asset.
2. State in the next line where it is going, for example that it is on its way to their inbox now.
3. Add the sender name and subject line so they can find it if it filters to promotions.
4. Keep any product mention out of this first block entirely.
5. Only then introduce the accelerate question that bridges topic to product.
6. Reuse the same asset wording in the delivery email so the two match.
7. If one page serves several assets, use a dynamic field rather than a generic 'your download'.
8. Check the page against the source post side by side and remove any wording mismatch.

**Tools:** Unbounce

**Pitfall:** Opening with the product offer breaks scent and reads as a switch. The signal is a high bounce rate on the thank you page paired with support emails asking where the download is.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau template pages the confirmation step should name the specific template and say the email is arriving, before mentioning the demo. That keeps the download promise intact while still making the offer.

**Apply anywhere:** Open your offer page by naming the exact asset the visitor requested and saying how it will arrive. Hold the product pitch until after that block.

### 12. Pick a landing page that answers every question behind the query  `157.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 157*

Grow and Convert's landing page rule goes past keyword presence. The page should include the keyword in its title and also directly address all of the questions behind that keyword. They use QuickBooks Online's page for 'accounting software for small business' as the model: the headline uses 'accounting software' and the tagline just below reads '#1 small business accounting software'. Scrolling down, the page covers reviews, features, benefits, integrations and pricing plans. Their point is that these are precisely the things a buyer searching for that category wants to check, so the page satisfies intent end to end rather than restating the keyword and asking for a demo. They treat this section coverage as the reason such a page converts at a good rate.

> "not only includes the keyword in the title"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite QuickBooks Online's small business accounting page, which covers reviews, features, benefits, integrations and pricing under a keyword-matched headline.

**How to do it**

1. Write the target query at the top of a document and list every question a buyer would ask after searching it.
2. Check the destination page contains the keyword in the page title and the H1.
3. Add a tagline directly under the H1 that restates the keyword with its qualifier, as QuickBooks does with '#1 small business accounting software'.
4. Confirm the page carries sections for reviews, features, benefits, integrations and pricing, in that order down the page.
5. Add any section from your question list that is missing, especially pricing, which buyers check before requesting a demo.
6. Remove sections that answer a different query, since they signal the visitor is on the wrong page.
7. Retest by landing on the page cold and asking whether every question on your list is answered without leaving it.

**Pitfall:** A landing page that repeats the keyword but hides pricing and integrations. It matches on wording and still bounces, because the visitor cannot finish evaluating without leaving.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's paid destinations need reviews, features, integrations and pricing on the same page. Since Pabau includes every feature in every subscription, the pricing section can state that plainly, which removes a common objection.

**Apply anywhere:** Choose a destination that carries the keyword in the title and then answers the whole buying question: reviews, features, benefits, integrations and pricing on one page. Missing pricing is the most common reason a matched page still bounces.

### 13. Place product mentions in emails 1, 4 and 6 of an eight-email course  `155.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 155*

Erika's autoresponder follows a fixed ask pattern that Grow and Convert lay out email by email. Email 1 is a welcome that shows her transformation and introduces the $15 Just Start program, catching people while they are still on the post-opt-in high. Emails 2 and 3 deliver pure value on strength conditioning and cardio with no ask at all. Email 4 covers active rest and how to fit the week together, then gently mentions the premium plans at the bottom. Email 5 tackles belly fat, a core pain point. Email 6 is the first hard sell, landing after almost a week of lessons. Email 7 covers willpower and reminds readers of the guide in the PS. Email 8 closes the course.

> "In emails 1, 4 and 6"

**Evidence:** Eight-email sequence with asks in 1, 4 and 6 produced $632 of sales against $492 ad spend.

**How to do it**

1. Write eight emails and assign each one a single teaching topic before writing any sales copy.
2. Put the paid offer in the welcome email as an optional next step, not a pitch.
3. Keep emails 2 and 3 completely free of any product mention.
4. Add a soft one-line mention at the foot of email 4, framed as the thing that puts the lessons together.
5. Use email 5 to name the reader's sharpest pain point with no ask.
6. Make email 6 the single hard-sell email, once about a week of value has landed.
7. Reduce email 7's ask to a PS reminder.
8. Close the course in email 8 and move subscribers to the main list.

**Tools:** Mailchimp

**Pitfall:** Selling in emails 2 and 3 breaks the trust the sequence is built on. The hard sell only works because the reader has already had days of usable advice.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's lead-magnet follow-up emails should follow this cadence rather than pitching a demo in every send. Put the demo ask in emails 1, 4 and 6 and keep 2, 3 and 5 purely instructional for practice owners.

**Apply anywhere:** Structure an eight-email nurture course so the offer appears in emails 1, 4 and 6 only, with the single hard sell held back to email 6.

### 14. Point every content CTA at the product instead of the email list  `173.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 173*

KlientBoost deliberately did not put effort into growing an email list. Every call to action on their content pointed at the product instead, meaning get a proposal, request a demo, or talk to sales. Grow and Convert note they have written about and modeled this choice extensively, and offer a spreadsheet model for deciding which of the two a given article should do. The result KlientBoost reports two years in is about 50,000 monthly unique visitors producing 30 to 40 inbound leads a week, roughly 150 a month. That is a 0.4% visitor-to-lead conversion rate, which Grow and Convert call good and very achievable for B2B. The decision is a deliberate trade: no nurture list, but every visitor gets exactly one next step and it is a revenue step.

> "they focused all calls to action on their product"

**Evidence:** 50,000 monthly uniques producing 30-40 leads a week, about 150 a month, a 0.4% conversion rate, with no email list effort.

**How to do it**

1. List every CTA currently on your blog: newsletter boxes, gated PDFs, demo buttons, and count them per article.
2. For each article, decide whether the reader is close enough to buying that a product CTA is credible.
3. Replace the newsletter CTA with a product CTA on every article where the answer is yes.
4. Use one product CTA per article rather than stacking demo, download and subscribe together.
5. Place it in the body near the point where the article solves the reader's problem, not only at the end.
6. Measure visitor-to-lead rate monthly and benchmark against 0.4% for a B2B content site.
7. Keep gated assets only where the partner distribution or the sales handoff genuinely needs them.
8. Re-run the decision per article as pages age, since a page's intent mix shifts.

**Pitfall:** Running both CTA types on the same page splits attention and usually lowers both. The failure signal is a growing list that produces no pipeline while demo requests stay flat.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog and template pages should each carry the book-demo CTA as the single next step rather than competing with template downloads. Where a template download exists, David should keep the demo link as the closing CTA in the Conclusion so the revenue path is never the second option.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide per article whether the reader is ready for a product CTA or a list CTA, then run only one. If your content already attracts buyers, sending everyone to a demo or proposal request outperforms building a list you then have to nurture.

### 15. Prefer a built contextual path over a popup or widget  `138.8`
*core · content insights · source 138*

Nat Eliason's closing lesson is that what moved Cup & Leaf readers from reading to shopping was not a fancy popup or another widget on the blog. It was creating contextual product collections. He is explicit that this is the point rather than a drawback: it is not as easy as slapping up a new popup, and the effort is what makes it work. A popup is generic by construction, so it interrupts every reader with the same offer. A collection built for the article's exact topic continues the reader's own train of thought. The practical consequence for a content team is that conversion work on a blog is a build task shared with the store or product team, not a plugin someone installs in an afternoon.

> "wasn't just a fancy popup or"

**Evidence:** Cup & Leaf's sales from SEO rose over 300% in two months on about 175% more traffic, attributed to contextual collections rather than to any on-blog widget.

**Pitfall:** Teams reach for the popup because it is the only lever the content team controls alone. It produces a small lift and then stalls, and the real gain stays locked behind work that needs the store or product team.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should budget conversion work on the blog as engineering time for matched feature and use-case destinations, not as another exit-intent modal. The modal is not where the 300% came from.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat blog conversion as a build task, not a plugin. A popup applies the same offer to every reader; a destination built for the article's exact topic continues what the reader was already doing.

### 16. Price a service by pricing the in-house team it replaces  `169.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 169*

Hyam and Devesh Khanal set Grow and Convert's price by costing the alternative, not by surveying competitors. They listed every role a working in-house content operation needs and attached a real salary range to each: a mid or senior content marketing manager at $80k-120k+, an optional full-time editor at $50k-120k, freelance writers at $250-1k per article, an optional analytics consultant at $50-100/hr, promotion help or paid budget at $1k-3k a month, an SEO strategist at $3k-5k a month part-time, and freelance developer time. The floor came out around $100k a year for just a manager and articles, and the full stack $300k-500k. Against that, $72,000 a year read as a bargain. They opened at $6,000 a month with a plan to step to $7k and $8k, and now charge $10k.

> "you're looking at around $300k–500k per year"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert launched at $6,000/month against a $100k-500k/year in-house cost stack, landed its first two clients within weeks, and has since raised to $10,000/month with rare price pushback.

**How to do it**

1. List every role a customer would need to hire to get the outcome you sell, including the optional ones.
2. Attach a real salary or rate range to each role from current market data, not from memory.
3. Compute a low-end total that covers only the mandatory roles and a high-end total that covers the full stack.
4. Add benefits, healthcare and tooling on top so the comparison is honest.
5. Set your annual price meaningfully below the low-end total so the value gap is obvious on a sales call.
6. Divide by twelve for the monthly figure and check it against what buyers in your market already pay other vendors.
7. Start at the lower end while you are unproven, and write down the step-ups you will take as case studies accumulate.
8. Rehearse the comparison as a single sentence for the call: our price versus their build cost.

**Pitfall:** Costing only the obvious roles. Hyam's list is mostly optional line items, and those are what turn a $100k comparison into a $300k-500k one; leave them out and your price looks expensive.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's pricing and comparison pages should cost out the alternative stack a clinic runs without it, front desk hours, separate booking tool, separate marketing tool, paper notes, and put the annual total next to the subscription. Same move on the 'Pabau vs' template pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Price against the cost of the thing your buyer would otherwise build. Cost every role including the optional ones, total the low and high ends, then set your annual price clearly under the low end and lead the sales call with that comparison.

### 17. Publishing volume and promotion spend barely move content CAC  `152.7`
*core · content insights · source 152*

Two levers content teams argue about most turned out to be nearly irrelevant in Grow and Convert's model. Raising promotion spend per article from $0 to $500 changed CAC from $93 to $130. The variable cost is small relative to the traffic upside it buys.

> "That changed the CAC from $93 – $130. Not a big deal."

**Evidence:** Promotion $0-$500 gave CAC $93-$130; 1 to 8 articles a month gave CAC $78-$113.

**Pitfall:** The 'without reducing quality' clause carries the whole argument. Scaling from one to eight posts a month with the same editorial capacity usually cuts quality, and the traffic gain that justified the spend never arrives.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not treat freelance writing fees or article promotion as the place to economize. Cutting a $300 writing fee saves less than it costs in lost traffic on a page that could rank.

### 18. Put a CTA after paragraph one, at 25/50/75%, and a popup at 30%  `77.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 77*

Schneider's answer to how automated content produces leads is placement, not volume. Calls to action are scattered through the page: one after the first paragraph, then further CTAs at 25%, 50% and 75% scroll depth, with a popup fired at 30% scroll. Signups from those pages are then tracked back to the article. This is close to what source 56 describes, with one specific disagreement worth recording: source 56 fires the pop-ups themselves at 25%, 50% and 75%, while Schneider uses a single popup at 30% and keeps the other three depths as inline units. The 30% figure is early — it catches the reader who has committed to the page but has not yet reached the point where they leave. Schneider's implicit position is that a bottom-funnel article without this CTA scaffolding is a traffic asset, not a lead asset.

> "after first paragraph, 25% scroll rate, 50% scroll rate"

**Evidence:** Schneider lists the exact placements: after the first paragraph, 25%, 50% and 75% scroll, with a popup at 30%, and says signups from these pages are then tracked.

**How to do it**

1. Insert an inline CTA block immediately after the first paragraph of every bottom-funnel article.
2. Add three more inline CTA blocks triggered or placed at 25%, 50% and 75% scroll depth.
3. Configure exactly one modal popup to fire at 30% scroll depth, not on exit and not on a timer.
4. Vary the design and wording of the four inline units so the page does not read as one CTA repeated.
5. Point every unit at the same conversion action so attribution stays clean.
6. Fire a single custom conversion event on signup and attach the landing-page URL to it.
7. Report signups by article, not sessions by article, and use the gaps between topics to pick what the agent writes next.
8. Cap it at one popup per session so a returning reader is not hit repeatedly.

**Tools:** Google Analytics 4, PostHog

**Pitfall:** Firing the popup and the inline units at the same depth means the reader gets two interruptions within a few hundred pixels and bounces. Schneider's 30% popup deliberately sits between the 25% and 50% inline placements.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog articles carry one Pabau section and a book-demo link near the end. Adding scroll-depth CTA units earlier in the article, with signup or demo events reported per URL, would show which topics actually produce bookings rather than which produce sessions.

**Apply anywhere:** Scatter CTAs through the article instead of parking one at the end: after the first paragraph and at 25%, 50% and 75% scroll, plus one popup at 30%. Track the resulting signups per URL so you learn which topics convert.

### 19. Put an unclickable naked domain in your cold email signature  `59.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 59*

The most immediately usable tactic in the episode. Jackie found that including a naked domain - just example.com as plain text, not a clickable link - in the cold email signature produces about a 10% direct visit rate: blast 100 people a day and 10 of them will type the domain and visit the site. The reason it can't be a link is deliverability: making it clickable reduces deliverability, so the domain has to be unclickable. This pairs with his agency positioning advice, which is to niche the domain itself - easyroofing.com rather than a generic agency name - so the domain does the qualifying. Cody's variation on the same problem: send the first email with no links at all so it inboxes, then a second email a day later carrying the hard CTA and the link. Jackie's broader reframe is that he doesn't care about reply rate - he's thinking about how to cold email 100,000 people in his exact ICP, get 40% to open it, and treat that as an impression, with the second email simply driving traffic to the application.

> "you should always include a naked domain"

**How to do it**

1. Choose a domain that describes the specific service and niche, not your company name.
2. Put that domain as plain unlinked text in the email signature.
3. Do not hyperlink it - the deliverability cost is what makes the naked version work.
4. Send the first email with no links at all so it lands in the inbox.
5. Send the second email a day later with the explicit CTA and link.
6. Make sure the destination page can convert a cold visitor: a VSL, a strong offer, and a booking link.
7. Measure direct visits and signups rather than reply rate.

**Tools:** Calendly

**Pitfall:** The volume model this sits inside - 100,000 cold emails to an ICP treated as impressions - is unsolicited bulk email, which carries CAN-SPAM and UK GDPR exposure regardless of how the links are formatted.

**Apply at Pabau:** The formatting insight transfers to any outreach Pabau does: a plain-text domain in the signature converts, links in a first-touch email hurt deliverability, and the destination page has to be built for a cold visitor.

**Apply anywhere:** The formatting insight transfers to any outreach: a plain-text domain in the signature converts, links in a first-touch email hurt deliverability, and the destination page has to be built for a cold visitor.

### 20. Put the search term in both the ad headline and the description  `157.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 157*

Grow and Convert's rule is that ideally both the ad headline and the ad copy contain the search term the ad was built to show for. Their worked example is the query 'accounting software for small business'. NetSuite's ad headline reads 'NetSuite Accounting Software - #1 SMB Cloud Accounting' and the first line of the description includes both 'accounting software' and 'small business', which tells the searcher immediately that the ad is for them. The QuickBooks ad below it omits 'software' and 'small business' from both headline and copy. They note QuickBooks is a massive brand and can get away with it, but that a lesser-known brand needs the exact search terms in its ad copy to win the click against competitors. Brand strength is the variable that decides how much term-matching you need.

> "both your ad headline and ad copy should include the search term"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert compare NetSuite's term-matched ad with QuickBooks' non-matched ad on the query 'accounting software for small business'.

**How to do it**

1. For each ad group, write down the single search term the group exists to serve.
2. Write headline one to contain that term verbatim, prefixed or suffixed with your brand name.
3. Write the first description line to contain the term plus its qualifier, for example both 'accounting software' and 'small business'.
4. Keep the remaining headlines for differentiators, proof points and the offer.
5. Search the term yourself and compare your ad against the competitor ads on the page for term coverage.
6. If you are the less-known brand in the auction, match the term more literally than the incumbent does.
7. Track click-through rate per ad group and rewrite any ad below the account average.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Copying the ad style of the category leader. A dominant brand can win clicks without the search term in the headline; a challenger doing the same loses the auction on relevance.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau is the challenger in most of its auctions, so ad headlines should carry the literal query, for example 'Aesthetic Clinic Software', rather than a brand-led line. The same principle applies to Pabau's SERP titles.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the search term into both the headline and the first description line. If you are the challenger rather than the category leader, match the query more literally than the incumbent does.

### 21. Put the store CTA immediately after the introductory paragraph  `138.7`
*core · best practices · source 138*

In Cup & Leaf's best black tea article, the inline link and the visual call to action both sit right after the introductory paragraph. Nat Eliason names the position explicitly. It matters because a reader searching 'best black tea' has already decided to buy black tea; making them scroll through a roundup before offering the shortcut wastes the highest-attention moment on the page. The two link formats run together at that position rather than one or the other, so both the scanner and the reader have something to click. Deeper in the article the section-matched callouts take over. This is a placement rule, not a copy rule: the same CTA lower down performs worse.

> "Right after the introductory paragraph"

**Evidence:** Cup & Leaf's best black tea article carries both an inline and a visual CTA immediately after the intro paragraph, both pointing to the black tea collection, which then showed significant traffic growth.

**How to do it**

1. Identify the article's introductory paragraph, the one that states what the post covers.
2. Immediately after it, insert an inline text link to the matching collection using anchor text naming the collection, for example 'find the black tea you're looking for'.
3. Directly beneath that, insert the visual CTA block pointing at the same URL.
4. Do not use a generic 'shop now' button; the wording should restate the article's own topic.
5. Keep the rest of the article's CTAs section-matched rather than repeating this one.
6. Compare click-through rate for this position against a lower placement on two or three articles before rolling it out site-wide.

**Pitfall:** Pushing the store link to the end of the roundup so the article 'earns' the click. The reader arriving on a 'best X' query already has commercial intent, and the delay just loses the ones who bounce mid-article.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau's comparison and 'best software for X' articles, the first CTA should sit right after the intro and name the reader's job, not wait for the Pabau section before the conclusion. The Pabau section stays where the block contract puts it; this is an additional early link.

**Apply anywhere:** Put the first commercial link immediately after the introductory paragraph on any bottom-funnel roundup, as both an inline text link and a visual block, with anchor text that restates the article's topic.

### 22. Qualify inbound leads on headcount and ad spend before any call  `173.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 173*

At 30 to 40 inbound leads a week, KlientBoost needed a filter, because most were unqualified. Dane's process routes every web lead to an account executive who checks two things before any human contact: LinkedIn for whether the company has a reasonable employee count, and SpyFu for whether it is spending enough on ads to afford management. Only after that does anyone reach out. The first call re-validates the SpyFu number by asking the prospect directly how much they spend on ads. Dane says only 3 to 4 of the 30 to 40 weekly leads survive to the free-audit stage, roughly a 10% pass rate. That harsh filter is what makes the later steps affordable, because the free audit only ever goes to companies with budget.

> "uses LinkedIn to see if the company has a decent employee count"

**Evidence:** KlientBoost took 30-40 leads a week from 50,000 monthly visitors and let only 3-4 through to the audit stage, while holding 80 clients and $300,000 monthly recurring revenue.

**How to do it**

1. Route every inbound form fill to one owner rather than a shared inbox.
2. Look the company up on LinkedIn and record headcount against a minimum threshold you set for your price point.
3. Check spend or scale in a third-party tool such as SpyFu before contacting them.
4. Disqualify silently and quickly if either check fails; do not book a call to be polite.
5. Reach out only to survivors, and open the first call by asking directly what they currently spend.
6. Compare their answer to your tool estimate and treat a large gap as a qualification flag.
7. Track the pass rate weekly; if far more than 10% of leads survive, your thresholds are too loose.
8. Only start unpaid work such as an audit after both checks and the spend question have passed.

**Tools:** LinkedIn, SpyFu

**Pitfall:** Skipping the pre-call research and qualifying live burns a sales hour on every unqualified lead. The signal is a calendar full of calls and a flat close rate.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's demo requests should be scored before booking: practice size, number of practitioners, and whether they are running paid acquisition. David should have the pre-demo check written into the SDR workflow so unqualified single-room clinics get a self-serve path instead of a sales hour.

**Apply anywhere:** Score every inbound lead on two objective external checks, company size and spend, before a human touches it. Set the thresholds so roughly one in ten survives, and only invest unpaid discovery work in the survivors.

### 24. Reallocate spend manually to the search terms that produced leads  `157.14`
*core · concrete actions · source 157*

Grow and Convert's third strategy step is manual optimization instead of automation. Within a single ad group they commonly see that certain keywords and search terms produce quality conversions while others take only a few clicks and go nowhere. The action is to shift spend and effort onto the terms that are working, which raises return on ad spend directly. This is the counterpart to turning off broad match, DSAs and smart campaigns: once you have removed Google's automation, you are the optimization loop, and the loop runs on the search terms report rather than on keyword-level data alone. They frame paid search as demanding this because you pay for every click, so control over where spend lands is the whole point.

> "certain keywords or search terms will result in quality conversions"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe finding, inside one ad group, some keywords producing quality conversions and others only a few clicks.

**How to do it**

1. Open the search terms report, not the keywords report, for the last 30 days.
2. Segment by conversions and mark every term that produced a qualified lead, checking quality with sales rather than form fills alone.
3. Add each converting search term as its own exact match keyword so you bid on it directly.
4. Raise bids or move budget toward the ad groups holding those terms.
5. Negate the terms that spent money with no conversions after a meaningful click volume.
6. Pause keywords that have accumulated clicks over several weeks without a single qualified lead.
7. Repeat weekly at first, then monthly once the account stabilizes.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should judge paid search terms on qualified demo bookings recorded in the CRM, not on form submissions, since aesthetic practice enquiries vary widely in fit.
