### 1. Decide leads or traffic in writing before you pick any topic  `107.1`
*core · best practices · source 107*

Grow and Convert argue the first strategy decision is one most B2B teams never consciously make: is the blog for leads or for traffic and brand awareness. They say almost every B2B company they have worked with over 10+ years actually wants leads, and treats traffic as the means. A traffic-first strategy only makes sense if you are already the category leader, their examples being Salesforce and QuickBooks. Traffic-first thinking rests on two assumptions they call flawed: that some share of thousands of readers must convert, and that email capture plus a drip sequence will eventually create buyers. Reading an industry article does not mean someone is in the market. Once the goal is written down as leads, every later choice follows from it, including which keywords are allowed onto the calendar.

> "almost everyone is doing it for leads, traffic is just a means"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated experience across 10+ years of B2B content engagements.

**How to do it**

1. Write one sentence stating whether the blog exists for leads or for traffic, and get leadership to sign it.
2. If the answer is leads, add a rule that no keyword enters the calendar unless a buyer would plausibly search it.
3. List your current top ten blog posts by traffic and mark which ones a buyer in-market would search for.
4. Report leads and pipeline from the blog as the headline metric, with sessions as a secondary line.
5. Only choose traffic-first if you are already the category leader and the goal is explicitly mind-share.
6. Revisit the statement each quarter, because pressure to chase volume returns every planning cycle.

**Pitfall:** Teams say leads but report traffic, so the calendar quietly fills with top-of-funnel topics. The signal is a blog with rising sessions and flat demo requests.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should state on the Pabau content plan that the blog exists to generate demo requests from aesthetic and healthcare practices, then audit the calendar against that. Any article that no practice owner would search while shopping for software goes to a lower tier.

**Apply anywhere:** Write down whether your blog exists for leads or for traffic, get it agreed, and then judge every planned topic against that one sentence. Report the chosen metric first in every content update.

### 2. Decide scale or lifestyle explicitly before choosing any growth tactic  `170.8`
*core · best practices · source 170*

Campbell's central piece of advice is to make the choice consciously and not straddle it. 'Either you decide on lifestyle or you decide on scale but don't straddle the line.' He is careful to say the lifestyle choice is respectable and that his advice simply does not apply to it: 'If you want to just build a lifestyle business, this advice doesn't really matter.' The reason the decision matters is that it changes what you do with margin. The scale path means reinvesting it, which he says is where he sees other early entrepreneurs trip up. He frames the test sharply: if you make $150k a year and 20% is left over, does it go into the business or into a down payment on a house.

> "Either you decide on lifestyle or you decide on scale"

**Evidence:** Campbell paid himself $50,000 a year in Boston as a twenty-something CEO of a profitable business doing half a million a year, so the margin could fund ProfitWell.

**How to do it**

1. Write down which of the two you are building, as a sentence, and date it.
2. Set the annual number that counts as success under that choice.
3. Decide in advance what percentage of surplus margin gets reinvested, and write it into the budget before the year starts.
4. Under the scale choice, cap founder and owner compensation at a figure that leaves the reinvestment percentage intact.
5. Judge every proposed growth tactic against the written choice, not against the last thing you read.
6. Revisit the decision once a year, deliberately, rather than letting it drift with cash flow.

**Pitfall:** Straddling. Campbell says the discipline is easier to say than to do, and founders with scale ambitions who quietly take the margin as income never fund the thing that would have scaled.

**Apply at Pabau:** The same logic governs Pabau's content budget. David should fix the annual number of articles and visuals up front as a reinvestment commitment, so the queue survives quarters when short-term demand-gen spend looks more attractive.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide in writing whether you are building for scale or for lifestyle, then set the reinvestment percentage before the year starts. Judge every growth tactic against that written choice rather than against current cash comfort.

### 3. Defend branded keywords with both organic pages and paid ads  `37.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 37*

Jotform targets its own top branded keywords - its brand name, 'Jotform login,' 'Jotform pricing' - with both a strong organic page and paid search ads simultaneously, specifically so competitors can't intercept that demand. The risk is real and visible: searching 'Jotform pricing' surfaces a competitor page from Zyte titled 'Jotform pricing breakdown, plans, costs, and limits, 2026' and another from checkthat.ai titled 'Jotform pricing 2026' - third parties building content specifically to capture searchers who already wanted Jotform. Jotform also runs paid ads on important non-branded terms it wants to keep dominating, like 'survey' or 'job application template.'

> "running paid ads on their top branded keywords"

**How to do it**

1. List your top branded search terms - your brand name alone, '[brand] login,' '[brand] pricing,' '[brand] reviews,' '[brand] alternative,' and similar.
2. Confirm you have a strong, well-optimized organic page owning each of these terms, such as an actual pricing page for '[brand] pricing.'
3. Run paid search ads on these same branded terms in parallel with your organic presence, even though you already rank organically for your own brand name.
4. Periodically search your own brand name plus 'pricing,' 'alternative,' 'review,' and 'vs [competitor]' to check who else is ranking there.
5. If you find third-party pages built specifically to intercept your branded searches, treat that as evidence competitors or comparison sites are actively targeting your brand demand.
6. Prioritize defending branded terms with both organic and paid once your brand is large enough to attract third-party pricing/comparison content.

**Pitfall:** Assuming that ranking organically for your own brand name is protection enough ignores that competitors and comparison sites will build pages specifically targeting '[brand] pricing' or '[brand] alternative' - without paid ads and strong owned pages on these terms too, that traffic (searchers who already know and want you) can be intercepted before they reach you.

### 4. Delay top-of-funnel content until bottom-funnel keywords are exhausted  `103.7`
*core · best practices · source 103*

Grow and Convert are explicit that they are not anti-top-of-funnel, but they name only two conditions for starting it. First, when they have worked with a client for two or more years and exhausted much of the bottom-of-funnel keyword set; at that point top-funnel content builds topical authority, which strengthens rankings for the conversion pages. Second, when the product is very specific and simply has few bottom-funnel keywords to begin with, which is common for startups in niche categories and new industries. Outside those two cases, top-funnel comes later because bottom-funnel content converts so much better per piece. They add that AI answer engines now handle broad educational queries without sending traffic, so the traffic case for top-funnel is weaker than it was.

> "exhausted much of their bottom of funnel keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use a two-year engagement plus keyword exhaustion as the practical trigger for moving up-funnel.

**How to do it**

1. Count how many bottom- and middle-funnel keywords from your matrix still have no page; if the number is above zero, do not start top-funnel.
2. Set the exhaustion test explicitly: every category, comparison, template and JTBD variant either published or checked off as unwinnable.
3. If the product is genuinely niche and the bottom-funnel list runs to only a handful of terms, allow top-funnel from the start.
4. When you do start, pick top-funnel topics that sit inside the same cluster as your ranking conversion pages.
5. Internally link every new top-funnel page to the bottom-funnel page it supports.
6. Measure the top-funnel batch on assisted and first-interaction conversions, not on sessions.
7. Cut any top-funnel topic whose answer an AI engine gives fully in one paragraph.

**Pitfall:** Teams declare the bottom-funnel list exhausted after publishing the head terms, when the qualified variants and comparison pages are still untouched. That is not exhaustion, it is impatience.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog has many general aesthetics and clinic-marketing articles while comparison and vertical category pages are missing. The order should reverse: finish the bottom-funnel matrix, then use top-funnel pieces to support those pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Only move up-funnel after the bottom-funnel keyword matrix is genuinely exhausted, or if your product is niche enough that the list is short. Then pick top-funnel topics that support pages already converting.

### 5. Demand conversion data from past clients before signing an agency  `179.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 179*

Grow and Convert say the fastest tell in an agency sales call is what the agency reaches for when you ask how it measures success. If the conversation stays on rankings and traffic with no mention of leads or revenue, that is how the agency thinks about SEO. They tell buyers to ask the agency to walk through how its work translated into business results for companies similar to yours, and to ask for the underlying data. Two answers disqualify: an inability to produce the data at all, and a pivot to domain authority and search rankings. Both mean the agency is not tracking conversions, so it cannot know whether its own work paid back. They add that this is not a rare failure; many agencies do not report on conversions in any form.

> "pivot to talking about domain authority and search rankings"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say they see the same story repeatedly: businesses pay retainers, get ranking and traffic reports, and see no change in pipeline.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the agency, in the first call, how they measure success for clients, and say nothing else until they answer.
2. Note whether leads, demos, signups or revenue appear unprompted in the answer, or only rankings and traffic.
3. Ask them to walk through one named client in your industry from published page to closed business.
4. Ask to see the report or dashboard behind that story, not a slide summarizing it.
5. Treat a pivot to domain authority or average position as a disqualification, not a partial answer.
6. Ask what conversion tracking they would set up on your site in month one, and who owns it.
7. Run the identical question set at every agency on the shortlist so the answers are comparable.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Prompt / template:**

```text
How do you measure success for your clients? Walk me through how your SEO work translated into actual business results for a company similar to ours, and show me the data.
```

**Pitfall:** Accepting a case study that shows a traffic curve only. Traffic growth with no conversion line is the exact artifact this filter exists to catch, and it looks impressive in a deck.

**Apply at Pabau:** If David commissions outside SEO help for pabau.com, the trial should be scored on demo requests attributable to the specific pages produced, not on sessions. Set up the conversion goal before the first article ships so the data exists to judge by.

**Apply anywhere:** Before hiring an SEO agency, ask how they measure success and listen for whether leads or revenue appear without prompting. Make them walk one similar client from a published page to closed business and show the underlying report. If they cannot produce it, or they redirect to domain authority and rankings, they are not tracking conversions and cannot prove their work pays back.

### 6. Design valid tests before spending big on an unproven tactic  `30.7`
*core · best practices · source 30*

ZipSprout spent $25,000 testing whether nonprofit sponsorship links could move Google Business Profile rankings, but the study was set up without a rigorous methodology, so the result was only 'possible movement' — not conclusive enough to act on despite the real spend. Garrett's explicit takeaway is that before paying to test whether any tactic is scalable or worth the investment, you need to design the test properly upfront (clear metric, defined success threshold, a comparison baseline) rather than spending first and trying to interpret ambiguous results afterward.

> "that's a nice $25,000 lesson for everybody"

**How to do it**

1. Before running a paid test of a new tactic, write down the specific metric you'll measure and the numeric threshold that would count as a real effect.
2. Select a control set of pages or listings that will not receive the treatment, matched as closely as possible to the test set on starting conditions.
3. Record baseline values for the chosen metric on both test and control groups before starting.
4. Apply the treatment only to the test group while holding other variables constant.
5. Set a fixed evaluation checkpoint in advance rather than judging results as they trickle in.
6. At the checkpoint, compare test vs. control against the pre-defined threshold rather than rationalizing ambiguous movement as a win.
7. If the result is inconclusive, treat that as a signal to redesign the experiment (larger sample, real control, longer duration) before spending further budget on the same unproven tactic.

**Pitfall:** Spending significant budget on a test without a predefined control or success threshold produces data too ambiguous to act on — Garrett calls his own $25,000 GBP-link test 'worse than an anecdote... shitty data' precisely because the study wasn't designed to actually prove anything either way.

### 7. Digital gravity: buyers don't move down a funnel, they enter orbits  `61.1`
*core · content insights · source 61*

Jordan Mix of Late Checkout describes the model he and Cody developed as the correction to funnel thinking. Nobody buys in a linear fashion, so instead of building a funnel you build orbits. Every asset you create is another orbit ring, and as you add rings your planet's mass grows, which pulls more people into orbit. Someone sees an ad and enters your space, sees a video and enters a new orbit, sees a newsletter and enters another - and all that time your mass is attracting more people because it's getting bigger. He ties it to the old law of seven (needing seven brand exposures before a buying decision) and says it's probably more now, given retargeting hits people 20 times before a purchase. The payoff is occupying digital real estate in the consumer's brain, so when they are ready to purchase, you are who they go to. His refinement of the metaphor is the useful part: real orbits are elliptical, not circular, so at different moments different people are closer to a purchase - and you can't know where someone is in that journey, which is precisely why constant presence is required rather than a timed sequence. Cody adds that each ring is itself a mini funnel, and Jordan's alternative framing is rabbit-holing: someone discovers you in one place and then consumes everything everywhere, which is why podcasts work - inbound leads arrive saying they listened to 40 hours.

> "no one buys in a linear fashion like a funnel"

**How to do it**

1. List every asset and channel where your brand is currently visible - each is one orbit ring.
2. Judge additions by whether they add mass in the space your buyer already occupies, not by channel fashion.
3. Accept that you can't know where a given person is in their journey, and plan for continuous presence over sequenced nurture.
4. Make each ring a functioning mini funnel with its own next step, rather than a pure awareness play.
5. Create depth for the rabbit-holers: enough long-form material that a motivated prospect can consume hours of it.
6. Measure the aggregate - branded search and inbound - rather than trying to attribute each ring.

**Pitfall:** Jordan's own caution is that people try to build every ring at once and end up looking like Saturn when they should be Mercury - the model describes where you're going, not where to start.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the practical reading is that the podcast, the templates, the YouTube videos and the newsletter aren't separate campaigns competing for budget - they are rings, and the measurable output of all of them together is branded search.

**Apply anywhere:** The practical reading is that the podcast, the templates, the videos and the newsletter aren't separate campaigns competing for budget - they are rings, and the measurable output of all of them together is branded search.

### 8. Discount validation that came from a high-touch delivery format  `183.3`
*core · best practices · source 183*

Grow and Convert's 10 phone-course buyers all praised the course, so they treated demand as validated and built a scalable online version. Post-course interviews said otherwise. Many buyers said the main reason they purchased was the four hour-long one-to-one calls with both founders, and several called $750 for that access a no-brainer. The thing that sold was the access, not the curriculum. The authors flag this as the critical logical misstep: 15-20 happy customers of a high-touch product does not validate the same material sold as a self-serve product. When you strip the delivery format, you have changed the product, so the demand evidence does not carry over.

> "the main reason they purchased was _because_ they got 1:1 phone time"

**Evidence:** After 15-20 companies bought and praised the phone and in-person training, the self-serve online course reached under half its revenue goal.

**How to do it**

1. Write down which delivery format produced your existing proof: one-to-one calls, in-person days, self-serve, or a done-for-you service.
2. Ask every buyer of the high-touch version what the main reason for purchase was, and record the exact phrase.
3. If access to people is named more than the material is, mark the demand as validating access, not content.
4. Before building the self-serve version, run a separate paid test of it at a real price to a fresh segment.
5. Keep the seat cap small so a failed self-serve test costs little.
6. If the self-serve test underperforms, keep the high-touch offer and raise the price instead of scaling the wrong thing.
7. Never count customers of the high-touch version toward the self-serve version's demand evidence.

**Pitfall:** Counting praise from high-touch customers as proof for a scalable product. Grow and Convert call this their critical logical misstep, and the signal is that buyers describe the people, not the material, when asked why they bought.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's demo-led sales motion is high touch. If David tests a self-serve signup path, he should not assume demo-stage praise transfers. Ask new customers what actually made them buy, and if the answer is the demo call, the self-serve page needs a different proof mechanism, not the same copy.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not carry demand evidence across a change in delivery format. If buyers of a high-touch version say they bought for the access, the self-serve version is a different product and needs its own validation test.

### 9. Double down on a keyword pattern competitors have not adopted  `120.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 120*

Grow and Convert describe a client in a niche where competitors had not yet adopted the competitor-versus-competitor keyword strategy. Despite the client's low domain rating, they achieved position 1 rankings for those terms, and the pages drove substantial conversions even though the search volume was low. Their response was to prioritize more keywords of the same shape rather than treat the wins as one-offs. The wider point is that monitoring exists to find a repeatable pattern, not just to report progress. When a subset of keywords proves either easier to rank for or unusually productive on conversions, the queue should tilt toward that subset. They pair this with the reminder that low volume is not a reason to skip a term when the conversion rate is high.

> "competitors hadn't yet adopted the"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client reached position 1 for competitor-versus-competitor terms despite a low domain rating, and those low-volume terms drove substantial conversions.

**How to do it**

1. Group every published page by keyword pattern: category, competitor versus competitor, competitor alternatives, pain point.
2. Record position and conversions per page in one sheet using Ahrefs and Google Analytics.
3. Calculate average position and total conversions per pattern, not per page.
4. Identify any pattern where you hold top three positions despite a low domain rating.
5. Check whether competitors publish that pattern at all; an empty field is why it was cheap.
6. Enumerate every remaining term of that shape, including the ones with negligible volume.
7. Load the next quarter's queue mostly with that pattern while the window stays open.
8. Re-check quarterly, because the pattern stops being cheap once competitors copy it.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Reading the win as proof your site is now strong overall. It is proof one keyword pattern is uncontested in your niche, and that advantage disappears as soon as a competitor starts publishing the same shape.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should check whether aesthetic practice software competitors publish versus-comparison pages at all. If the field is empty, David should ship a run of them quickly rather than one test.

**Apply anywhere:** Group published pages by keyword pattern and measure position and conversions per pattern. When one pattern wins despite a weak domain, check that competitors ignore it, then fill the queue with every remaining term of that shape.

### 10. Drop high-volume tangential topics with no credible next step  `66.27`
*core · best practices · source 66*

Content targeting search demand that is only loosely related to the organization's product, service, expertise or audience outcome. The framework's verdict is direct: even if it attracts visits, it has no credible next step or business value, and it is often fully satisfied by an AI answer anyway. It covers high-volume lifestyle, career, trend or industry topics that are only loosely related to the site and offer no useful next step. It scores Low on all five value dimensions at Medium effort. This type is the hardest to cut politically, because it is usually the traffic the content programme reports on — the pages with the biggest impression numbers and the best-looking growth chart. The framework's answer is that traffic is not one of the six dimensions. Business value is defined as supporting acquisition, conversion, retention or support, and a visitor with no credible route to any of those has not created value regardless of how many of them there are.

> "targets search demand that is only loosely related to the organization's product, service, expertise or audience outcome"

**How to do it**

1. List the site's highest-traffic pages and, for each, write the credible next step a visitor takes toward a business outcome. Not a hypothetical one — one you can see in the data.
2. Pull assisted conversions and returning-visitor rates for those pages to test whether the next step actually happens.
3. Flag the pages where traffic is high and the next step is absent or unused. That set is this content type.
4. Check whether the topic connects to your genuine expertise or audience outcome. Loose topical adjacency is not a connection.
5. Stop commissioning the category, and remove its keywords from the research pipeline so they do not reappear as 'opportunities'.
6. For existing pages, decide by performance: consolidate the few that feed a real journey into the relevant hub, and leave or retire the rest rather than investing further.
7. Reset the reporting so the content programme is measured on outcomes by content type instead of on sessions, because sessions are what keep this category funded.
8. Redirect the freed capacity to the prioritized types, which are more expensive per page and where the return actually sits.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Defending the category with its traffic numbers. Traffic is not one of the six scored dimensions — business value is defined against acquisition, conversion, retention or support, and volume without a next step scores Low no matter how large it is.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau this is the loosely-related wellness, career and general business content that draws impressions with no route to a demo. Judge it on assisted conversions rather than sessions, stop commissioning it, and move the budget to templates, feature documentation and practice-specific guides.

**Apply anywhere:** This is the loosely-related lifestyle, career and general business content that draws impressions with no route to a conversion. Judge it on assisted conversions rather than sessions, stop commissioning it, and move the budget to product documentation, templates and audience-specific guides.

### 11. Exhaust bottom-funnel competitor keywords before moving up the funnel  `118.9`
*core · content insights · source 118*

Grow and Convert give an explicit sequencing rule rather than a balance. Target the competitor keywords with high buying intent first, because they reach ROI sooner, and only after those opportunities are exhausted move up the funnel to brand-building terms. The reasoning comes straight from the Geekbot numbers: bottom-of-funnel content produced more than three times the leads from a tenth of the traffic, so the same production budget spent bottom-first pays back far earlier. They frame top-of-funnel not as wrong but as a later phase whose case rests on brand rather than on pipeline. The practical consequence is that a content plan should be readable as a sequence, with the bottom-funnel list dated first and the awareness list dated after it, rather than as a mix balanced from month one.

> "You'll achieve ROI sooner by targeting competitor keywords"

**Evidence:** Geekbot's 22 bottom-of-funnel posts produced 1,348 leads against 397 from 42 top-of-funnel posts, from a tenth of the traffic.

**How to do it**

1. Count the unexhausted bottom-funnel keywords on your list: category, comparison and JTBD terms with no page yet.
2. If that count is above zero, do not schedule a top-of-funnel article this quarter.
3. Date the bottom-funnel list first in the calendar and only then add awareness topics after it.
4. Define 'exhausted' concretely: every category synonym has a page, every named competitor has an alternatives page, and every buyer task has a JTBD post.
5. Track leads per cohort as pages ship, so the sequencing decision is re-checked against your own data.
6. When the bottom-funnel list is genuinely exhausted, move up the funnel and justify those pieces on brand and reach, not on lead targets.
7. Revisit the bottom-funnel list whenever a competitor launches or the product ships a new module, since it refills.

**Pitfall:** Teams declare the bottom-funnel list exhausted after publishing the four or five obvious head terms, when the comparison and template variants are still untouched. Check the definition, not the feeling.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's calendar should be readable as a sequence: alternatives pages for each named practice management rival and JTBD posts for clinic tasks dated before any general aesthetics-industry explainer.

**Apply anywhere:** Schedule the whole buying-intent keyword list before any awareness content, and define exhaustion concretely as a page per category synonym, per named competitor and per buyer task.

### 12. Exhaust the three pain-point frameworks before targeting any high-volume term  `90.6`
*core · best practices · source 90*

Grow and Convert set an explicit ordering rule rather than a ban. Produce everything available in the three pain-point frameworks first, and only when you feel you have exhausted the long-tail keywords in those buckets does it make sense to take a broader approach to higher-volume keywords in your category. They note that the bottom two frameworks contain a limited set of keywords, which is why the triangle diagram gives them the smallest area, and that for some clients they have spent years working exclusively inside those two buckets without running out. The reason for the order is compounding: high-intent pages are also easier to rank because the competition is chasing volume, so the early wins arrive faster and fund the broader work later. They add that the boundaries between the buckets are blurred and that arguing over which bucket a keyword sits in does not matter; prioritizing by buying intent does.

> "it makes sense to take a broader approach to finding higher volume keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report spending years exclusively on category and comparison keywords for some clients, and that lower-volume high-intent keywords are also easier to rank for because competitors chase volume.

**How to do it**

1. Build the full candidate list in framework one: every synonym for your product category.
2. Build framework two: every '[you] vs [competitor]' and '[competitor] alternatives' pairing for every competitor named by sales.
3. Build framework three: every 'how to' query describing a task your product performs.
4. Sort the combined list by buying intent, not by volume or difficulty.
5. Publish against that list until you genuinely run out of candidates, re-running customer interviews before declaring it exhausted.
6. Do not spend time debating which bucket a borderline keyword belongs to; keep it if a searcher could plausibly buy after reading.
7. Only then open a fourth list of high-volume, low-competition terms your audience might read, and cap what share of the calendar it takes.
8. Recheck the conversion report each quarter and move budget back to the frameworks if the broader posts produce no signups.

**Pitfall:** Teams declare the bottom-funnel list exhausted after twenty posts because it is harder work to find than a volume export. Grow and Convert have run some clients for years inside the bottom two frameworks alone, so an early 'we ran out' usually means the customer research was never done.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog calendar should be checked against this order. Before commissioning another broad aesthetics industry article, confirm every competitor comparison page and every clinic-task how-to already exists.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish everything in your category, comparison and jobs-to-be-done lists before commissioning a single high-volume topic, and re-run customer research before you claim the list is exhausted.

### 13. Expect AI Overviews to shrink top-funnel traffic while bottom-funnel holds  `108.18`
*core · content insights · source 108*

Grow and Convert give the asymmetry a mechanism rather than treating AI Overviews as a general threat. Informational top-of-funnel content is being summarized directly in results, so the traffic that used to flow from it is shrinking. Bottom-of-funnel, high-intent content is largely unaffected, because a searcher with buying intent still has to click through and land on your site to sign up and use the service. An AI answer can satisfy 'what is CRM' completely; it cannot complete a trial signup. They use this to argue that a strategy already built on high-intent keywords is more resilient to AI-driven changes in search than one built on volume, and they note the top-of-funnel decline has made an already weak conversion story worse. This is a planning assumption, not a prediction: budget for informational traffic decay and hold bottom-funnel forecasts steady.

> "high-intent content remains largely unaffected because searchers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated observation that AI Overviews and zero-click results are absorbing informational queries while bottom-funnel content still requires a click-through to convert.

**How to do it**

1. Split your existing content inventory into informational and buying-intent halves by target keyword.
2. Pull the last 12 months of clicks for each half separately in Search Console.
3. Compare the trend lines; expect decay on the informational half and stability on the other.
4. Stop forecasting future traffic from informational pages at historical rates.
5. Move new production budget to category, comparison and jobs-to-be-done terms.
6. Keep informational pages that feed internal links into converting pages, and let the rest age.
7. Re-run the split every quarter so the decay rate is measured rather than assumed.
8. Judge the informational half on assisted conversions and internal link value, not on sessions.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Reading a site-wide traffic decline as an algorithmic penalty when it is informational pages losing clicks to AI answers while buying-intent pages hold steady. Splitting the inventory is what makes the difference visible.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should split blog reporting into informational and buying-intent halves before reading any traffic drop. A fall concentrated in definition-style aesthetics articles is expected, and does not justify changing the template or software pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Split your content into informational and buying-intent halves and track them separately. Expect the informational half to lose clicks to AI answers, and do not read that decline as a site-wide penalty.

### 14. Expect bottom-funnel articles to out-convert top-funnel roughly three to one  `111.8`
*core · content insights · source 111*

Grow and Convert publish the split for Geekbot: 1,348 conversions from bottom-of-funnel articles against 397 from top-of-funnel articles. That is the number behind their sequencing argument, and it is more useful than the usual claim that bottom-funnel converts better, because it also shows the top-funnel figure is not zero. They say explicitly that Geekbot still receives a nontrivial number of conversions from top-of-funnel articles, and that this is what makes continued efforts to scale content worth pursuing. So the ratio is roughly 3.4 to 1 in favor of bottom-funnel, not 50 to 1. Read it as a sequencing rule rather than a ban. Build the bottom-funnel pool first because each page is worth several times a top-funnel page, then scale into top-funnel once the pool is gone, knowing the return per page drops but stays real.

> "they still receive a nontrivial number of conversions from top of funnel articles"

**Evidence:** Geekbot: 1,348 conversions from BOF articles versus 397 from TOF articles.

**Pitfall:** Quoting the ratio as a reason never to write top-funnel content. The same data shows top-funnel produced 397 conversions, which is the whole justification for scaling past the finite bottom-funnel list.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should tag every Pabau /blog/ article as bottom or top funnel in the tracking sheet and report conversions by tag. If the ratio is far worse than 3 to 1, the top-funnel pieces are not tying back to a Pabau feature.

### 19. Find your market gap by shopping your own category as a buyer  `169.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 169*

Benji Hyam built Grow and Convert's positioning out of a buying experience, not a brainstorm. As the first marketing hire at ThinkApps he needed content help, researched agencies online and booked calls with about 10 companies over a month. Every pitch was the same: X articles per month plus 'promotion' that meant drafting social updates for the client to post, at $6k-10k+ per month. He probed two questions on each call, how do you create the content and how do you measure results, and got the same weak answers. That month of calls produced the three gaps his agency was later built on. The procedure is repeatable in any category: become the buyer, take ten calls, ask the two hardest questions, and write down every place the answer disappoints.

> "setup calls with about 10 different companies"

**Evidence:** Hyam took ~10 agency calls over a month at ThinkApps, found none accountable to leads, built in-house instead and grew the blog past 35,000 monthly uniques in a little over six months.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the service or product category you might sell into and act as a real buyer with a real requirement.
2. Search for providers and book calls with roughly 10 of them over about a month.
3. On every call ask exactly how the work gets produced and who produces it.
4. On every call ask exactly what metric they will be held accountable to, and push past 'traffic will grow over time'.
5. Ask for sample deliverables and judge whether a non-expert could have produced them by searching.
6. Record the price quoted next to the substance offered, so you can see what the market pays for what.
7. Write the disappointments as a short numbered gap list, one line each, in the buyer's words.
8. Build the offering so each bullet on that gap list becomes a named part of the service.

**Pitfall:** Running this as desk research instead of real sales calls. You only hear the evasive answers about measurement and writer sourcing when a provider thinks you are about to buy.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should have someone book demos with three or four competing practice management vendors as a prospective clinic and log where each pitch goes vague, then turn those gaps into the comparison and alternatives pages on pabau.com.

**Apply anywhere:** Before writing your positioning, buy your own category. Book about ten sales calls with competitors as a genuine prospect, ask how the work is really produced and what metric they will own, and turn every disappointing answer into a gap your offer closes.

### 20. Fix keyword intent, then product selling, then CTAs, in that order  `113.1`
*core · best practices · source 113*

Grow and Convert set out a three-step conversion-content framework and stress that the steps are ranked, not parallel. Step 1 is finding topics and keywords people at the buying stage actually search. Step 2 is writing the product or service into the content so it sells. Step 3 is CTAs, design and page optimization that support the first two. They argue that if Step 1 is wrong, no amount of Step 3 work will make content convert, and that nailing Step 1 while failing Step 2 gives traffic with no conversions. They call headline craft, formatting and visuals the window dressing of content: fine, obvious, and not that important. They have had clients arrive with good headlines, clean blog design and good user experience whose content still barely converted.

> "these steps are also in order of importance"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert built a decade of SaaS and B2B client work on this order, and report clients with good headlines, clean design and good UX whose content still barely converted.

**How to do it**

1. Before touching design or CTAs, list the keywords each converting page targets and label each one category, comparison, jobs-to-be-done, or none of those.
2. Reject any page whose keyword falls in none of those three buckets; requeue it as a keyword problem, not a CRO problem.
3. For pages that pass, read the body copy and check the product or service is discussed by name, with features, differentiators and proof.
4. Only after both checks pass, spend time on CTA placement, formatting, visuals and readability.
5. When a page gets traffic but no conversions, diagnose Step 2 first: the keyword worked, the selling did not.
6. When a page gets neither traffic nor conversions from buyers, diagnose Step 1: the keyword had no buying intent.
7. Stop funding headline, layout and visual work on pages that have not cleared Steps 1 and 2.

**Pitfall:** Teams with a polished blog assume conversion is a CRO problem and buy A/B testing and layout work. Grow and Convert's clients arrived with all of that covered and still barely converted, because the keywords carried no buying intent.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should audit Pabau's blog in this order. Take each post, name its keyword bucket, and only then look at layout. A post on generic aesthetics-industry advice does not need a better CTA, it needs replacing with a comparison, alternatives or practice-management-software page.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your blog in this order. Name each post's keyword bucket first, check the product is actually sold inside the copy second, and only then look at layout and CTAs. A post targeting a no-intent keyword does not need a better CTA.

### 21. Fix positioning before touching the product when marketing underperforms  `159.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 159*

Grow and Convert's central argument is that 'product market fit' has two halves and most teams only work the product half. When signups are low they ship an MVP, add features, then pivot the product, while the market-fit half stays as a one-line target statement like 'our new CRM targets sales managers'. They define positioning as how the offering is perceived: who customers think it is for, what they think makes you unique, which pain points they think you solve, and how credible they find you. Their rule is to exhaust positioning changes before considering a product change, because positioning pivots are far cheaper and faster than product pivots. Their own course sold 10 seats to a 6,000-person list, then 65 seats a year later with an identical product and new sales copy.

> "If your positioning is wrong, your marketing will fail"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's content marketing course: ~10 sales from a 6,000-person list (0.16%) at first launch, 65+ seats and roughly 1% a year later with the product unchanged.

**How to do it**

1. Before approving any roadmap change driven by weak sales, write down the four positioning answers: who customers think it is for, what they think is unique, which pain points they think you solve, how credible they think you are.
2. Get those answers from customers, not from the team, using survey replies and recorded calls.
3. Compare each answer to what you intended, and list every gap.
4. Rewrite the homepage hero and the main sales page to close the biggest gap first, changing nothing in the product.
5. Re-measure the same conversion metric over the same traffic source for at least one full cycle.
6. Only consider a product change once the research surfaces a burning pain point your current product genuinely cannot serve.
7. Log the cost comparison for stakeholders: copy rewrite in days versus feature build in months.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau page converts badly, David should test the positioning of the copy before commissioning new content or asking product for features. Rewriting the hero of a comparison or template page is a days-long test; a content rebuild is not.

**Apply anywhere:** When a page converts badly, test the positioning of the copy before commissioning new content or requesting product changes. A hero rewrite is a days-long test; a rebuild is not.

### 23. For a small brand, three to five SEO landing pages beat a blog  `56.19`
*core · best practices · source 56*

Both hosts land on the same conclusion: for a company trying to get signups or sales, an SEO landing page is a better piece of content than a blog post, because you start from keywords with high purchase intent and the copy can be direct - not 'what is this' but 'here is how our brand does exactly what you are looking for'. Edward's version of the argument is the long cut: he'll spend three days on one bottom-of-funnel landing page targeting a keyword with intense buying desire, the page won't even be long at 400-500 words, and if he picks the keyword well he never has to touch it again. It takes a couple of weeks to a couple of months to rank, then stays, because he chose a lower-competition keyword nobody else was targeting and gave people exactly what they want. Cody's addition is concentration: for a smaller brand it is far more effective to focus on three to five landing-page keywords and get those ranking, and to concentrate link building on them and the homepage, than to spread effort across a content programme.

> "SEO landing pages are what my method"

**How to do it**

1. Pick three to five keywords with genuine purchase intent for your product, weighted toward lower competition over volume.
2. Spend real time on the keyword choice - Edward attributes the durability of these pages to picking terms others weren't targeting.
3. Write a short page (400-500 words is enough) that states how your product does the specific thing the searcher is looking for.
4. Don't write an explainer - the searcher already knows what they want; the page's job is to match it.
5. Point your link building at those pages and the homepage rather than spreading it across the blog.
6. Expect weeks to a couple of months to rank, and then leave it alone.
7. Once a page ranks in your niche, use it as the base for expanding into adjacent keywords.

**Pitfall:** This only works if the keyword is genuinely lower competition and genuinely high intent. Applied to a competitive head term, three days on one page buys nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's highest-return SEO work may not be articles at all - a handful of tightly written landing pages for high-intent practice-management searches, with links concentrated on them, is the pattern both practitioners endorse.

**Apply anywhere:** Your highest-return SEO work may not be articles at all - a handful of tightly written landing pages for high-intent commercial searches, with links concentrated on them, is the pattern both practitioners endorse.
