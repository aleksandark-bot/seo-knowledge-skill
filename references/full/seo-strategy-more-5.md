### 1. Run six questions at any agency that claims SEO drives its traffic  `178.5`
*useful · concrete actions · source 178*

When an agency says it relies primarily on SEO to drive traffic, Grow and Convert give a fixed six-question script to test whether there is a process underneath. Ask for the keyword research process, how keywords get chosen from that research, the process for analyzing the SERP, how those SERP analyses show up in the writing, examples of articles they wrote that rank highly right now, and whether they do digital PR or build high-authority backlinks to those articles. They say a service worth hiring answers all six in detail and shows client results. The value of the script is that the questions escalate: research and selection are easy to bluff, but SERP analysis feeding specific writing decisions, plus live ranking examples, are not.

> "What's your process for analyzing search engine results pages (SERPs)?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say content or SEO services worth hiring can answer these questions in detail and show results from other clients.

**How to do it**

1. Ask for the keyword research process and the tools it runs in.
2. Ask how a keyword gets selected from the research output, and what disqualifies one.
3. Ask for the SERP analysis process for a chosen keyword.
4. Ask how that SERP analysis changes the writing, and require a concrete example of a structural decision it drove.
5. Ask for URLs of articles they wrote that rank highly now, and check the positions yourself.
6. Ask whether digital PR or link building supports those pages, and who pays for it.
7. Disqualify anyone who answers the first two well and the middle two vaguely.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Stopping at the first two questions. Keyword research process is the easiest part to describe convincingly, and it tells you nothing about whether the writing is informed by the SERP.

**Apply at Pabau:** David can run the same six questions on any freelancer or agency pitching Pabau, and should verify the ranking examples in a live SERP rather than trusting the screenshot.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask any SEO-reliant agency six questions: keyword research process, selection criteria, SERP analysis process, how that analysis changes the writing, live ranking examples, and whether link building supports them.

### 2. Scale a working channel in waves and re-check conversions each wave  `176.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 176*

Grow and Convert describe how they committed rather than jumped. They only had a few SEO-based articles published, saw good results, and stopped to really test that channel. They produced more SEO pieces, then a few more after that, and kept seeing those pieces produce large numbers of conversions before doubling down hard. The sequence matters: a small batch, a deliberate pause to check the conversion result, then a larger batch. That structure gives you the evidence to commit without betting the whole budget on a single early signal, and it is the opposite of both shiny-object hopping and blind scaling.

> "We produced more SEO pieces, then"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert ran three escalating waves of bottom-of-funnel SEO pieces before doubling down hard on the method.

**How to do it**

1. Publish an initial batch of three to five pieces in the candidate format.
2. Stop publishing and wait until they rank and have accumulated conversion data.
3. Check conversions per piece, not traffic, against the previous format.
4. If the result holds, publish a second, larger batch in the same format.
5. Re-check conversions after the second batch before increasing volume again.
6. Only after two consecutive confirming waves, move the majority of the budget onto the format.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Skipping the pause between waves. If you scale straight from the first batch you never learn whether the early result was the topic or the format.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should scale any new page route, such as a new code-reference type, in waves of three to five pages with a ranking-and-demo check between waves, rather than commissioning fifty at once.

**Apply anywhere:** Scale a promising format in waves of three to five pieces, pausing after each wave to check conversions per piece. Commit the budget only after two confirming waves.

### 3. Scale in cycles: 3 pages, rank, 6 more pages, repeat links  `42.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 42*

After the first three easy-keyword pages are ranking, the prescribed next cycle is to publish six more pages and repeat the same foundational-plus-referral link-building process again. This is explicitly framed as "a pretty obvious cycle" that repeats and compounds rather than a one-time push. The claim is that roughly one year after starting, if you've consistently focused on links that pass real referral traffic, brand mentions, and pages that satisfy search intent while maintaining a natural or varied backlink profile, the compounding effect becomes dramatic, described as being able to "literally print leads." The reasoning given is that you're now both targeting less-competitive keywords with real intent and carrying more built-up authority than competitors who only partially target those same terms.

> "you put up six more and you continue to build links again"

**How to do it**

1. After your first three easy-keyword pages reach first-page rankings, plan a second batch of six new pages targeting the next tier of easy or action-intent keywords.
2. Repeat the same link-building motion used for the first three pages, foundational links then referral-traffic links and brand-mention building, for this new batch rather than assuming existing authority alone will rank them.
3. Keep your backlink acquisition methods varied across batches, using different tactics and different link sources, specifically to maintain a natural-looking backlink profile rather than repeating one identical tactic at scale.
4. Track how many weeks or months each batch takes to rank, and use that as your internal benchmark for planning the next batch's timeline.
5. Set expectations internally around a roughly one-year horizon for the compounding effect to become clearly visible, rather than judging the strategy's success after only one batch.
6. Continue scaling the page count in each subsequent cycle beyond six once the pattern of ranking success repeats (inferred continuation of the stated 3-then-6 progression).

### 4. Scale up-funnel for volume only after high-intent keywords are exhausted  `129.17`
*useful · content insights · source 129*

Grow and Convert sequence funnel coverage rather than choosing between the levels. All their estimating examples use buying-intent keywords and their volumes, deliberately, because that is the conservative case. But they note most businesses have many lower-intent queries their audience searches that also drive conversions, just at a much lower rate. The instruction is order: tackle the high-buying-intent keywords first, then move up the funnel to keywords with much higher search volumes, which increases conversion volume beyond the conservative estimate. This is also why they say ROI grows linearly and then levels off — the flattening point is the exhaustion of high-intent opportunity in your space, and the up-funnel expansion is what continues growth past it. The sequencing matters because a program that starts up-funnel measures itself on the worst conversion rates it will ever see.

> "you can move up the funnel and take into account potential keywords"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say lower-intent queries do drive conversions but at a much lower rate, and place them after the high-buying-intent set in sequence.

**How to do it**

1. Build and rank all identified high-buying-intent keywords first, and confirm they rank before expanding.
2. Measure the conversion rate those pages actually deliver and record it as your intent benchmark.
3. Only when the high-intent list is exhausted, build the higher-volume, lower-intent list.
4. Keep the two groups tagged separately in reporting so the blended conversion rate never hides either.
5. Model up-funnel pages on volume times a much lower conversion rate, not on the high-intent rate.
6. Use the up-funnel pages to feed internal links into the high-intent money pages.
7. Stop expanding up-funnel when the added conversion volume no longer covers production cost.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Starting up-funnel because the volume looks better. The pages convert at a fraction of the high-intent rate, and reporting the blended number makes the whole channel look weak while the high-intent opportunity sits unbuilt.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should finish the high-intent set — software comparisons, templates, code references practices need to complete a task — before commissioning broader aesthetic-industry topics, and report the two groups' conversion rates separately.

**Apply anywhere:** Sequence the funnel rather than choosing a level. Build and rank every high-buying-intent keyword first, record the conversion rate they deliver, then expand into higher-volume lower-intent topics modeled on a much lower rate. Keep the two groups tagged separately so the blended number never hides either one.

### 5. Sell the strategy and prove it with case studies for service businesses  `111.11`
*useful · best practices · source 111*

Grow and Convert argue service businesses are the hardest to do content strategy for, and name three reasons. Costs are higher and more decision makers are involved, which adds friction and time. There is no trial or demo equivalent, so customers commit before seeing value, which requires a deeper level of trust built through content. And service pain points are more specific and nuanced, requiring deeper expressed expertise, which also means lower search volumes and less traffic. The consequence is a different goal. For SaaS the job is to help buyers discover a product that solves a problem they have. For a service business the job is to build trust and show you are the best resource to solve the problem before the prospect ever reaches out. Their method is to sell the strategy behind the service and use detailed case studies to prove the strategy works.

> "the goal of content marketing for a service business is to build trust"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say this is what they have historically done on their own blog, including the article itself.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the strategy your service runs, as a named, opinionated method rather than a list of deliverables.
2. Publish that strategy in full on the blog, including the parts a competitor could copy.
3. Attach a detailed case study to each claim in the strategy, with the client, the timeframe and the numbers.
4. Accept lower search volume on service keywords and judge those pages on leads, not sessions.
5. Write at the expertise level of a buyer who already knows the basics of the category.
6. Name the decision makers involved in the purchase and address each one's objection somewhere in the content.
7. Replace the demo, which you do not have, with a case study showing the outcome for a company like theirs.
8. Refresh the case studies as new results land so the proof stays current.

**Pitfall:** Publishing the strategy without proof. Without case studies naming clients, timeframes and figures, the strategy reads as another opinion post and builds no trust.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sells software, so the SaaS route applies to most pages. But for the consultancy-flavored content aimed at multi-location groups, David should pair each recommended workflow with a named practice case study rather than relying on the demo to carry the trust.

**Apply anywhere:** If you sell a service, publish the method behind it in full and prove every claim with a detailed case study naming the client, timeframe and numbers. The case study replaces the demo you cannot offer.

### 6. Sequence the hybrid as rank first, then convert, on a weak domain  `146.10`
*useful · best practices · source 146*

Grow and Convert point out that ServiceTitan's version of the hybrid started from an advantage: the comparison landing pages already existed and already ranked, so adding editorial content pushed them higher. Sites without that starting position have two other routes. The first, and the one they recommend considering, is to create the blog post first, because that has the highest likelihood of ranking, and then build a landing page template around it once the ranking is achieved. The second is to build the hybrid from the start. They treat both as untested enough to warrant explicit testing rather than a rule, and say it is worth testing either way based on what they are seeing.

> "you could create the blog post first to have the highest likelihood of ranking"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert note ServiceTitan's gain came from pre-existing, already ranking comparison landing pages, and offer post-first as the alternative for sites without one.

**How to do it**

1. Check whether you already have a page ranking on page one for the target comparison or category term.
2. If you do, add the editorial content into that existing page and keep the URL.
3. If you do not, publish the plain blog post first and leave it alone until it holds a stable top-three or top-five position.
4. Only then add the landing page hero, CTA and comparison table around the existing body content.
5. Change one thing at a time and leave at least four weeks between the ranking change and the conversion change, so you can attribute each.
6. Log the test as a hypothesis with a before and after position and conversion rate, since the sequencing is unproven.

**Pitfall:** Adding landing-page furniture to a post that has not settled its ranking. Any position movement then confounds with the normal fluctuation of a new page and you learn nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, most competitor terms have no ranking product page, so David should publish the article first and only add the landing page frame once it holds a position.

**Apply anywhere:** If you have no ranking page on the term, publish the article first and only add the landing page frame once it holds a position.

### 7. Sequence top-funnel content after the high-intent list is exhausted  `108.19`
*useful · best practices · source 108*

Grow and Convert do not dismiss top-of-funnel keywords, they sequence them last and give them a different job. They say those terms can be valuable for building topical authority with search engines, for overall traffic, and for creating internal linking opportunities into the core pages that target bottom-funnel keywords. What they refuse is starting there, because conversion rates are so much lower and, in an environment where AI answers informational queries directly, the traffic payoff is shrinking too. Their rule is that once the high-intent opportunities are exhausted, top-of-funnel content makes sense as a way to strengthen the overall SEO foundation. That reframes the success measure. A top-funnel post published under this rule should be judged on the rankings it helps its internal link targets reach, not on its own conversions.

> "Once you've exhausted the high-intent opportunities"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated ordering: high-intent first, top-of-funnel afterwards for topical authority, traffic and internal linking into bottom-funnel pages.

**How to do it**

1. Publish and rank the category, comparison and jobs-to-be-done keywords first.
2. Declare the high-intent tier exhausted only when every term on the sheet has a live page.
3. Then build a top-funnel list, choosing terms topically adjacent to your converting pages.
4. Require each top-funnel post to link to at least two bottom-funnel pages with descriptive anchors.
5. Cap in-body links so the authority is concentrated rather than spread.
6. Set the success metric for these posts as position change on the linked pages, not their own conversions.
7. Review after six months and retire top-funnel posts that neither rank nor support a converting page.
8. Do not let the top-funnel tier grow faster than the high-intent tier is refreshed.

**Pitfall:** Starting with top-funnel because the volume looks better. You end up with a large blog, a low conversion rate and no page ranking for anything a buyer searches.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's informational aesthetics articles should be commissioned after the software-category, alternatives and workflow pages exist, and each should link into those pages with descriptive anchors rather than standing alone.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish the buying-intent keywords first. Add top-funnel content only once that list is exhausted, give each post internal links into your converting pages, and judge it on those pages' rankings rather than its own conversions.

### 8. Set effort and link budget from who ranks, not from a difficulty score  `108.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 108*

Grow and Convert use the identity of the ranking domains as their competitiveness read. If the top results are dominated by high-authority sites like G2, Capterra and major industry publications, expect to invest more heavily in content quality, promotion and link building for a shot at page one. If smaller blogs and direct competitors hold the SERP, the barrier is lower and content quality alone may carry it. They add two checks most people skip. If a direct competitor ranks, study what they do well and where they fall short, then beat that specific page rather than a generic quality bar. If no direct competitor ranks at all, treat it as a chance to hold the SERP before they arrive. This is a resourcing decision made before the brief, not a rank-difficulty number pulled from a tool.

> "If the top results are dominated by high-authority sites"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name G2, Capterra and major industry publications as the signal that heavier content, promotion and link investment are required.

**How to do it**

1. List the ten ranking domains for the target keyword.
2. Count how many are review platforms or major publications versus small blogs and competitors.
3. Tag the keyword high-effort when review sites and publications hold five or more slots.
4. For high-effort keywords, budget deeper content plus a planned link campaign before publishing.
5. For low-effort keywords, publish first and only build links if the page stalls.
6. Where a direct competitor ranks, read their page and list what it covers well and what it omits.
7. Write the brief to beat that specific page on the omissions you found.
8. Where no direct competitor ranks, prioritize the term to claim the SERP before they do.
9. Revisit the domain mix after six months, since review sites often move in later.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** Treating every keyword as equal effort. Publishing an average-depth article into a G2-and-Capterra SERP with no link plan wastes the article and the writer's time.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, aesthetics-software SERPs usually contain Capterra, G2 and Software Advice. Those keywords need a link plan attached to the brief. Practice-workflow and template terms are mostly small blogs, so those can ship on content quality alone.

**Apply anywhere:** Read the ranking domains to set effort. Review platforms and major publications mean deeper content plus a link campaign; small blogs and competitors mean content quality alone may be enough.

### 9. Shift budget back toward brand as LLM understanding replaces familiarity  `76.15`
*useful · general insights · source 76*

The paper applies Binet and Field's finding that the optimal marketing budget split is 60 percent brand and 40 percent performance, where brand covers awareness, positioning and long-term equity and performance covers direct response aimed at immediate conversion. In practice, it argues, companies over-invest in short-term campaigns and push performance above 40 percent, sacrificing long-term brand development. The result is being top of mind for consumers at the cost of being defined as the authoritative company in the space. Under AEO the driving factor for selection moves from user familiarity with brand names to LLM brand understanding, because users increasingly search for solutions rather than specific brands and rely on the model to recommend. The authors add a limit: this applies mainly to commoditized and substitutable goods. Luxury and specialized goods, where consumers search by name, may still get direct demand that bypasses LLM-mediated discovery, at least for now.

> "the driving factor for selection evolves from user familiarity with brand names to LLM brand understanding"

**Evidence:** Binet and Field's 60% brand / 40% performance split, with the paper noting firms in practice push performance above 40%.

**Pitfall:** Reading this as a licence to cut performance spend immediately. The paper's caveat is that name-searched luxury and specialized goods still get direct demand, so the reallocation is category-dependent.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's category is substitutable software chosen after research, which is exactly where LLM understanding replaces name familiarity. That argues for funding entity work, earned media and original research over more direct-response spend.

**Apply anywhere:** If your category is substitutable and chosen after research, LLM understanding replaces name familiarity. Fund entity work, earned media and original research over more direct-response spend.

### 10. Shift your planning question from can I build this to what should I build  `88.5`
*useful · best practices · source 88*

The author argues the binding constraint has moved. Teams used to have more ideas than people or time, so ideas died in a Google Doc because there was no designer or developer to build them. With AI, execution capacity is no longer the limit; the limit is the quality of the ideas. He warns that if all you bring is execution speed, writing fast, designing fast, shipping fast, AI is a problem for you because it does that too. The value now sits in original thinking, a deep understanding of the customer and a clear point of view. The operational consequence is that planning meetings should stop filtering ideas on feasibility and start filtering on interestingness, then resurrect the ideas that were previously killed on cost.

> "If all you bring to the table is execution speed"

**How to do it**

1. Open the old ideas backlog and pull every item that was rejected for lack of design or developer time.
2. Re-score each on interestingness and customer relevance only, ignoring build cost.
3. Pick the top three and prototype each with an AI coding tool in a single day rather than scoping them.
4. In future planning sessions, ban feasibility as a first-round filter; judge ideas on whether anyone would share them.
5. Reassign the time freed from execution into customer research and point-of-view development.
6. Track which shipped ideas earned links or mentions, and feed that back into what counts as interesting.
7. Review the backlog quarterly, since what is buildable keeps expanding.

**Pitfall:** Teams keep applying the old feasibility filter out of habit and quietly kill the ideas that would now be cheap. The signal is a roadmap that looks the same as last year's despite new tooling.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has a standing requirement for an original visual per article and can now build interactive tools cheaply. Revisit any calculator, comparison tool or interactive block idea previously shelved for developer time, since one genuinely useful tool out-earns most link budgets.

**Apply anywhere:** Reopen the ideas you killed for lack of design or dev resource and re-score them on interest alone. Execution speed is no longer a differentiator; the quality of the idea is.

### 11. Size the keyword inventory to justify the pre-breakeven trough  `150.13`
*useful · general insights · source 150*

Grow and Convert argue the answer to 'how much business can we generate from this channel' is not a forecast, it is an inventory count of keywords in your space that carry business value. Their case for content rests on the fact that SEO, unlike paid, does not need continued spend to keep producing traffic and leads: once a page ranks high enough, usually the top half of page one, it keeps generating leads for as long as it ranks, and returns compound as first-page rankings accumulate. So if there are enough commercially valuable keywords left to attack, content produces strong long-term returns. Showing this to the person asking about ROI is what buys tolerance for the initial trough, the months where spend exceeds return. Without that framing, the trough reads as failure rather than as the expected shape.

> "the initial trough where you're spending more money"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use the size of the business-value keyword inventory as their answer to how much business content can generate, and the compounding of first-page rankings as the argument for tolerating the trough.

**How to do it**

1. Build a list of every keyword in your space with commercial intent, not the whole keyword universe.
2. Filter to ones where a page of yours could plausibly reach the top half of page one.
3. Multiply the count by your average conversions per ranking page to get the channel's ceiling.
4. Present that ceiling alongside the breakeven lead target so upside and cost sit together.
5. Explain that ranked pages keep producing without further spend, unlike paid channels.
6. Show the expected trough on the chart in advance, with the month you expect it to end.
7. Revisit the inventory annually; when it runs thin, say so rather than publishing filler.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** If the commercially valuable keyword inventory is small, content cannot reach breakeven no matter how well it is executed, and the honest answer is to say so before the retainer starts.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's commercial keyword inventory in aesthetic and healthcare practice management is finite, so David should count it once, use it as the channel ceiling in reporting, and flag when the remaining terms stop justifying new production.

**Apply anywhere:** Answer 'how much can this channel produce' by counting the commercially valuable keywords you could realistically rank for, not by forecasting traffic. Present that ceiling with the breakeven target and show the pre-breakeven trough in advance so it reads as expected.

### 12. Slide the volume requirement against deal size and retention  `129.18`
*useful · content insights · source 129*

Grow and Convert make the search-volume bar explicitly relative. What counts as decent volume depends on the size of the target audience and the price of the product, and the two move together. A high-priced B2B SaaS whose customers spend thousands of dollars a month and stay an average of eighteen months will obviously have far fewer searchers than a consumer product like a pre-workout drink mix. Their point is that this is fine, because that business may only need five or ten customers a month to break even on monthly spend. The corollary is the harder case: a low-priced B2C or ecommerce product needs far more traffic and conversions to recoup the same SEO cost, which is why they demand many three- and four-figure keywords there. The bar is set by required customers per month, not by an absolute volume number.

> "you may only need 5 or 10 customers per month to break even"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's example: a B2B SaaS at thousands per month with 18-month average retention may need only 5 to 10 customers a month to break even, so low keyword volume is acceptable.

**How to do it**

1. Calculate average revenue per customer over the retention period, not per month.
2. Divide your monthly SEO cost by that figure to get required new customers per month.
3. Convert that to required leads using your lead-to-close rate.
4. Convert required leads to required traffic using a 1% conversion rate for high-intent pages.
5. Compare required traffic with the summed volume of your keyword list to see whether the space can supply it.
6. Set your per-keyword volume floor from that comparison rather than from a generic benchmark.
7. Recalculate the whole chain whenever pricing, retention or SEO spend changes.

**Tools:** Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Applying a single volume standard across a portfolio with different price points. A low-ACV product judged by a high-ACV business's thin-volume tolerance will never recoup its content cost.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's subscription value and practice retention put it firmly in the low-volume-tolerant band. Derive the required demo requests per month from subscription value times retention, and use that to justify building pages for genuinely small-volume clinic queries.

**Apply anywhere:** Set your volume bar from required customers per month, not an absolute number. Divide monthly SEO cost by revenue per customer across the retention period, convert to leads and then to traffic at 1%, and compare that with the summed volume of your keyword list. High price and long retention justify thin volume.

### 13. Split content strategy by revenue line and run a prompt set for each  `81.12`
*useful · best practices · source 81*

Grow and Convert organize Constitution Lending's content around the four distinct parts of the business rather than around one topical hub: lending to investors buying property, investing where people buy shares in loans, cash for houses where the company buys homes in 24 to 48 hours, and a fund investing in non-performing loans. Each line has a different buyer asking different questions, so each gets its own bottom-of-funnel keyword list and its own tracked prompt set. The reporting follows the same split, which is what let them see that the newer lines were behind rather than averaging everything into one visibility score. Notably the fund line, competing with Blackstone and Vanguard, still produced AI visibility for prompts like buying non-performing loans faster than they expected.

> "our content strategy focuses on four core areas"

**Evidence:** Constitution Lending's four lines tracked separately showed strong lending and investor visibility, early traction on cash-for-houses and non-performing loans within months of publishing.

**How to do it**

1. Write down each distinct revenue line and the buyer persona attached to it.
2. Build a separate bottom-of-funnel keyword list per line rather than one site-wide list.
3. Give each line its own site section so internal links and topical signals stay separated.
4. Create a distinct prompt basket per line in your AI tracker.
5. Report visibility per line, never as one blended site number.
6. Sequence publishing so the highest-margin line is fully covered before the next one starts.
7. Expect newer lines to lag and set the review point several months out rather than weeks.
8. Reallocate effort to the line with the widest gap between business value and current visibility.

**Tools:** Traqer

**Pitfall:** A single blended AI visibility score hides a failing business line behind a strong one, and you keep investing where you already win.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should split its keyword and prompt tracking by what it actually sells to whom: clinic software buyers, template and form seekers, and code-reference lookups are different demand pools and should not share one visibility number.

**Apply anywhere:** Give each revenue line its own keyword list, its own site section and its own tracked prompt set. Blended visibility reporting hides the line that is failing behind the line that is working.

### 14. Spread the first content batch across difficulty levels to prove the process early  `133.8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 133*

On a low domain rating site Grow and Convert changed one thing in their normal process: they picked pain-point topics with varying levels of competitiveness on purpose, to see whether the less competitive terms would rank much faster, and they accepted much lower estimated monthly search volume on those terms to get the test. The point is not that easy keywords are better. It is that a batch of uniformly competitive targets gives you no information for six months, whereas a spread tells you within weeks whether the site can rank at all. If the easy terms move and the hard ones do not, the site is fine and you are simply outgunned on authority. If nothing moves, including the easy terms, something structural is broken, which is exactly the conclusion the Circuit rank tracker forced.

> "varying levels of competitiveness to try to see if we could rank"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert deliberately varied competitiveness across six starting topics for Circuit, DR 18, accepting much lower estimated volume on the less competitive terms.

**How to do it**

1. Build the bottom-of-funnel topic list first, without filtering on difficulty or volume.
2. Score each candidate for keyword difficulty in Ahrefs and split the list into low, medium and high bands.
3. Choose the first batch of roughly six articles with at least two from each band.
4. Accept low estimated volume on the low-difficulty picks; they are diagnostic as well as commercial.
5. Publish the batch and track every target keyword weekly.
6. At the eight-week mark, read the pattern: low-difficulty terms moving means the site works and you need authority for the rest.
7. Nothing moving, including the low-difficulty terms, means stop publishing and audit the site structure and indexation.
8. Rebalance the next batch toward the band that actually moved.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Filling the first batch entirely with easy low-volume terms. You get rankings and no revenue, and you never learn whether the site can compete where the money is.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build each new cluster's first batch with a deliberate mix of difficulty, for example one head term like practice management software alongside narrow qualified terms, so the cluster's viability is readable in two months rather than two quarters.

**Apply anywhere:** Make your first content batch on a new site or new cluster a deliberate mix of low, medium and high difficulty targets. The easy ones are a diagnostic: if they do not move either, the problem is structural, not competitive.

### 15. Stop treating the blog as a funnel feeder rather than a selling asset  `143.8`
*useful · content insights · source 143*

Grow and Convert name the underlying belief that produces low blog conversion rates. Most businesses, in-house marketers, content agencies and SEO agencies view the blog strictly as a channel for getting people into a conversion funnel, not as an asset for directly selling products and services. That framing produces the keyword choices, high volume and low intent, and the writing rule that follows from it, do not be salesy, give advice and hope they remember you later. Grow and Convert call that last hope not likely. They present the two failures as one: the keyword choice and the no-selling rule reinforce each other, so fixing either alone leaves the other in place. The correction is to treat each post as a page whose job is a sale, which changes both what you target and how you write.

> "not as an asset for directly selling products and services"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert draw the pattern from working with dozens of clients and measuring conversions on hundreds of their posts.

**Pitfall:** Teams adopt bottom-funnel keywords but keep the no-selling writing rule, then report that bottom-funnel content did not work for them. The two changes only pay off together.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content brief should state the selling job for each article, not only the keyword and outline. A brief that specifies a buying-intent term but asks for neutral educational copy reproduces the failure Grow and Convert describe.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the selling job into the content brief alongside the keyword. Adopting buying-intent keywords while keeping a no-selling writing rule reproduces the same weak conversion rates.

### 16. Target keywords big brands own instead of conceding them  `106.13`
*useful · best practices · source 106*

Grow and Convert's fourth named mistake is the client objection that a big brand ranks for a term so there is no point competing. Their answer is that it takes longer, not that it is impossible, and it rests on two moves: be more selective about which keywords you target, and build each page specifically for the search intent of its keyword rather than sprinkling the term into an existing page. Their client Cognitive FX sells post-concussion treatment and competes against WebMD, Healthline, the CDC, Mayo Clinic, Very Well Health and the NIH. As of 2023 they ranked first for over 60 valuable keywords against those sites, and content drives about half of all their leads. Their sequencing advice is to start these keywords immediately because they are slow, and pad with easier high-intent terms for near-term results.

> "we're ranking #1 for over 60 valuable keywords"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX ranked #1 for over 60 keywords in 2023 against Mayo Clinic, the CDC, the NIH and Very Well Health, with content driving roughly 50% of all leads.

**How to do it**

1. List the buying-intent keywords you have been avoiding because a large brand ranks for them.
2. Start those pages first, since they take the longest to rank.
3. Build one page per keyword, written entirely to that query's intent, rather than adding a section to an existing page.
4. Read the big brand's ranking page and find what its scale prevents it doing: specificity, first-hand practitioner detail, current data.
5. Write to that gap rather than matching their breadth.
6. Add several lower-competition high buying-intent keywords to the same batch to produce results while the hard pages mature.
7. Review the hard pages at nine to twelve months, not at three.

**Pitfall:** Sprinkling the target keyword through existing pages instead of building a dedicated one. Grow and Convert say you have a very slim chance of ranking unless the entire page matches the intent of the query.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not concede aesthetics and healthcare software terms to large incumbents. Build one dedicated page per query, written from practitioner detail Pabau has and generic sites do not, and start those pages before the easy ones.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not skip a valuable keyword because a major brand ranks for it. Start those pages first because they take longest, build one dedicated page per query rather than editing an existing one, and run easier high-intent terms alongside for near-term results.

### 17. Test disruption stories and narrower category keywords as the next step  `80.11`

Grow and Convert close by naming what they are trying next, which is useful as a roadmap of where a team that already does bottom-funnel content goes. ### 18. Test real penalty thresholds yourself with disposable micro-sites  `26.4`
*useful · best practices · source 26*

Rather than trusting SEO folklore about what triggers Google penalties, Dooley's agency runs a standing experiment: every new apprentice's first assignment is to deliberately try to get a disposable test site hit with a manual action (via scaled content abuse and mass low-quality backlinks) within their first three months. Across 47 apprentices who tried this, only one ever triggered a manual action, and the resulting aggressive test sites went on to outperform over 70% of a comparison set of sites in the same period — showing the real boundary before penalty is much further out than most SEOs assume. The exercise is deliberately run on sites the company 'doesn't care about' so the learning has no downside, and it's re-run per industry/geo since thresholds don't transfer.

> "these beat over 70% of the sites in that time period"

**How to do it**

1. Pick a niche/geo you have no revenue dependency on for the test.
2. Register a disposable domain and scaffold a basic site quickly (Dooley notes Claude Code can build a simple site in under a minute, hosted free on Cloudflare Pages).
3. Assign one team member to push aggressive tactics on it: scaled/mass low-quality content and mass low-quality backlink acquisition, with the explicit goal of triggering a Google manual action.
4. In parallel, build a second, conservative 'by the book' control site in the same niche for comparison.
5. Add both sites to Google Search Console and check the Manual Actions report (Security & Manual Actions) weekly.
6. Track rankings and traffic for both sites over the same multi-month window.
7. Record whether and when a manual action notice appears for the aggressive site, and compare its performance against the conservative control and a broader competitor set.
8. Use the outcome to calibrate your own real risk tolerance instead of secondhand 'Google parrot' claims, and re-run the test in any new industry or country before assuming the same boundary applies.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Claude Code, Cloudflare Pages

**Pitfall:** Don't take secondhand SEO-community warnings at face value — Dooley's own data (1 manual action out of 47 deliberate attempts, with the aggressive sites beating 70% of the comparison set) shows the real risk threshold is far higher than commonly assumed, but the exact threshold is industry- and geography-specific and must be re-tested rather than generalized.

### 19. Test replacement distribution channels for a month when your main one decays  `128.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 128*

When Grow and Convert saw community promotion stagnating in December 2018, they did not iterate on it. Devesh says he realized the agency was at risk if they did not find other distribution tactics, and spent his days and nights that December testing other channels. He names what he tested: Quora ads, purchasing placements in email newsletters, and distribution platforms like Outbrain. After a month of testing he published the path forward, and it was a specific portfolio rather than a single replacement: paid Facebook distribution as the short-term traffic tactic, SEO and link building as the long-term tactic, and community promotion demoted to a minor part of the service. The transferable procedure is the shape: recognize decay, run a time-boxed channel test across several named options at once, and commit to a portfolio split by time horizon.

> "I explored various options like Quora ads, purchasing email newsletters"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert spent December 2018 testing Quora ads, paid email newsletters and Outbrain, then committed to paid Facebook plus SEO and link building with community promotion demoted.

**How to do it**

1. Name the decay signal explicitly, such as flat traffic per published piece despite the same promotion effort.
2. Time-box a channel test to about a month rather than running it open-ended alongside business as usual.
3. Shortlist four or five specific named channels to test, such as paid social, Quora ads, paid newsletter placements and content discovery platforms.
4. Give each channel a small equal budget and the same articles so results are comparable.
5. Score each on cost per landing page view, whether the traffic converts, and whether it scales without manual labor.
6. Pick a portfolio, not a winner: one channel for short-term traffic and one for compounding long-term traffic.
7. Demote the decaying channel rather than dropping it, keeping it for the cases where it still works.
8. Publish or document the decision so the team stops relitigating it.

**Tools:** Facebook, Quora, Outbrain

**Pitfall:** Testing one replacement channel at a time while the old one keeps sliding. By the time you have a verdict the traffic base has fallen, and the portfolio split never gets designed.

**Apply at Pabau:** If a Pabau acquisition channel flattens, David should run a one-month parallel test of three or four named alternatives on the same pages, then commit to one short-term and one compounding channel rather than a single replacement.

**Apply anywhere:** When your main distribution channel decays, time-box a one-month parallel test of four or five named alternatives using the same articles and equal budgets. Score each on cost per landing page view, conversion and scalability, then commit to a portfolio with one short-term channel and one compounding one.

### 20. Test the recover-ad-spend model against two questions before copying it  `155.14`
*useful · best practices · source 155*

Grow and Convert refuse to present the case study as plug and play, and give a two-part framework for deciding whether it transfers. First, importance of lead collection: Erika's business builds trust with a lot of content before an occasional product pitch, so collecting the email is central. > "(1) Importance of Lead Collection"

**Evidence:** Erika Volk Fitness is bootstrapped, which is why covering $492 of spend in near real time mattered even though the sum is small.

### 21. The 2012 update killed thin EMD content, not exact-match domains  `67.20`
*useful · content insights · source 67*

Dirk corrects the standard framing that exact-match domains died in 2012. ### 24. Treat a competitor keyword list as a herd signal, not a plan  `118.11`
*useful · general insights · source 118*

Grow and Convert open with a warning about the tactic they are teaching. Competitor keyword research is near-universal advice, and the result is a herd: everyone in a space researches everyone else, copies what they find, and the whole category converges on the highest-volume terms available. On the surface that looks fine, since more volume means more traffic. Their argument is that the convergence is itself the evidence of a mistake, because the terms everyone copied were selected for volume rather than for buying intent. Follow a competitor's list blindly and you land where they are: a large archive that gets traffic and does not produce leads, demo requests or form fills. The useful reading of a competitor list is therefore inverted. The keywords nobody in the herd has picked up are worth more than the ones everyone ranks for.

> "most companies follow a herd mentality where they all research"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert argue this across multiple published case studies, with Geekbot as the worked example where top-of-funnel traffic converted at 0.19%.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush

**Pitfall:** A competitor's traffic figure is taken as proof their keyword choices work. Traffic is the only number visible from outside; their conversion rate is not, and it is the one that decides whether the list is worth copying.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not benchmark its keyword plan against what other practice management vendors rank for. The rows worth most are the buying-intent terms none of them have built a page for.

**Apply anywhere:** Read a competitor keyword list as evidence of what the whole category copied from each other, and pay most attention to the buying-intent rows nobody has taken.

### 25. Treat a downturn as the window to build content authority cheaply  `168.13`
*useful · general insights · source 168*

CPC Strategy launched in 2007 and grew 50% year over year through the heart of the Great Recession. Nii Ahene attributes the survival to a lean approach: the founders managed client campaigns themselves and did not hire their first employee for a few years. The growth, separately, came from content. The combination is the point. In a downturn, paid acquisition budgets compress and competitors cut content programs, which lowers the cost of ranking and of earning industry attention at exactly the moment cash is tightest. Founder-produced news and data cost time rather than money. Grow and Convert published this piece as another downturn approached, framing the same play as repeatable rather than a one-off of 2008.

> "a lean approach protected it from the crash of the market"

**Evidence:** CPC Strategy grew 50% year over year during the Great Recession while keeping expenses low and running content in-house, and reached 135 staff by 2018.

**How to do it**

1. During a downturn, audit which competitors have paused publishing or laid off content staff, using their blog post dates.
2. Identify the keywords and beats those competitors held and check whether their pages are now stale or unmaintained.
3. Shift budget from paid acquisition, which competes on price, into content, which competes on effort.
4. Keep production founder-led or in-house so the program costs time rather than cash while revenue is uncertain.
5. Prioritize news and proprietary data over evergreen explainers, since those build the authority position competitors are vacating.
6. Hold the cadence through the whole downturn rather than pausing, because the advantage comes from continuity.
7. Expect the position to be defensible after recovery, since competitors restarting from zero face your accumulated authority.

**Pitfall:** Cutting content first is the default reflex in a downturn, and it hands the beat to whoever kept publishing. The cost shows up two years later as a competitor who owns the SERPs you used to hold.

**Apply at Pabau:** If aesthetic practice software marketing budgets tighten, David should protect the publishing cadence and cut paid spend instead. The competitors who pause will leave treatment and operations keywords unmaintained, and stale pages are the cheapest ones to outrank.

**Apply anywhere:** In a downturn, check which competitors have stopped publishing, move budget from paid into in-house content, prioritize news and proprietary data, and hold the cadence throughout rather than pausing.
