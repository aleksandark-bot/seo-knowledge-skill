### 1. Enter a content channel where nobody covers the process end to end  `169.16`
*useful · concrete actions · source 169*

Before starting the agency, Hyam ran marketing at ThinkApps as the first marketing hire and fourth employee, testing acquisition channels for an app building service. Paid ads produced a fairly high CAC. Educational events in San Francisco drew people with no money for services. He then researched content in the app building space and found the specific opening: no one was writing helpful content on how to build mobile and web apps from start to finish. That is a process gap rather than a keyword gap. Everyone covers pieces of a workflow; nobody documents the whole thing for someone actually doing it. He built an in-house team of freelance writers plus an editor, kept strategy, promotion and conversion himself, and in a little over six months grew the blog past 35,000 monthly unique visitors and made it the company's number one acquisition source.

> "no one was writing helpful content on how to build mobile and web apps from start to finish"

**Evidence:** At ThinkApps, Hyam grew the blog to over 35,000 monthly unique visitors in a little over six months and made it the company's number one acquisition source.

**How to do it**

1. Test the cheaper channels first and record why each fails, as Hyam did with paid CAC and events attracting non-buyers.
2. Write out the full process your customer goes through to get the outcome your product supports, start to finish.
3. Search each stage of that process and note which stages have good content and which have none.
4. Look specifically for the absence of any end-to-end guide covering the whole workflow.
5. Confirm the gap is a demand gap and not a no-audience gap by checking whether buyers ask about it on calls.
6. Build the end-to-end piece first and hang stage-level articles off it.
7. Keep strategy, promotion and conversion in-house and outsource only drafting, which is the split Hyam used.
8. Track the channel against acquisition source, not traffic, and give it six months before judging.

**Pitfall:** Mistaking a topic nobody writes about for an opportunity when it is actually a topic nobody searches. Hyam validated the gap against a service he was already selling to a known audience.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog should own the end-to-end process guides for running an aesthetics practice, opening, staffing, compliance, booking, follow-up, rather than only single-stage articles, and link the stage articles into that spine.

**Apply anywhere:** Map the full process your customer goes through, search every stage, and look for the missing end-to-end guide. Build that piece first, hang stage-level articles off it, and judge the channel on acquisition source after six months.

### 2. Escalate to depth, links or media when an update changes nothing  `119.10`
*useful · best practices · source 119*

Grow and Convert are explicit that not all updates make a difference and that rankings are not guaranteed to move just because you edited a page. They name the escalation paths when a refresh fails: the content may need more comprehensive detail, better-quality links, or more relevant media to reclaim its position. The fourth possibility they raise is that the keyword is simply hard and you are not pushing hard enough on promotion. They make the same point earlier about a stalled page: if a post climbed page one and then would not push past position five, updating is one option, but link building may be what is actually required. The value here is having a decision rule instead of rewriting the same page repeatedly.

> "not all updates make a difference"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite a client post that climbed page one then would not push past position #5, and name link building as the alternative lever.

**How to do it**

1. At the one-month review, compare the settled position against the pre-update position.
2. If it has not improved, stop editing the copy and diagnose instead.
3. Compare your page's depth against the top three results and note every subtopic they cover that you do not.
4. Pull referring domain counts for the top three and for your page in Ahrefs.
5. If their link profiles are clearly stronger, move the page into the link building queue rather than the rewrite queue.
6. If depth is the gap, commission the missing sections rather than reworking existing ones.
7. Check whether the ranking pages carry video, original charts or product screenshots you lack, and add relevant media.
8. If the keyword is far above your site's usual difficulty range, accept it needs promotion and budget for it.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Treating every stall as a content problem. A page frozen at position five behind stronger link profiles will not move no matter how many times the copy is rewritten.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should cap refresh attempts at one per page per quarter. When an article does not respond, David should route it to digital PR or internal link support instead of a second rewrite.

**Apply anywhere:** If a refresh does not move a page in a month, stop rewriting. Diagnose whether the gap is depth, links, media or promotion, and act on that instead.

### 3. Expect a conversion-led rebuild to move signups far more than traffic  `139.17`
*useful · general insights · source 139*

The Keeper Tax numbers are worth holding as a benchmark for what a conversion-focused content rebuild produces. Over 3.5 months Brandon grew the site from around 10,000 unique visitors a month by 400% through content and SEO. Ahrefs showed 148 keywords at position one and 996 in the top 10. Signups from content went from barely any conversions in May to over 700% growth a month by September and were still rising afterwards. Before the work, most of the company's signups came from Google, Facebook and Instagram ads, so the organic leads were genuinely incremental rather than substituting for paid. The shape of the result matters: signups grew faster than traffic did, because the keyword selection changed rather than only the volume of publishing.

> "in 3.5 months, I've been able to grow Keeper Tax's site"

**Evidence:** Keeper Tax over 3.5 months: 400% traffic growth from ~10,000 monthly uniques, #1 for 148 keywords, top 10 for 996, and over 700% monthly signup growth from content between May and September.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Reading the 400% traffic figure as the headline misses the point. The traffic came partly from volume keywords the client demanded; the signup growth came from the intent-led selection.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David reports on Pabau's content program, lead with demo requests from organic rather than sessions, and set the internal expectation that a keyword-selection change moves signups before it moves traffic.

**Apply anywhere:** Expect a conversion-led keyword rebuild to move signups faster than sessions, and report the signup line first so the change is not judged on traffic alone.

### 4. Expect a forty-minute edit to be the difference on a bottom-funnel post  `71.25`
*useful · content insights · source 71*

The whole session ran about forty minutes from brief to publishable draft, with roughly ten of those on the intro. Devesh's repeated claim through it is that the generated text was already better than generic AI output, and that the remaining gap was human-ness no AI tool will add. His examples of that gap are specific rather than stylistic: the not-X-but-Y cut, the bolder claims with mechanisms attached, the removed repetitions, the company-history paragraph, and the reader-facing reframe. He also warns the gap cannot be closed by intent alone. You cannot decide to add human-ness and then add a bunch of nonsense; you have to be good at it. The realistic reading is that AI closes most of the distance and a skilled forty-minute pass closes the rest.

> "this took maybe 40 minutes"

**Evidence:** Devesh produced a publishable draft for 'AI content brief generator' in about 40 minutes total, roughly 10 of which were the intro.

**How to do it**

1. Budget roughly 40 minutes of human editing per AI-drafted bottom-funnel post, with about 10 on the intro.
2. Spend that time on five specific things: repetition, AI constructions, hedged claims, self-referential research narration, and section order.
3. Do not spend it on rephrasing sentences that already read acceptably.
4. Have someone who knows the subject do the pass, since the edits require judgment about what claim is defensible.
5. Measure the output by whether a reader could disagree with the claims, not by whether the prose sounds human.
6. Track how much of the 40 minutes goes to each of the five categories, and fix the tool's training where one category dominates.

**Tools:** Wave Writer, Google Docs

**Pitfall:** Handing the edit to someone without domain judgment produces cosmetic changes and leaves the repetition and hedging intact, which is the part that actually separates the draft from generic output.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can plan around roughly an hour of human editing per AI-drafted article, but only with an editor who knows aesthetic and healthcare practice workflows well enough to judge which claims are defensible.

**Apply anywhere:** Plan around 40 minutes of human editing per AI-drafted post, aimed at repetition, AI constructions, hedging, self-referential narration and section order, and give it to someone with real domain judgment.

### 5. Expect a top-funnel piece to need chance to convert anyone  `110.12`
*useful · content insights · source 110*

Grow and Convert describe the failure mechanism of off-product topics precisely. If someone reads an article on sales presentations on a CRM company's blog, nothing in that topic suggests they need a CRM. Converting them requires a massive leap: the reader has to decide mid-article that they are in the market for new CRM software. That means you are relying on chance, hoping that some fraction of people reading a sales article also happen to need a CRM right now. The topic is doing nothing to find or filter people who do. This reframes top-funnel content as a probability bet rather than a marketing asset, and explains why the conversion rate gap between funnel stages is so large rather than merely noticeable.

> "You're relying on chance"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's CRM example: a reader of a sales presentation article must independently decide they are in the market for a new CRM for that article to convert.

**How to do it**

1. For each planned article, write the sentence the reader would have to think to convert.
2. If that sentence requires the reader to change what they came for, mark the piece as chance-dependent.
3. Estimate what share of that topic's audience plausibly needs your product right now.
4. Compare that share to a product-intent topic where the share is near total.
5. Reorder the calendar by that share, highest first.
6. Only keep chance-dependent pieces if the product-intent list is genuinely finished.

**Pitfall:** Justifying an off-product topic by audience overlap. Overlap with your industry is not overlap with buying intent, and the tell is a healthy traffic number with almost no signups.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles on general aesthetics topics reach practice staff but do not filter for anyone shopping for software. Before commissioning one, write the leap the reader would have to make and compare it against a comparison or alternatives page where no leap is needed.

**Apply anywhere:** For every planned article, write the exact thought a reader must have to convert. If that thought requires them to abandon what they came for, the piece converts by chance and belongs behind your product-intent topics.

### 6. Expect comparison keywords to be harder now than when they were cheap  `90.8`
*useful · content insights · source 90*

Grow and Convert flag a change in their own advice. When they first started targeting comparison and alternatives keywords years ago, those terms were not popular in the SEO community and were easy to rank for. They have since grown in popularity, so some of these keywords now carry real competition. Their position is unchanged in direction but softened on difficulty: comparison content is still, in their opinion, an under-utilized content type that most brands would benefit from targeting, but you should not plan on the easy wins the framework produced originally. The practical consequence is that the head comparison terms between two large vendors are now contested, while the long-tail pairings involving your own brand or a smaller competitor remain open. That is also where the zero-volume finding sits, so the two points reinforce each other.

> "they have grown in popularity in the SEO community"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say comparison keywords were easier to rank for when they began using them years ago and now have more competition, while still calling them under-utilized.

**How to do it**

1. Split the comparison list into two: pairings that include your brand, and pairings between two third parties.
2. Search each third-party pairing and check whether dedicated review-site pages already hold the top three; deprioritize those.
3. Keep every pairing that names your own brand, since you have the strongest claim to it and competition is thin.
4. For contested third-party pairings, add a qualifier such as an industry or business size to open a narrower SERP.
5. Do not use reported difficulty scores as the filter; read the actual results and judge who they are written by.
6. Track rankings on the comparison set separately from the rest of the blog, since the difficulty profile is different.

**Pitfall:** Planning a comparison content push on the assumption that these are still uncontested. Grow and Convert say the head pairings have attracted competition since the tactic became widely known, so a plan built on old case studies will miss its ranking timeline.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's competitor comparison pages should lead with pairings naming Pabau, and treat third-party pairings between two large aesthetics platforms as the harder second wave.

**Apply anywhere:** Prioritize comparison pairings that include your own brand, and treat contested pairings between two third-party vendors as a harder second phase needing a qualifier to open a narrower SERP.

### 7. Expect interesting, high-quality stories to grow traffic and not convert  `176.8`
*useful · content insights · source 176*

Grow and Convert's counter-example is specific and worth recording. They produced interview-based stories with influential people in each client's industry and shared them on social, on the logic that these were more interesting and higher quality than the top-10 lists and ultimate guides everyone was pumping out. The traffic result was strong and fast across clients. The conversion result was close to zero, and the traffic died out once the social push ended. So editorial quality and interest do not predict conversion, and a format can beat the competition on craft while losing on business outcome. The pages that converted were ranked bottom-of-funnel pieces that kept pulling traffic continuously.

> "more interesting and "higher quality" than top 10 lists"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert grew client traffic a lot and very quickly with interview stories, yet many of those stories produced barely any conversions.

**How to do it**

1. Separate your content inventory into interest-led pieces and search-led bottom-of-funnel pieces.
2. Report conversions per piece for each group rather than blended blog conversion rate.
3. Check the traffic decay curve for the interest-led group; a fast peak and decline confirms the pattern.
4. Cap the interest-led group at a fixed share of the calendar, funded as brand spend, not as pipeline.
5. Move the freed capacity to keywords where searchers are already evaluating solutions.
6. Re-run the comparison each quarter so the split stays evidence-led.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Defending the interest-led format on engagement metrics. Time on page and shares can be excellent while conversions stay at zero.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat founder-interview and industry-story posts as brand spend with a capped share of the calendar, and keep the pipeline expectation on bottom-of-funnel practice-management and template pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Budget interest-led editorial as brand spend with a capped share of the calendar, and hold your pipeline expectation on ranked bottom-of-funnel pages instead.

### 8. Expect list posts to beat landing pages when intent is to view options  `146.8`
*useful · content insights · source 146*

Grow and Convert's mechanism for why blog posts outrank product landing pages on bottom-of-funnel category keywords is intent, not content length alone. The intent behind a term like 'proposal creation software' is usually to see the available options. A list post satisfies that directly, while a product landing page answers a narrower question the searcher did not ask. They observed the same pattern in the SERP they used as the opening example, where product landing pages, home pages and list-style blog posts all appear together. A second mechanism sits alongside it: a product landing page is typically thin on content, with less room to discuss positioning, explain how the product differs from competitors, and hold attention long enough to satisfy the query.

> "the intent of a BOF product category keyword is to view options"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's SERP for 'proposal creation software' shows product landing pages, home pages and list-style blog posts ranking together.

**Pitfall:** Treating a category keyword as a buying keyword. The searcher is comparing, not choosing, and a single-product page gets skipped even when it ranks.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau targets a plural or category phrasing such as software for a treatment vertical, David should brief a comparison piece, and reserve product pages for terms where searchers already want Pabau specifically.

**Apply anywhere:** When targeting a plural or category phrasing, brief a comparison piece, and reserve product pages for terms where searchers already want your brand specifically.

### 9. Expect page-one long-tail rankings around month five against WebMD-class sites  `141.14`
*useful · content insights · source 141*

The concussion treatment center faced SERPs owned by WebMD, Healthline and MayoClinic, sites with domain authorities close to double the client's. Grow and Convert did not try to compete on the head terms. They went after long-tail high-intent keywords from the start, and their stated content requirement was that the pieces be extremely detailed, structured properly, and thorough enough to outrank the incumbent content. The result, read off an Ahrefs screenshot, was first-page rankings within the first five months for multiple keyword variations on the low-volume high-intent terms. The harder terms only began gaining traction on pages 1 to 2 later, and were queued for link building. So the practical expectation against a big-publisher SERP is roughly five months to page one on the long tail, with the head terms as a second phase supported by links.

> "we started ranking within the first 5 or so months"

**Evidence:** Concussion treatment center: first-page rankings for multiple long-tail keyword variations within roughly five months, against sites with nearly double its domain authority.

**How to do it**

1. Check the top 10 for your target head terms and note whether large publisher sites hold them.
2. If they do, drop the head terms from the first phase entirely and build a long-tail high-intent list instead.
3. For each long-tail term, check what the ranking page actually covers and plan a piece that is more detailed and better structured than it.
4. Publish and expect page-one placements on multiple keyword variations at around month five, not month two.
5. Track keyword variations, not just the exact target, since the wins arrive spread across variants.
6. Once the long tail is exhausted, point internal links from those ranking pages at the head-term pages.
7. Add external link building to the head-term pages as the second phase, targeting page 1 to 2.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Chasing the head terms first against WebMD-class incumbents burns the first five months with nothing ranking and no conversions to show for it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's health-adjacent topics sit in SERPs owned by large publishers, so David should keep first-phase targets on long-tail practice-operations and software terms, and treat any head medical term as a later phase supported by internal links from pages that already rank.

**Apply anywhere:** When large publishers own your head terms, target long-tail high-intent variants first with pieces that are more detailed and better structured than the incumbents. Expect page one at around month five, then support the head terms with internal and external links as a second phase.

### 10. Expect thin competitor pages to be the opening on commercial SERPs  `96.16`
*useful · content insights · source 96*

Grow and Convert's TapClicks analysis produced a specific and repeatable finding. The strength of the ranking pages was visual: they gave plenty of visual examples showing what the dashboard products look and feel like to use. The weaknesses were that most results were thin on content, overweighted beginner topics like 'What is a paid search dashboard?', and failed to communicate what made each product different from the others, leaving users unable to work out which product was best for them. That pattern, thin plus generic plus non-differentiating, is common on product-led commercial SERPs because vendors write about themselves rather than about the buyer's choice. The gap it leaves is the comparison question, and answering it directly is often enough to outrank pages with more authority.

> "Most results were thin on content"

**Evidence:** TapClicks reached position 2 for 'paid search dashboard' by keeping the visual examples and adding explicit product differentiation the ranking pages lacked.

**Pitfall:** Copying the competitors' strength, in this case visual examples, without fixing their weakness. You match the SERP and give the reader no reason to choose you.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau comparison and category terms, expect competitor pages to be thin and non-differentiating. Answer 'which one fits my practice type' directly, with visual examples of the product in use.

**Apply anywhere:** On commercial SERPs, expect thin, non-differentiating vendor pages. Keep their visual examples and win by answering the comparison question they avoid.

### 11. Expect top-funnel content to convert only when it sells the product in the piece  `103.12`
*useful · content insights · source 103*

Grow and Convert are blunt about why most top-funnel content converts at close to zero. The ultimate guides, infographics and 'tips for X to do Y' pieces B2B marketers produce are beginner-level and uninteresting, sometimes off-putting, to advanced B2B audiences. More importantly, they rarely sell the company's product; often the product is not mentioned in the piece at all, which they say almost guarantees a minuscule conversion rate. Their own top-funnel winners are the exceptions that reach the right audience and weave the product in. The examples are Geekbot's 'daily standup meeting' and 'daily standup questions' pieces and Circuit's 'how to start a delivery business with contract drivers'. Each names a specific practitioner situation rather than an industry overview.

> "almost guarantees a minuscule conversion rate"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite Geekbot's daily standup pieces and Circuit's delivery business guide as top-funnel content that converted well because the audience was right and the product was woven in.

**How to do it**

1. Open each existing top-funnel article and count the mentions of your own product.
2. Flag any article with zero product mentions as a rewrite candidate, regardless of its traffic.
3. Check the reading level against your buyer: if the piece explains something your audience already knows, it is aimed at the wrong reader.
4. Rewrite so the product appears at the point in the workflow where it would actually be used.
5. Replace generic tips sections with the specific situation the target practitioner is in.
6. Kill infographic-and-ultimate-guide formats that have no natural product entry point.
7. Re-measure on first-interaction product conversions 90 days after the rewrite.

**Pitfall:** High-traffic top-funnel pages get protected because of their sessions. Traffic is exactly why nobody notices they have converted nobody for two years.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's general aesthetics articles should be audited for product mentions. Any that never mention practice management software need either a rewrite that places Pabau in the workflow or removal from the priority set.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit top-funnel articles for product mentions and reading level. A piece that never names your product, or that teaches your buyer something they already know, will not convert whatever its traffic.

### 12. Expect top-of-funnel briefs to be chosen for staffing reasons, not strategy  `140.14`
*useful · content insights · source 140*

Lewis offers a blunt explanation for why so many companies default to top-of-funnel content. Those readers are beginners, so the work can be done by an average skilled freelance writer compiling the top ten results, with no expert involved. It often ranks. Bottom-of-funnel content cannot be produced that way, because it needs expertise in the topic and in the company, and the searcher wants detailed comparisons between solutions. She adds a caveat against using this as permission to write shallow top-of-funnel pieces: you should be teaching the reader something new regardless of funnel stage. Treating this as an operational constraint rather than a strategic belief changes what you fix. If the real blocker is that nobody has expert access, buy the interview time instead of arguing about the funnel.

> "it doesn't require a knowledgeable expert to write the content"

**Evidence:** Lewis, a non-expert, only succeeded on bottom-of-funnel accessibility terms because her client provided per-topic subject matter interviews.

**How to do it**

1. When a plan is all top-of-funnel, ask whether the team has any route to an internal expert.
2. If not, treat expert access as the thing to buy: book recurring interview slots with sales or product.
3. Assign bottom-of-funnel comparison work only to writers who have had that interview.
4. Keep any top-of-funnel pieces you do commission, but require one thing in each that is not on page one.
5. Audit existing top-of-funnel posts for whether they teach anything, and refresh or merge the ones that do not.
6. Move the budget from freelance volume to fewer expert-backed bottom-of-funnel pieces.

**Pitfall:** Mandating bottom-of-funnel content without securing expert time produces comparison articles written from competitor marketing pages, which knowledgeable searchers abandon quickly.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before David commissions more Pabau comparison content, he should lock recurring interview time with product and support. Without it the writer falls back on rival marketing pages, and aesthetic practice owners spot that immediately.

**Apply anywhere:** Recognize that a top-of-funnel-heavy plan is often a staffing decision, because beginner content can be assembled from the top ten by any competent freelancer. Bottom-of-funnel work cannot. If your plan is drifting upward, buy recurring expert interview time rather than arguing about strategy, and require every piece to teach something not already on page one.

### 13. Expect topic complexity to track how much buyers read  `137.13`
*useful · content insights · source 137*

Grow and Convert observe that content quality and complexity correlate with readability. If people read a lot about a topic, there are interesting, in-depth and complex ideas to discuss. If everything in a space is simple, it would not produce a community of in-depth readers. They contrast ranking for 'best mens boots', which is mostly a matter of showing up with a list, photos and basic feature differences, with their Cognitive FX posts on post concussion syndrome, how it works and how to treat it. This gives a practical read on what a topic demands before you commission it: a low-complexity space rewards format and coverage, a high-complexity space rewards depth and expertise, and using the wrong mode in either wastes the budget.

> "notice what is correlated with high readability"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert contrast 'best mens boots' with their advanced post concussion syndrome content, which outranked large medical publishers.

**How to do it**

1. Before commissioning, read the top five results and judge whether they are list-and-photo format or explanatory.
2. If the space is list-and-photo, brief for coverage, comparison tables, images and structure rather than depth.
3. If the space is explanatory, brief for mechanism, research and expert input, and budget for an expert interview.
4. Check whether an in-depth reader community exists as a second confirmation of complexity.
5. Set word count and cost from that judgement rather than from a fixed house template.
6. Do not send a shallow format into a complex space, or a 3,000-word explainer into a boots-style SERP.

**Pitfall:** Applying one house content template to every topic. A deep explainer in a simple SERP loses to a clean list, and a clean list in a complex SERP is ignored by readers who have already read ten of them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /templates/ pages sit in the simple mode and need format, download and clarity. Clinical and compliance topics on /blog/ sit in the complex mode and need a specialist interview and an original diagram. David should set the brief type from the SERP, not from a single house template.

**Apply anywhere:** Read the SERP to decide which mode a topic is in. Simple spaces reward format, coverage and images; complex spaces reward mechanism and expert input. Match the brief to the mode instead of running one template everywhere.

### 14. Expect unbacked claims to cost trust and conversions, not just quality  `99.14`
*useful · content insights · source 99*

Khanal frames the cost of claim-only writing in commercial terms, which is what makes it a business problem rather than a style preference. When an article lists claim after claim with little detail, explanation or discussion, the reader gets the feeling that the whole piece is stating the same thesis over and over with no added insight or color. For the business that owns the blog, he says this is a really bad outcome, because it reduces trust and ultimately the likelihood that readers will convert. The mechanism is that detail is the only available proof of expertise at the moment of reading. A reader who cannot tell whether the author knows the subject defaults to assuming they do not, and a vendor who cannot demonstrate knowledge in an article is not a credible vendor for the work. Khanal separately notes detail is also what makes a piece worth linking to.

> "It will reduce trust and ultimately the likelihood that readers will convert"

**Evidence:** Khanal's stated outcome chain for claim-only articles: same thesis repeated, no added insight, reduced trust, reduced conversion.

**Pitfall:** Content teams measure the failure as low engagement or weak rankings and respond with more posts. The actual failure is a trust deficit inside the pages they already have.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau article gets traffic but no demo requests, audit it for claim density before rewriting the CTA. Unbacked claims about workflow benefits are the likelier cause than CTA placement.

**Apply anywhere:** When an article gets traffic but no conversions, audit it for claim density before rewriting the CTA. Unbacked benefit claims are a likelier cause than CTA placement.

### 15. Expect users to adopt your product for a pain point you did not build for  `159.7`
*useful · content insights · source 159*

Grow and Convert name two distinct pain point failures. The first is simply getting it wrong: you build software for pain point X and users end up using it for pain point Y. They say this happens with countless SaaS startups and it happened with their own course, which was built and sold as a way to drive leads for a company while buyers wanted it to make themselves better marketers. The second is having too many pain points and choosing badly among them. The distinction matters because the fixes differ. Wrong pain point is a research failure and is fixed by talking to users about what they actually do with the product. Too many is a prioritization failure fixed by picking one theme for the hero. Neither is fixed by adding features.

> "You build software for pain point 'X', but users"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own course was positioned around company lead generation while buyers wanted individual skill growth, a gap only visible after post-launch interviews.

**Pitfall:** Reading low conversion as a demand problem when it is a mismatch between the advertised pain point and the one users actually feel.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should periodically ask new customers what problem they bought the software to solve, then check whether the top answer matches what the marketing site leads with. A mismatch is a copy job, not a product job.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask new customers what problem they bought the product to solve, then check the answer against what your marketing site leads with. A mismatch is a copy problem, not a product one.

### 16. Fact-check every category claim you make about a named competitor  `72.9`
*useful · best practices · source 72*

Mid-edit Khanal tries to assert that Copy.ai and Wordtune are short-form-only tools and stops to check. He opens their sites, finds his memory was not reliable, and says you do not want to say the wrong thing, you need to be accurate. He extends this to legal and relationship exposure: a named vendor like Jasper could email asking what you are talking about, and the risk scales with your brand's size. His new-brand caveat is honest rather than permissive, noting nobody may care what a brand-new tool publishes, but he still does not publish the claim. The point is that competitor categorization feels like a low-stakes editorial choice and is actually a factual claim about another company's product that the vendor can check.

> "You need to be accurate"

**Evidence:** Khanal checked Copy.ai and Wordtune live, found their feature sets broader than his framing assumed, and abandoned the categorization rather than publish it.

**How to do it**

1. Highlight every sentence in a draft that assigns a named competitor to a category or limits what it does.
2. Open that vendor's own product, solutions and pricing pages, not a third-party roundup.
3. Confirm the claim against their current feature list, since vendors expand scope constantly.
4. Rewrite the claim to describe what the vendor says it does, or delete it.
5. If the claim survives, cite or link the vendor page you verified it against.
6. Scale the checking effort to your brand size: the bigger you are, the more likely a vendor pushes back.

**Pitfall:** Trusting your own recollection of a competitor's scope. Khanal was confident Copy.ai was short-form only and had to check; the signal is a claim you can state fluently but cannot point to a page for.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's fact-check pass already covers this, but the rule should extend explicitly to category claims, not just numbers: any sentence saying a competitor only does X must be checked against that competitor's own site before publishing.

**Apply anywhere:** Verify every claim you make about what a named competitor does or does not do against that vendor's own pages, because scope claims are factual claims they can dispute.

### 17. Find product and content gaps in what expert customers get wrong  `170.3`
*useful · content insights · source 170*

Campbell traces ProfitWell's founding to a specific moment. His team was sitting in the boardroom of a client preparing to IPO and realized the client was calculating monthly recurring revenue incorrectly. It was the third company that CEO had taken public. The signal Campbell reads is not that the CEO was incompetent, but that if the most sophisticated customer in the market gets a fundamental calculation wrong, the whole market does. That is a content gap as much as a product gap: the definitive, correct explanation of the calculation has an audience of experts, not beginners. Campbell paired it with a second observation, that accurate financial data was simply hard to collect, and built the product on both.

> "they were calculating MRR completely incorrectly"

**Evidence:** The client was a company about to IPO, run by a CEO taking his third company public, and it was miscalculating MRR.

**Pitfall:** The temptation is to write the beginner explainer instead. If your most experienced customers get something wrong, the beginner version is already everywhere and the expert-level correction is the piece nobody has written.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau account manager finds an established clinic miscalculating something basic — no-show rate, client retention, treatment margin — that is the article. David should commission the exact-definition piece with a worked example, not another introductory overview.

**Apply anywhere:** Watch for the calculation or definition your most sophisticated customers still get wrong. That is your highest-value article, because the beginner version already exists and the authoritative correction does not.

### 18. Fix mirage content by interviewing practitioners instead of summarizing search results  `122.10`
*useful · content insights · source 122*

Grow and Convert show what FundersClub published after the exercise. > "not just a content marketer Googling around and summarizing info"

**Evidence:** FundersClub's post-exercise pieces on technical founders selling and on keeping engineers happy were both built from founder and academic interviews.

**How to do it**

1. For each pain point on the researched list, name one practitioner who has actually solved it.
2. Book a 30-minute interview and ask for the specific decisions and numbers, not general advice.
3. Build the piece around that interview, quoting the practitioner directly and by name.
4. Where no interview is possible, use a named expert's stated opinion rather than a synthesis of ranking pages.
5. Ban briefs that ask a writer to research a topic online and summarize it.
6. Check the finished piece for at least one claim that could not have been found on page one of Google.

**Pitfall:** A summarize-the-SERP piece reads competently and contains nothing the profiled buyer did not already know, which is the same failure as the Series A checklist in a different costume.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should source at least one named clinic operator or clinical practitioner quote per blog post on practice operations. That is also what supports the experience half of E-E-A-T on health-adjacent topics.

**Apply anywhere:** Build pieces from interviews with people who have solved the problem, not from a writer's summary of what already ranks. Expert opinion is what keeps a post in sync with an experienced reader.

### 19. Fluff signals shallow research and repels the buyers you most want  `92.8`
*useful · content insights · source 92*

Grow and Convert argue fluff has a specific business cost rather than being a style complaint. Most readers will not name it as fluff, but they get a sense of unease reading shallow writing. To someone who knows the topic, such as a high-end savvy B2B buyer, fluff immediately reads as weird or amateurish. Their question is how you expect to convert the best customers when your content is full of it. They also diagnose where it comes from. Writers must produce words, and when they do not truly know what the reader cares about, they fill the space with fluff. As proof they point out that the same writer, asked to write about their own hobby or a political issue they care about, produces dramatically less fluffy writing full of specific arguments. The writer has not changed, the depth of knowledge has. They distinguish fluff from their older concept Mirage Content: fluff is at the word and phrase level, Mirage Content is when the arguments themselves are beginner-level or cliche. A piece can be fluff-free and still be Mirage Content.

> "fluff immediately stands out as weird or amateurish"

**Evidence:** Fluff is Grow and Convert's number one challenge with new writer applicants, and clients repeatedly named it as their biggest annoyance with previous content teams.

**How to do it**

1. Treat fluff density as a research-depth metric, not a style issue: count fluff phrases per 1000 words and log it per writer.
2. When a draft comes back fluffy, do not line-edit it; send the writer back for interviews or product testing.
3. Run a second check for Mirage Content, asking whether any argument in the piece is above beginner level.
4. Ask a subject-matter expert, not an editor, to read the draft and mark anything that reads as obvious to them.
5. Rewrite marked passages with a specific number, workflow or scenario from research.
6. Use hobby-level specificity as the bar: the draft should carry details only someone inside the space would know.

**Pitfall:** Editing fluff out of a shallow draft produces a shorter shallow draft. Grow and Convert say clients complained about spending hours turning fluffy writing into something concrete, which is the signal the research, not the prose, is missing.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau draft comes back padded, send it back for source work rather than editing the padding out. The fix is a support-team interview or a screenshot walkthrough of the feature, not tighter sentences.

**Apply anywhere:** When a draft comes back padded, send it back for source work rather than editing the padding out. The fix is an expert interview or hands-on product use, not tighter sentences.

### 20. Give an update a month and expect rank volatility before judging it  `119.9`
*useful · best practices · source 119*

Grow and Convert set expectations for what happens after a refresh. It can take a few weeks or a month for an update to make a significant impact, meaning rankings recover or improve. In that window you will likely see volatility, with the post jumping around as Google works out where to place it. They say the movement eventually settles and you then see the content's new rank. This matters operationally because a team that checks the tracker three days after a rewrite will read the noise as a failure and either revert the change or start a second rewrite on top of the first. The base's related finding is that Google actively tests new and updated pages for roughly two weeks, which fits this observation.

> "a few weeks or a month for updates to make a significant impact"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated timeframe from client work: a few weeks to a month to impact, with volatility throughout as Google finds a new home for the post.

**How to do it**

1. Log the update date and the pre-update position for the keyword the day you publish the change.
2. Do not read the ranking at all for the first two weeks.
3. From week three, record the position weekly rather than daily.
4. Treat any swing inside that window as testing, not as a result.
5. Judge the update at the one-month mark against the pre-update position you logged.
6. If the position has settled higher, stop and leave the page alone.
7. If it has settled flat or lower, escalate rather than re-editing the same copy.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Stacking a second rewrite on top of the first inside the volatility window. You lose the ability to attribute either change and often undo the part that was working.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should record the update date and starting position for every refreshed article and review at 30 days. David should block re-edits of a page inside that window unless something is factually wrong.

**Apply anywhere:** After a refresh, log the date and starting position, ignore the rankings for two weeks, and judge the outcome at one month. The swings in between are testing, not signal.

### 21. Good content solves the underlying problem, not the literal query  `28.9`
*useful · content insights · source 28*

Endre's operational definition of 'good content' evolved from answering the literal query, such as how to sign the word hello, to addressing the broader situation behind the query, such as how to communicate respectfully and confidently as a beginner interacting with the Deaf community in a real situation like customer service. This shift, moving a step down the funnel from single-sentence lookups toward broader communication-situation guidance, is what created a lot more engagement and continuous traffic than the narrower informational content, because it goes beyond the surface layer of the immediate question into context, culture, and the specific problem the reader is actually trying to solve.

> "That's created a lot more engagement for us"

**Evidence:** Endre's direct contrast: content that only teaches 'hello, welcome back' versus content that also covers how to get the attention of a member of the Deaf community respectfully and normalizes making mistakes as a learner, with the broader version explicitly credited as what created a lot more engagement and got picked up well by search engines.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau content answers a narrow how-to query, such as how to reduce no-shows, extend it to the broader situation the reader is actually navigating, such as the full patient-communication and scheduling workflow around no-shows, rather than stopping at the literal answer, since the broader framing is both harder for an AI Overview to fully extract and more engaging for the reader.

**Apply anywhere:** When your content answers a narrow how-to query, extend it to the broader situation the reader is actually navigating — the full workflow surrounding the immediate question — rather than stopping at the literal answer, since the broader framing is both harder for an AI Overview to fully extract and more engaging for the reader.

### 22. Google judges subjective content only by how people react to it  `53.11`
*useful · content insights · source 53*

David Quaid pushes back on the common SEO assumption that Google can directly assess content quality. He distinguishes verifiable factual content, "the capital of France is Paris," which Google can check as correct, from the much larger volume of content that is opinion, observation, ideological, or philosophical, territory where he argues Google fundamentally can't weigh in. His conclusion is that for that non-factual majority of content, the only way Google can effectively judge it is indirectly, by observing how people react to it (behavioral signals), rather than by understanding or evaluating the substance of the writing itself. He collapses this into a simple operating summary of what SEO actually is: mostly relevance plus authority plus avoid spam tactics, not content quality judged on its own merits.

> "the only way it can really measure it is how people react"

**Evidence:** David Quaid's direct contrast between factual, checkable claims and opinion/ideological/philosophical content, concluding the only way it can really measure it is how people react with it, and his summary framing that SEO is mostly relevance plus authority plus avoid spam tactics.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau content shouldn't be optimized on the assumption that Google, or an LLM, directly comprehends and rewards well-reasoned arguments in subjective or advisory content — instead, treat user behavioral signals such as engagement, low pogo-sticking, and return visits as the real proxy Google uses, and keep investing in relevance and authority-building, internal linking and backlinks, rather than assuming quality writing alone will be independently recognized.

**Apply anywhere:** Your content shouldn't be optimized on the assumption that Google, or an LLM, directly comprehends and rewards well-reasoned arguments in subjective or advisory content — instead, treat user behavioral signals such as engagement, low pogo-sticking, and return visits as the real proxy Google uses, and keep investing in relevance and authority-building, internal linking and backlinks, rather than assuming quality writing alone will be independently recognized.
