### 1. Prefer a country TLD over a .com when you are targeting one market  `67.13`
*useful · best practices · source 67*

Dirk treats country-code TLDs as a real ranking signal for geo-specific targeting, and credits it as part of why his one successful revived domain worked, since it was not a .com and sat in a very specific geo. His practical guidance is to check registration friction per country before committing: some ccTLDs need local presence, such as .ca requiring Canadian citizenship or a Canadian address, while others like .co.uk are easy to register from anywhere. Where you need one you cannot register directly, he says to use partner entities in that country to acquire it. He extends the same logic to brand protection: if a competitor takes yourbrand.ca and you later want to rank in Canada, they hold the upper hand there.

> "a dot ca is always much more of a stronger signal"

**Evidence:** Dirk's revived travel site ran on a country-specific TLD in a very specific geo, and he says he believes that is part of why it worked.

**How to do it**

1. Decide the single country the site will target before choosing the domain.
2. Check that country's registry rules for local-presence requirements, such as .ca needing Canadian citizenship or a Canadian address.
3. Where local presence is required, arrange a partner entity in that country to hold the registration.
4. Register the ccTLD version of your brand in every market you plan to enter, ahead of entering it.
5. Prefer the ccTLD over a .com for a single-market site, since Dirk treats it as a stronger geo signal.
6. Point the ccTLD at genuinely localized content rather than a duplicate of the .com.
7. Add every ccTLD you hold to the same central renewal monitoring as the main domain.

**Pitfall:** Waiting until you enter a market to buy its ccTLD. By then a squatter or competitor may hold the brand version, and Dirk says at that point they hold the upper hand in that country.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau opens a new market, register the ccTLD for the brand in that country, using a local entity where registration requires local presence. This is cheaper as prevention than as recovery.

**Apply anywhere:** Register the country TLD for your brand before you enter a market, using a local partner entity where the registry requires local presence, and favor a ccTLD over a .com when you are targeting one country.

### 2. Prefer the positioning pivot because it is cheaper than a product pivot  `159.15`
*useful · best practices · source 159*

Grow and Convert state it plainly: product pivots are more costly and time consuming than positioning pivots. Their advice is to first consider what pain points your existing product or service can already solve before trying to add new things to it. The sequence they endorse for a genuine pivot is strict. The initial product-market-fit hypothesis fails, you run customer research to find who to target and what they care about, you find a burning pain point in a specific group, and only then do you discover your current product cannot solve it. Their examples show the same shape: the course adjusted to help individuals improve, QuickBooks adding tax functionality if research showed taxes were the burning issue, a dev agency learning or acquiring AI and machine learning skills for a segment with burning demand.

> "product pivots are more costly and time consuming than positioning pivots"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own turnaround came from a copy change alone: same product, roughly six times the conversion rate.

**How to do it**

1. When growth stalls, list every pain point your current product already solves well, before listing anything you could build.
2. Run customer research to find the burning pain points in your target group.
3. Match each burning pain point against the existing list and take any match as a repositioning opportunity, not a build.
4. Reposition the site and sales copy around the best match and measure conversion for one full cycle.
5. Only if no burning pain point maps to existing capability, scope a product change.
6. Cost both routes explicitly before deciding, in weeks of work rather than in enthusiasm.
7. Reposition alongside any product change, since new functionality with old messaging does not sell itself.

**Pitfall:** Building first and researching second. Teams add features to solve a pain point they never confirmed anyone has, then still have to reposition afterwards.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau feature area underperforms in demos, David should first check whether the marketing pages already have a capability worth leading with, before the request goes to product as a gap.

**Apply anywhere:** When growth stalls, list what your product already does well and match it to researched burning pain points. Reposition first; scope a build only when nothing matches.

### 3. Price SEO in the mid to high thousands per month before modeling value  `129.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 129*

Grow and Convert put a band around SEO cost so the estimate does not stall. It will not be $50,000 a month and it will not be $200 a month; expect somewhere in the mid to high thousands, depending on team makeup. They disclose that the most popular plan at their own agency is $10,000 a month. Cost is almost entirely people: an agency, a group of freelancers and consultants, or an in-house manager or team. For in-house work they give a specific method — estimate the percentage of each employee's time that would go to SEO and multiply by their salary. Their instruction is not to chase precision. Get a number that is close enough and move on to estimating value, because if SEO is genuinely worth it the value should dwarf the cost rather than edge past it.

> "The most popular plan for our SEO agency, for example, is $10,000 per month."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's most popular agency plan is $10,000 per month, and they place normal SEO spend in the mid to high thousands monthly.

**How to do it**

1. List the roles the work needs: keyword research, content production, editing, link building, technical fixes.
2. Get two or three real quotes for agency, freelance and in-house options rather than guessing a market rate.
3. For existing employees, estimate the percentage of their time SEO would take and multiply by salary, then add it to the total.
4. Anchor the result in the mid to high thousands per month; treat anything near $200 or near $50,000 as a modelling error.
5. Add the non-people costs: keyword and rank tooling, and any paid link or placement budget.
6. Round to a single monthly figure and stop refining it.
7. Move immediately to the value estimate, which is where the decision actually turns.

**Pitfall:** Spending days pricing the cost side. It is the fixed, knowable half of the equation, and precision there buys nothing when the value side carries all the uncertainty.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should carry a single fixed monthly SEO figure that includes the percentage of internal marketing time spent on content, editing and visuals, not just external costs. That figure is the breakeven line every page family gets judged against.

**Apply anywhere:** Price SEO quickly and roughly. Get real quotes for agency, freelance and in-house options, add the percentage of any existing employee's salary that would go to the work, and land on a single monthly figure in the mid to high thousands. Then stop refining it and spend the effort on the value estimate.

### 4. Price a premium exact-match domain as opportunity cost, not sunk cost  `171.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 171*

Sol Orwell bought Examine.com for $41,000 before building anything, which inverts the usual advice to ship an MVP and prove traction first. His justification is a resale argument rather than a branding one. He had worked in the domain industry, and he says he could have sold Examine.com for $30,000 to $35,000 within 24 hours. That makes the real sunk cost $5,000 to $10,000, with the remaining $30,000 sitting as opportunity cost in an asset he can recover. He flew to Panama to buy it because some of the biggest domain brokers are there. The order matters: the idea came first, the domain second.

> "a domain name is not a sunk cost, it's an opportunity cost"

**Evidence:** Examine.com cost $41,000; Sol estimated a 24-hour resale of $30,000-$35,000, leaving a real cost of $5,000-$10,000.

**How to do it**

1. Settle the idea before you shop for a domain; Sol is explicit that the idea came first.
2. Check comparable sales for the exact string before naming a price, so you know the liquid resale value.
3. Calculate the true cost as purchase price minus the 24-hour resale price, and judge the deal on that number only.
4. Buy through an established broker rather than a marketplace listing when the name is category-defining.
5. Treat the difference between purchase and resale as the fee you are paying for the brand, and size it against expected revenue.
6. Do not mortgage anything for it; Sol's framing is explicitly that he was not an artist chasing a dream.
7. Re-check the resale value periodically, since the asset only stays an opportunity cost while a buyer exists.

**Pitfall:** The recoverable-asset argument only holds for a genuinely liquid, category-defining name. A niche or branded coinage has no 24-hour buyer, so the whole purchase price is sunk.

**Apply at Pabau:** Not a move for Pabau's main domain, but the framing is useful if David ever evaluates buying a category term for a microsite. Price it on the resale spread, not the sticker.

**Apply anywhere:** If you are weighing a premium exact-match domain, price it on the spread between what you pay and what it would resell for in a day. That spread is the real cost. It only works for liquid, category-defining names.

### 5. Prioritize bottom-of-funnel awareness stages before broad topics  `11.1`
*useful · content insights · source 11*

Nathan Gotch's five stages of customer awareness model ranks content priority from bottom to top of a pyramid: most-aware (near-conversion queries like 'hire Nathan Gotch as an SEO consultant' or a SaaS free-trial/demo query), product-aware (branded, review-type queries), the 'best + solution [+ location]' stage which he calls where most SEO magic happens today and the stage to track across both traditional search and AI answers, then broader informational queries such as 'why isn't my website ranking on Google?', and finally fully unaware queries like 'how do I get more customers online?'. He states explicitly this concept generalizes beyond SEO to YouTube, X, and LinkedIn content strategy. The actionable rule is to build pages bottom-up, most-aware first, because that drives revenue even though search volume is smaller there; the single biggest mistake he sees is starting at the broad, unaware, high-volume end because the volume looks seductive, when that traffic converts poorly and needs the most nurturing to reach a sale.

> "You want to start at the bottom of the funnel"

**Evidence:** Gotch's own funnel examples at each stage (a SaaS 'free trial or demo' query as most-aware, 'best SEO consultant in St. Louis' as the AI-answer-tracking stage, 'why isn't my website ranking on Google?' as broad/informational, 'how do I get more customers online?' as fully unaware), and his explicit naming of starting at the unaware stage as the mistake a lot of people make.

**Apply at Pabau:** When planning Pabau's content calendar, sequence new pages by funnel stage rather than by search volume — prioritize the highest-intent, most-aware pages before broad educational content, and track 'best [solution] for [use case]' style queries specifically across both Google and AI answers since Gotch flags that as where the current competitive battle is.

**Apply anywhere:** When planning your content calendar, sequence new pages by funnel stage rather than by search volume — prioritize the highest-intent, most-aware pages before broad educational content, and track 'best [solution] for [use case]' style queries specifically across both Google and AI answers since Gotch flags that as where the current competitive battle is.

### 6. Prioritize owned content because it pays out on two channels at once  `79.15`
*useful · best practices · source 79*

Grow & Convert's resource-allocation argument is that owned content is the only GEO tactic that returns on two channels from one effort. Their position is that SEO still works, is still driving far more traffic than ChatGPT, is the foundation of Google AI Overviews, and is still producing leads month over month for clients. They show monthly free trial starts from people landing on their content over two years for one client, noting the figure excludes conversions from AI mentions the content influenced and reflects only direct article traffic. Off-site mentions, by contrast, pay out on GEO alone. So when budget is the constraint, the same article both ranks and feeds the models, while a Reddit mention does one job. This is a prioritization claim, not a claim that PR is worthless.

> "two channels in one effort"

**Evidence:** Two years of monthly free trial starts from one client's Grow & Convert content, excluding AI-influenced conversions.

**How to do it**

1. Score each candidate GEO tactic by how many channels it pays into before you fund it.
2. Fund the article that both ranks and gets cited before funding a mention that only gets cited.
3. Report article performance with organic conversions and AI mentions side by side, so the dual return is visible.
4. Note explicitly that direct-traffic conversion numbers exclude AI-influenced conversions, so the article's real return is understated.
5. Revisit the split only once the bottom-of-funnel page set is ranking.

**Pitfall:** Attribution understates owned content, because conversions influenced by an AI mention of the article do not appear in the article's traffic numbers. Judging the channel on direct traffic alone underfunds it.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David compares a new Pabau article against a PR placement, count that the article earns organic traffic and AI citations from one spend. Pabau's reporting should show organic conversions and AI mentions on the same row per article.

**Apply anywhere:** When comparing a new article against a PR placement, count that the article earns organic traffic and AI citations from one spend. Report both on the same row.

### 7. Promote a post to communities once, then stop and let search compound  `105.11`
*useful · best practices · source 105*

Grow and Convert use their own history as the counter-example. They grew their blog to 32,977 unique visitors in five months using nothing but content promotion in communities, and they point out the honest reading of that result: they increased traffic, not leads. Social and community traffic is spikey by nature. If something catches, you get a burst that dies within a day or two, and holding a constant presence to keep generating those bursts is continuous work that rarely converts into consistent lead flow. Search behaves the opposite way. Once a page ranks it typically holds for months or years with light maintenance, so the investment compounds instead of resetting. Their conclusion is to share each post socially for a short-term bump and then move on.

> "to grow our blog to 32,977 unique visitors in 5 months"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert grew their own blog to 32,977 unique visitors in 5 months on community promotion alone, and note it raised traffic rather than leads; they report the same spikey pattern across dozens of clients.

**How to do it**

1. Publish the post and share it once to the communities and social accounts where it genuinely fits.
2. Cap the promotion window at a few days and do not build a recurring schedule around resurfacing it.
3. Do not count the resulting spike as a lead-generation result; record it as traffic only.
4. Redirect the recurring time you would have spent on social upkeep into ranking maintenance.
5. Spend that time on content updates and link building for pages already targeting buying-intent terms.
6. Track community and search traffic in separate GA4 reports so the spike never flatters the evergreen number.
7. Judge each channel on conversions, not sessions, after one full quarter.
8. Keep social as a supplementary channel and never let it become the foundation of the lead plan.

**Tools:** Google Analytics 4

**Pitfall:** Community promotion can produce impressive traffic charts that never move the lead count. The signal is a strong month of sessions from social with no matching movement in demo requests, followed by a collapse a day or two after each push.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should share each new article once and stop. David should put the recurring social effort into updating and link-building the bottom-funnel pages, since those hold rankings for months while a LinkedIn spike dies in two days.

**Apply anywhere:** Share each post once where it genuinely fits, cap promotion at a few days, and record the result as traffic rather than as leads. Put the recurring social upkeep time into updating and link-building the pages that rank for buying-intent terms, because those hold for months.

### 8. Publish long-form case studies as the asset that closes buyers  `178.15`
*useful · best practices · source 178*

Grow and Convert list six named long-form case studies as their answer to the proof factor, each with a specific outcome attached: Cognitive FX to 70,000 pageviews in 14 months, Leadfeeder scaled to over 200 signups a month, Geekbot's expansion from bottom-funnel to top-funnel content, Circuit from 920 to 14,577 sessions in six months, doing B2B content without domain expertise at Rainforest QA, and building a keyword strategy for a new innovative product. The pattern is that each case study covers a different situation a prospective buyer might be in, not six versions of the same win. They say this is the type of thing they looked for as buyers and could never find. A buyer self-selects into the case study that matches their circumstances, which is why coverage of situations matters more than the size of the numbers.

> "How we grew Cognitive FX to 70,000 pageviews in 14 months"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish six case studies spanning B2C, SaaS signups, funnel expansion, session growth, no-domain-expertise and new-category products.

**How to do it**

1. List the distinct buyer situations you serve: new product, no domain expertise, scaling from bottom to top funnel, low starting traffic.
2. Write one long-form case study per situation rather than several for your best result.
3. Put the specific outcome and timeframe in each title, including the starting figure where you have it.
4. Inside each, cover article count, keyword selection reasoning, time to rank and what did not work.
5. Link the set from your services page so buyers can self-select the matching one.
6. Refresh the numbers annually and note the current figure alongside the original.

**Pitfall:** Publishing six case studies that all describe the same kind of client. A buyer in a different situation reads them and concludes the process only works for that profile.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build case studies covering distinct practice situations, such as a single-room clinic, a multi-site group and a practice migrating from another system, and put the outcome and timeframe in each title.

**Apply anywhere:** Map the distinct situations your buyers arrive in and publish one detailed case study per situation, with the outcome and timeframe in the title, so buyers can self-select the one that matches them.

### 9. Read a tool purchase as a search for the easy way out  `175.7`
*useful · general insights · source 175*

The article's diagnosis of why stacks bloat is motivational, not budgetary. People invest in tools when they are looking for a simple fix, or when they are looking to take the easy way out. The confusion arrives later, when the tool does not produce results that it seems to produce for others. The gap is that the others already had the process the tool speeds up. This is worth naming in SEO specifically because the category is full of products that promise the outcome rather than the acceleration: rankings, AI visibility, content at scale. The author's blunt conclusion is that tools do not drive results, strategy does.

> "People invest in tools when they're looking for a simple fix"

**Pitfall:** Copying a competitor's stack because their results look good. You inherit their tools without their process and get none of the outcome.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a case study credits a tool for an SEO result, David should look for the process behind it before recommending the tool for Pabau. The blog should describe the process too, not just the software.

**Apply anywhere:** When a case study credits a tool for an SEO result, look for the process behind it before buying. Write about the process as well as the software when you publish.

### 10. Read great marketing as a diagnostic that reveals product problems  `160.12`
*useful · general insights · source 160*

Grow and Convert's underlying mechanism is that content marketing behaves like any other channel: executed properly, a lack of conversions signals a product/market fit or positioning issue rather than a content issue. They put it plainly, that good and even great marketing will not fix a product or company problem, it will only reveal one that needs solving. The failure mode they describe is the opposite reading. Marketers and founders assume people are not buying because not enough eyeballs have reached the site, so they chase more traffic, obsess over every detail of every article, and focus on the wrong metrics. Once the underlying issue is solved, they say content should scale as a channel over time.

> "It'll only reveal a problem that needs to be solved"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert traced every unsuccessful client engagement back to early-stage product or positioning gaps rather than to content execution.

**Pitfall:** Responding to flat conversions by adding traffic. More visitors from the same audience through the same broken offer produce the same result at higher cost, and reinforce the conclusion that the channel does not work.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau cluster brings qualified traffic and few demos, David should treat the data as a diagnosis of the offer or the demo page rather than a brief for more articles, and check the cold conversion rate before commissioning the next batch.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat flat conversions on well-executed content as a diagnosis, not a content brief. Marketing reveals product and positioning problems rather than fixing them, so investigate the offer before buying more traffic.

### 11. Read the agency's own sales call as the audition for its content  `179.13`
*useful · concrete actions · source 179*

Grow and Convert offer a passive test that runs before you ask any question. Watch what the agency does with its own sales conversation. If they ask detailed questions about your product, your customers and your competitors, and want to understand what makes you different and why customers choose you, they have the instinct their writers will need. If they go straight to keywords, traffic projections and their process, they do not. Their conclusion is direct: an agency that does not try to understand your business during the sales conversation is unlikely to produce content that accurately represents your business later. This is the same underlying capability the interview-based content process depends on, so the sales call is a free preview of the extraction skill you are actually buying.

> "Do they ask detailed questions about your product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say an agency that does not try to understand your business during the sales conversation is unlikely to produce content that accurately represents it afterward.

**How to do it**

1. Before the first call, decide you will let the agency lead and keep count of their questions.
2. Tally how many questions concern your product, your customers, your competitors and your differentiation, versus your budget, your traffic and their process.
3. Note whether anyone asks why customers choose you over alternatives, which is the differentiator question their writers will need answered.
4. Note whether they ask about your sales objections or common support issues, which signals they know where content material comes from.
5. Compare tallies across the shortlist rather than judging one call in isolation.
6. Down-rank any agency that opens with traffic projections before understanding what you sell.
7. Give the top scorer on curiosity a paid pilot on one buying-intent keyword rather than a full retainer.

**Pitfall:** Being flattered by a fast, confident pitch. An agency that arrives with traffic projections in the first call built them without knowing your product, which is exactly the process that produces generic content later.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau shortlists content or SEO vendors, David should score each first call on how many questions concerned aesthetic practices, competitors and differentiation, and treat that tally as a real selection criterion.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat the agency's own sales call as a preview of how its writers work. Count how many questions concern your product, customers, competitors and differentiation, versus your budget and their process. An agency that leads with traffic projections built them without knowing what you sell, and its content will read the same way. Compare the tallies across the shortlist and give the most curious one a paid pilot.

### 12. Read the client's domain authority to set the ranking timeline, not the plan  `140.12`
*useful · general insights · source 140*

Lewis attributes the speed of the ADA Compliance Pros results partly to inherited authority. The site's domain authority was around 42, which she says meant plenty of low hanging fruit and rankings for the target terms in a reasonable amount of time, roughly four months. She is careful about where the authority came from. The existing blog content had not been written to convert, but it had built authority over time, so the old content was useful as a base even though it was commercially useless. The lesson for planning is that the same bottom-of-funnel keyword set has a different timeline on a fresh domain, and four months is a figure attached to a mid-authority site with existing content, not a universal promise.

> "the site's domain authority was relatively strong (42)"

**Evidence:** ADA Compliance Pros ranked its target bottom-of-funnel terms in about four months on a site with roughly 42 domain authority and a pre-existing non-commercial blog.

**How to do it**

1. Check the site's existing authority and referring domains before quoting a timeline.
2. Pull the site's currently ranking pages to see whether prior content built topical authority in the area.
3. On a mid-authority site with existing content, plan for first-page results on low-competition bottom-of-funnel terms in about four months.
4. On a new or thin domain, extend the timeline and lean harder on paid distribution for the first six months.
5. Check the authority of the pages actually ranking for each target term, not just the site's own number.
6. Prefer terms where the ranking set includes at least one page weaker than yours.
7. State the timeline assumption in writing so a change in authority reopens it, not the strategy.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Copying a four-month case study timeline onto a brand-new domain sets up a failed engagement. The signal is month three with impressions but no page-one placements and a client who was promised leads.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has real domain authority and years of blog content, so David can expect new bottom-of-funnel comparison pages to move faster than a case study written for a startup site suggests. Use that to justify a larger batch, not a longer wait.

**Apply anywhere:** Before promising a timeline, check the site's authority and whether older content already built topical relevance. A mid-authority site with an existing blog can rank low-competition bottom-of-funnel terms in roughly four months; a new domain cannot, and needs paid distribution to fill the gap.

### 13. Read three signals that community content promotion has stopped working  `128.16`
*useful · general insights · source 128*

Grow and Convert built their blog from zero to over 24,000 visitors a month in 4.5 months on community content promotion, then called its decline publicly in December 2018 with three named reasons. First, over-crowded communities: more people were doing it than ever, people were flooding these communities with content, community managers became upset and members were overwhelmed by the flood of below-average company content. Second, algorithm changes: Facebook and LinkedIn de-prioritized posts containing external links to push companies toward paid ads rather than organic group discovery, and Reddit became harder to crack. Third, it was hard to scale as the agency grew, because it is manual labor per article per community. Those three are the diagnostic set for any manual placement channel, and the third one arrives regardless of the other two.

> "People were flooding these communities with content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert grew from 0 to over 24,000 visitors a month in 4.5 months on community promotion, then named crowding, link-suppressing algorithm changes and unscalability as the reasons it stopped working.

**How to do it**

1. Track referral traffic per community per article rather than in aggregate, so decay is visible early.
2. Watch for moderator pushback and rising removal rates, which precede the traffic drop.
3. Test whether posts with external links reach fewer people than posts without, on each platform you use.
4. Measure hours of manual work per article promoted and divide traffic by it to get a labor-adjusted return.
5. Treat a rising labor cost per visit as a decay signal in its own right, even while traffic holds.
6. When two of the three signals show, start the parallel channel test instead of pushing harder.
7. Keep the channel for the few communities where you have genuine standing, and stop the volume approach.

**Tools:** Reddit, Facebook, LinkedIn

**Pitfall:** Responding to falling returns with more volume. That accelerates moderator hostility and member fatigue, which is the mechanism that killed the channel in the first place.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau promotes articles in aesthetics practitioner groups, David should track referral traffic per group and hours spent per post, and treat rising labor per visit as the signal to shift budget to paid and search.

**Apply anywhere:** Watch three signals on any manual community promotion channel: crowding and moderator pushback, platform algorithms suppressing posts with external links, and rising manual hours per article. Track traffic per community and labor per visit, and start testing replacements when two of the three appear.

### 14. Rebrand a company blog as a media title to earn outsider trust  `123.8`
*useful · concrete actions · source 123*

Vistage Worldwide had lots of good content and a site that had existed for years, but only around 1,000 monthly unique visitors. Hyam's diagnosis was trust: readers outside the customer base did not see the site as a reputable source for CEOs, because the look and feel suggested company updates, branded content and opinions. The fix was structural rather than editorial. They renamed the site from 'Vistage Buzz Blog' to 'Executive Street' and stripped out much of the company branding so it read as a media publication covering topics that matter to C-level executives. The relaunch of that brand was then timed to the influencer guest post. In the year that followed the blog went from 1,000 to 20,000 monthly visitors.

> "remove a lot of the company branding from the site"

**Evidence:** Vistage's blog went from around 1,000 monthly unique visitors before the rebrand to 20,000 monthly visitors a year after launching as Executive Street.

**How to do it**

1. Check whether your blog's name contains the company name and reads as a corporate newsletter to a non-customer.
2. Rename the property to a title about the reader's job or subject, as 'Vistage Buzz Blog' became 'Executive Street'.
3. Strip company logos, product screenshots and company-news categories from the template and the navigation.
4. Rebuild the topic set around what the target reader needs professionally rather than what the company wants to announce.
5. Keep a clear but small ownership disclosure so the property is not deceptive about who runs it.
6. Time the rebrand launch to a piece of content that brings an outside audience, such as an influencer guest post.
7. Redirect the old URLs and keep the existing archive, since the content was not the problem.
8. Measure non-customer traffic and returning visitors, not just totals, to confirm the trust problem moved.

**Pitfall:** Rebranding the wrapper while the content stays company announcements. Outside readers still bounce, and you see it as a new name with the same monthly traffic. Also do not hide ownership entirely, which invites its own trust problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog carries heavy product framing. David does not need a rename, but the blog templates and category structure should read as an aesthetics practice-management publication, with product content separated out, so a non-customer trusts it.

**Apply anywhere:** If outsiders read your blog as a company newsletter, rename it to a title about the reader's subject, remove product branding and company-news categories from the template, keep the archive and a small ownership disclosure, and time the relaunch to content that brings an outside audience.

### 15. Redefine good content as leads and sales before changing the calendar  `136.7`
*useful · best practices · source 136*

Grow and Convert describe the hardest part of the shift as internal, not tactical. Their definition of good content had been what is most enjoyable, impressive or fun to read, and they had real pride in producing story journalism rather than list posts. Changing to what gets the most leads and sales for the business met resistance inside their own team. What settled it was agreeing that their differentiator was producing leads and sales, and being willing to be held accountable to that number and to report it openly as a goal of every engagement. The order matters: they changed the success metric first, and the content calendar followed. A team that changes the calendar without changing the metric will judge the new bottom-funnel posts by how good they feel to read and quietly drift back.

> "what gets the most leads and sales for the business?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say the shift was met with internal resistance and that results, and their willingness to be held accountable to leads, are what won the argument.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the definition of good content your team is actually using today, including the aesthetic parts.
2. Replace it with a single measurable definition: leads or sales attributable to the piece.
3. Agree the attribution method and the reporting cadence before commissioning anything new.
4. Report leads per article openly to the client or leadership every month, including the misses.
5. When someone argues a piece is better content, ask what number that claim predicts.
6. Keep producing the formats your team is proud of only where they also earn leads, and say so explicitly.
7. Revisit the definition after two quarters with your own conversions-per-article data in hand.

**Pitfall:** Skipping the metric change and going straight to the new formats. The team keeps grading work on craft, resents the list posts, and the strategy erodes within a quarter.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content reporting should lead with demo bookings attributable to each article, published monthly, before any change to the mix of blog versus template pages. That number is what settles arguments about whether a comparison page is worth writing.

**Apply anywhere:** Change your definition of good content to leads or sales attributable to the piece, and agree the attribution and reporting cadence, before you change what you publish. Otherwise the team keeps grading on craft and drifts back.

### 16. Refuse to be first to a new technology while your process still works  `176.3`
*useful · best practices · source 176*

Grow and Convert use ChatGPT-for-content as their worked example of a shiny object. The popular thought was that teams needed to get on it before everyone else. Their answer is that being first is rarely an advantage and usually the opposite: while others spend their time testing and figuring out what works, you can spend that time doing what already works for you and getting results. If your process later breaks down, you can then test their strategy and skip the cost of figuring it out from scratch. This is a deliberate second-mover position, and it is cheap because the early adopters publish their findings.

> "Rarely is there an advantage to being the first to try something"

**How to do it**

1. When a new technology gets loud, ask one question: is my existing process broken?
2. If it is not, write the technology's name and the date into a watch list and stop there.
3. Set a standing hour each month to read what early adopters have published about it, without running your own test.
4. Keep full production capacity on the process that is working during that period.
5. Only when your failure condition trips, adopt the version of the tactic that early adopters have already debugged.
6. Skip any part of their workflow they report as a dead end, so you inherit the savings.

**Pitfall:** Second-mover discipline fails when a genuine platform shift is underway. Watch for your existing channel's output declining, not for competitor noise, as the trigger to move.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep a dated watch list for tactics like new AI-answer formats rather than rebuilding the content process around each one. Production capacity stays on bottom-of-funnel practice-management topics.

**Apply anywhere:** Log loud new technologies on a watch list instead of testing them. Read what early adopters publish, keep producing what works, and adopt only a debugged version once your own process falters.

### 17. Reject the assumption that you must educate the whole funnel yourself  `110.14`
*useful · general insights · source 110*

Grow and Convert name the fallacy behind top-funnel-first strategy. Marketers accept that every customer passes through awareness, consideration and conversion, then wrongly conclude that their company must be the one to educate every prospect at every stage starting at the top. Unless you are genuinely creating a new category, other companies and individuals have already educated a large share of your prospects on top-funnel topics. Those prospects already know why the category matters, who the big players are and how it helps. They are not at the top of the funnel, they do not need top-funnel content, and they are the best prospects you have. Grow and Convert call the resulting spend a huge waste of resources on content that does little to generate trials or demos.

> "Marketers think _their company_ needs to be the one to educate"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert argue that outside genuinely new categories, other companies and individuals have already educated a large number of prospects on top-funnel topics.

**How to do it**

1. List the top-funnel topics currently in your calendar.
2. Search each one and note which established sites already rank and how thoroughly they cover it.
3. Mark any topic where three or more credible sources already educate the market as already covered.
4. Reallocate that budget to product-intent and pain-point topics.
5. Assume the prospect arriving on your site already knows the category exists and write accordingly.
6. Revisit only if you find a real gap where nobody in the market has explained something.

**Pitfall:** Building an awareness programme for a market that is already aware. The signal is that page one of your awareness keywords is full of well-established explainers from bigger sites.

**Apply at Pabau:** Practice owners already know practice management software exists and who the main vendors are. Pabau does not need to fund category education on pabau.com and should assume an informed reader who wants comparison and switching detail instead.

**Apply anywhere:** Stop assuming your company must educate prospects at every funnel stage. Check who already ranks for your awareness topics; where the market is already educated, move that budget to product-intent and pain-point content and write for an informed reader.

### 18. Replace absolute traffic goals with month-over-month self-comparison  `141.16`
*useful · best practices · source 141*

Grow and Convert argue you cannot predict traffic before starting. They put two of their own clients side by side to prove it: the B2B subscription business leveled off near 3,500 pageviews a month while the B2C concussion treatment center produced over 8,000 visitors a month on a handful of articles. They say they could not have predicted the second result, and that while general trends exist, such as B2C usually out-trafficking B2B, no a priori number is reliable. Their conclusion is that the strategy matters, not the arbitrary traffic goal. The replacement they propose for early-stage measurement is a two-part test: in the beginning, look for indications the strategy is working by seeing a few conversions arrive, then over time compete with yourself month on month to see traffic and conversions increasing.

> "It's the strategy that's important, not setting arbitrary traffic goals"

**Evidence:** Two Grow and Convert clients run on similar effort produced roughly 3,500 pageviews a month (B2B subscription) and over 8,000 visitors a month (B2C concussion center); they say the second was unpredictable in advance.

**How to do it**

1. Refuse to set an absolute monthly traffic target at kickoff for a new content program.
2. Set the month 1 to 3 success test as a handful of conversions arriving from any source, proving the topic selection converts.
3. From month 4 onward, set the target as beating the prior month on both traffic and conversions.
4. Keep traffic charts scoped to the articles you published, starting from zero, so comparisons are like for like.
5. Note the business model in the forecast: B2C usually generates more traffic than B2B for equivalent effort.
6. If a stakeholder demands a number, give a range with the reasoning and re-forecast after three months of real data.
7. Review the strategy, not the target, whenever a month misses.

**Pitfall:** Committing to a traffic number before any data exists forces the team toward high-volume head terms that generate sessions and no signups, which is the exact failure the bottom-funnel strategy exists to avoid.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content plan should be reviewed on demo requests per month and month-over-month growth of the pages published in that period, not against a fixed sessions target set before the first article ships.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not set an absolute traffic goal before you have data. Judge the first three months on whether a few conversions arrive at all, then compete against your own previous month on traffic and conversions. Keep charts scoped to the articles you published.

### 19. Replace ungated top-funnel guides with stories that sell the product  `91.17`
*useful · concrete actions · source 91*

Grow and Convert draw a hard line between disruption stories and what they call top-of-funnel content, the whitepapers, ebooks and guides many brands create. The distinction is not format or length, it is whether the piece sells. Top-funnel assets do not directly sell the product or service. Disruption stories do sell it, and in fact are entirely about the product and why it was created. Their claim is that this is what lets a shareable, non-search piece also convert, which is the combination most top-funnel content never achieves. The practical implication is a substitution rather than an addition. If you are already spending budget producing beginner guides for social distribution, that budget produces more when it goes into pieces built around your own product's reason for existing.

> "which don't directly sell the product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated contrast between disruption stories and the whitepapers, ebooks and guides brands create, based on dozens of client stories over six years.

**How to do it**

1. Audit the ungated top-funnel assets you produce for social and paid distribution.
2. For each, ask whether the piece ever states what your product does and why it exists.
3. Stop producing the ones that do not, and reallocate that budget to disruption stories.
4. Rebuild the strongest existing guide around your product's founding pain instead of the category overview.
5. Keep the same distribution channels, paid and organic social, since the audience is unchanged.
6. Compare conversions per piece before and after the substitution, at the landing-page level.
7. Retain genuinely useful reference guides only where sales actually sends them.

**Pitfall:** Marketing teams keep both, so the story competes for budget with assets that never converted. If the guide has produced no attributable leads, cut it rather than sitting it alongside.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's non-keyword content should be positioning pieces about why Pabau exists, not generic guides to running an aesthetic practice. The generic guides only earn their place when they target real search demand on /blog/.

**Apply anywhere:** If you produce ungated guides and ebooks for social distribution, check whether any of them sell your product. Move that budget into pieces built around why your product exists, since those can be shared and convert at the same time.

### 20. Require a process to work across every client type before adopting it  `176.5`
*useful · best practices · source 176*

Grow and Convert set an explicit constraint on their method search: they had clients spanning B2B, B2C, SaaS, service and ecommerce, so whatever process they used had to work across all of them. That constraint is what killed the interview-story approach and what made bottom-of-funnel SEO attractive, since pain-point keywords exist in every business model. For an agency or an in-house team serving several product lines, this is a useful filter to apply before a method is scaled: a tactic that only works for one segment creates a second process to maintain and a second set of results to explain.

> "whatever process we used had to work across all of them"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's cross-model constraint (B2B, B2C, SaaS, service, ecommerce) is what pushed them from interview stories to bottom-of-funnel SEO.

**How to do it**

1. List every business type or product line the content process has to serve.
2. For each candidate method, ask whether it produces the same outcome in the hardest of those segments, not the easiest.
3. Run the candidate on one piece in the hardest segment before scaling it anywhere.
4. Reject any method that needs a materially different workflow per segment.
5. Document the surviving method once, with segment-specific inputs rather than segment-specific processes.

**Pitfall:** A method that only works in your best-performing segment looks like a win in aggregate reporting while quietly failing elsewhere. Break results down by segment before scaling.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau serves aesthetics, dermatology, dental and wider healthcare practices. Any new content route should be tested on the hardest of those verticals first, not on the one with the most existing traffic.

**Apply anywhere:** Before scaling a content method, confirm it works in the hardest segment you serve, not just the easiest. Reject anything that needs a different workflow per segment.

### 21. Research which sites and people your buyer trusts, not just their pain  `161.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 161*

Khanal's definition of knowing your customer goes past demographics and pain points. He lists what you should actually be able to answer: the biggest challenge relating to your product, the questions prospects have before they know they need you, what they research once they have the need, what sites they read to educate themselves about your industry, and who they go to for advice, whether that is Google, a friend, an influencer, a magazine, a book or television. He also splits competition into direct and indirect. For a search team those last two items are the channel and link plan. The sites they read are your guest post and digital PR target list, and the people they trust are your partnership and podcast list.

> "Knowing what sites they read to educate themselves"

**Evidence:** Khanal lists the sites customers read and the people they turn to for advice as two of the seven things knowing your customer actually means.

**How to do it**

1. Add two questions to every interview: which sites do you read about this industry, and who do you go to for advice on tools like this.
2. Log every named site, publication, community, podcast and person verbatim.
3. Count mentions and keep anything named by two or more customers.
4. Check each named site for whether it accepts contributed articles, runs a vendor directory, or publishes roundups.
5. Turn the named sites into a digital PR and guest posting target list, ranked by mention count rather than by DR.
6. Turn the named people into a podcast, webinar and partnership outreach list.
7. Ask separately what they research once they know they have the need, and treat those phrases as keyword candidates regardless of reported volume.
8. Ask what they would do if your whole category did not exist, to surface indirect competition.

**Pitfall:** Building the outreach target list from a backlink tool instead of from customer answers. You end up pitching sites Google rates highly but your buyer has never opened, which produces links that pass no relevant audience.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should ask aesthetic and dermatology practices which industry publications, Facebook groups and trainers they follow, then aim guest content and digital PR at those named outlets rather than at generic healthcare blogs.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask customers which sites they read and who they ask for advice, then build your PR, guest posting and partnership target lists from the names that repeat.

### 22. Reserve traffic-first SEO for incumbents who already hold market share  `179.18`
*useful · general insights · source 179*

Grow and Convert are precise about when the traffic-first approach they criticize is actually appropriate, which makes the criticism usable rather than dogmatic. Chasing high-volume, loosely related keywords can make sense for increasing online presence or general brand awareness, and they say those are typically the goals of large incumbents who already hold significant market share. It is a bad fit for companies that want tangible revenue or business goals from an SEO investment, meaning measurable leads and sales. The distinction matters when reading agency case studies and competitor strategies, because a large incumbent publishing broad advice content is not evidence that the approach will work for a challenger. It also gives a buyer a clean way to state their own position in a sales call: name whether you are buying awareness or buying leads, because the two imply different keyword strategies.

> "typically goals for large incumbents who already have"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert measured low-to-zero buying intent, and correspondingly low conversion rates, on general advice keywords across dozens of clients.

**Pitfall:** Copying the content strategy of the biggest brand in your category. Their broad, high-volume topics are an awareness play funded by existing market share, and reproducing it as a challenger buys traffic that does not convert.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau is not the incumbent in most of its search categories, so broad aesthetics and clinic-advice topics on pabau.com should stay a minority of output behind category, comparison and pain point pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Traffic-first SEO on broad, high-volume topics is an awareness play that suits large incumbents with existing market share. If you need measurable leads and sales, it is the wrong strategy, and copying an incumbent competitor's content plan will buy traffic that does not convert. State up front whether you are buying awareness or buying leads, because the two imply different keyword lists.

### 23. Reuse the disruption story as the sales team's reference article  `91.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 91*

Grow and Convert list a secondary benefit that changes how the piece is used after publication. Because a disruption story clearly outlines the most fundamental pain points the business was created to solve and how you solve them, it becomes a sales tool and a reference article. Sales can send it repeatedly, and every other blog post or social post that touches the main value proposition can link to it rather than re-arguing the position from scratch. That makes it a hub: one canonical URL that carries the positioning, referenced from the keyword-driven pages that carry the search traffic. It also means the writing standard is higher than a normal blog post, because the piece is expected to be sent to prospects for years rather than read once from a SERP.

> "a sales tool and reference article for other blog posts"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own stated use of their Pain Point SEO, Mirage Content, Specificity Strategy and Content Brand articles as repeated lead sources and reference reads.

**How to do it**

1. Publish the disruption story at a stable URL you do not plan to change.
2. Send it to the sales team with a one-line note on when to use it, typically after a first call.
3. Add it to your sales sequences and proposal templates as the positioning read.
4. Add an internal link to it from every existing blog post that references your value proposition.
5. Make it the default link in social posts about your positioning, rather than restating the argument.
6. Add a contextual internal link to it from your highest-traffic bottom-of-funnel pages.
7. Refresh it whenever the positioning changes, and update the value-prop sentence everywhere at the same time.

**Pitfall:** If it lives only as a social ad destination, sales never uses it and other posts keep re-explaining the positioning inconsistently. Tell the sales team it exists on the day it publishes.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat its positioning article as the internal-link hub for value-prop mentions across /blog/ and /templates/, so the many articles that mention practice management software like Pabau point to one canonical explanation instead of each making their own case.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat the positioning story as a permanent hub: give sales the link, then internally link to it from every post that references your value proposition instead of re-arguing it each time.

### 24. Revenue objective (ROI vs. growth) decides SEO vs. paid  `29.8`
*useful · general insights · source 29*

While running SEO at like.com, Hank observed that the metric leadership chose to optimize for completely changed which channel 'won' internally. When the team optimized purely for revenue, paid search always won because it could be run as an arbitrage - spend enough to generate any positive margin at any scale. But when the target metric shifted to ROAS or ROI, SEO became the far stronger lever, since it doesn't carry a comparable incremental cost basis. This played out live during like.com's eventual acquisition process (it sold to Google in 2010), where the growth story leadership wanted to tell shifted week to week depending on who they were pitching.

> "distinct revenue objectives shape the strategy you run"

**Evidence:** At like.com, when the internal metric was pure revenue, paid search always won because it could be run as an arbitrage at any scale; when the metric shifted to ROAS/ROI (which happened repeatedly during the company's acquisition process before its 2010 sale to Google), SEO became the stronger lever since it lacks a comparable incremental spend cost.

**Apply at Pabau:** When building the internal case for SEO investment at Pabau, find out whether leadership is currently optimizing for raw growth or for ROI/efficiency - lead with the ROI framing, since that's the metric under which SEO structurally wins the internal budget argument against paid.

**Apply anywhere:** When building the internal case for SEO investment on your team, find out whether leadership is currently optimizing for raw growth or for ROI/efficiency - lead with the ROI framing, since that's the metric under which SEO structurally wins the internal budget argument against paid.

### 25. Run six positioning questions on a fixed schedule, not once  `169.17`
*useful · best practices · source 169*

Hyam ends with a standing review rather than a one-time exercise, saying you should continually ask yourself six questions to strengthen positioning against competitors: what problem can my product or service solve better than anyone else, what do my customers want it to do that no one else is doing, what do my customers think would be better than everything else out there, what pain points do my customers want me to solve that I am not solving currently, does my promise to customers live up to what I actually offer, and how can I get better in the eyes of my customers. Two of those are self-audits of over-promising and of unserved pain, which is where positioning usually decays. He pairs them with the point that Grow and Convert itself went through four pivots to arrive at what works, so the answers are expected to change.

> "you should continually ask yourself"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert went through four pivots to reach the positioning it now uses, and four failed product attempts before that.

**How to do it**

1. Put a recurring quarterly slot in the calendar for the positioning review with the founder and the person who owns messaging.
2. Answer all six questions in writing, sourcing answers from recent customer calls rather than from opinion.
3. For the unserved pain point question, pull the actual asks from support tickets and sales call notes.
4. For the promise question, read the money pages aloud against what delivery currently does and flag every gap.
5. Mark any answer that has changed since the last review and note what caused the change.
6. Turn each changed answer into one concrete edit to the sales page, the pitch or the roadmap.
7. Ship those edits before the next review so the exercise stays operational.
8. Keep the full history so you can see the pivots, as Hyam counts four of them.

**Pitfall:** Running it as a workshop with no output. Without a rule that every changed answer becomes one shipped edit, the review produces a document nobody reads and the site keeps its old positioning.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run these six questions quarterly against Pabau's homepage, pricing page and top comparison templates, and treat any unserved pain point that keeps appearing in support tickets as a content and roadmap item.

**Apply anywhere:** Review positioning on a schedule with six questions: what you solve better than anyone, what customers want that nobody does, what they think would beat the alternatives, which pain points you still ignore, whether your promise matches delivery, and how to improve in their eyes. Ship one edit per changed answer.
