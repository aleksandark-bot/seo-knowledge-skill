### 1. Benchmark AI visibility against the established and new-domain bands  `83.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 83*

Grow and Convert publish the two bands they see across clients, which gives an outside benchmark most teams lack. Clients with established brands and years of solid SEO presence show visibility around 59% on ChatGPT, 71% on Perplexity, 78% on AI Overviews and 85% on Gemini. Younger clients with newer domains and no long content or SEO history show roughly 16% on ChatGPT, 13% on Perplexity, 24% on AI Overviews and 8% on Gemini. Devesh says that gap matters, and it is the reason he does not think measurement is hopeless despite prompts being invisible. Use the bands to decide whether your number is bad or simply normal for your age, and to set a realistic trajectory rather than an arbitrary target.

> "clients with established brands and years of rock-solid SEO presence"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client data: established brand at 59% ChatGPT, 71% Perplexity, 78% AI Overviews, 85% Gemini; new domain at 16% ChatGPT, 13% Perplexity, 24% AI Overviews, 8% Gemini.

**How to do it**

1. Score your visibility separately on ChatGPT, Perplexity, AI Overviews and Gemini, since the bands differ sharply per engine.
2. Classify your own site as established or new by whether it has years of SEO presence in the category.
3. Compare each engine's score to the matching band: roughly 59/71/78/85 for established, 16/13/24/8 for new.
4. If you are new and near the low band, treat the number as expected and set the next milestone at the midpoint rather than at the established band.
5. If you are established and sitting in the low band, treat it as a content coverage problem, not a domain age problem.
6. Re-score on a fixed monthly cadence so the trend, not the absolute number, is the reported metric.
7. Report the per-engine spread to stakeholders, since a low ChatGPT score alongside a high AI Overviews score is a different problem from being low everywhere.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Traqer

**Pitfall:** Averaging the four engines into one visibility percentage hides the pattern. Gemini swings from 8% to 85% between the bands, so an average masks exactly the signal you need.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has years of SEO presence in practice management, so a ChatGPT score near 16% would signal a coverage problem rather than a young-domain problem. Score each engine separately and set the target against the established band.

**Apply anywhere:** Score AI visibility per engine and compare against the two published bands: roughly 59/71/78/85 for an established brand and 16/13/24/8 for a new domain. Judge your number against your site's age before setting a target.

### 2. Brand mentions are overtaking backlinks for AI answer visibility  `35.1`
*core · content insights · source 35*

Charles argues that once you're explicitly trying to get AI overviews, AI mode, or ChatGPT to mention your brand, backlinks matter noticeably less than before, because these systems don't need to weight physical links the way classic PageRank does — they can pull mentions from training data and from live retrieval instead. He frames this as links being 'slightly devalued' while mentions have 'gone from very little value to suddenly being massively increased,' with brand mentions filling the vacuum links used to occupy. This does not mean links are worthless, but it means a mention on a trusted source (even without a hyperlink) can now carry AI-visibility weight that used to require an actual backlink.

> "Mentions have gone from very little value to suddenly being massively increased"

**Evidence:** Direct claim from a 15-year SEO practitioner and link-building marketplace founder: the algorithm powering AI retrieval 'doesn't need to weight those physical links — it can take mentions into account based on its training data, and it will also take mentions into account based on retrieval.'

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should track unlinked brand mentions (via tools like Ahrefs' mentions/brand alerts or manual site: searches) as a KPI alongside backlinks, and weight digital PR outreach toward getting the brand named and discussed — not only hyperlinked — across trusted industry sources.

**Apply anywhere:** You should track unlinked brand mentions (via tools like Ahrefs' mentions/brand alerts or manual site: searches) as a KPI alongside backlinks, and weight digital PR outreach toward getting the brand named and discussed — not only hyperlinked — across trusted industry sources.

### 3. Budget for paid G2 listings and trade mentions with competitors  `82.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 82*

Grow and Convert describe Tier 2 of their Prioritized GEO strategy, citation outreach, and are blunt about how the placements are actually obtained. You identify the sources AI systems reference most for a topic, then approach those sites for a brand mention. Review platforms like G2 or Capterra generally require payment, while competitors are often willing to trade mentions with you. That is the commercial reality behind the tactic and it changes how you budget: some of the highest-value citation sources are a media buy, not an earned link, and some are a swap negotiated with a company you compete against. The underlying logic is the same as Tier 1 owned content. When the model searches, it sees your brand across several sources rather than only your own site, which raises the chance it names you prominently.

> "Sites like G2 or Capterra generally require payment, while competitors"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated Tier 2 process, with G2 and Capterra named as paid and competitors named as trade partners.

**How to do it**

1. Run your topic's prompt basket and log every cited domain, ranked by how many prompts it appears in.
2. Split the list into three buckets: paid review platforms, competitor-owned content, and independent trade publications.
3. For the paid bucket, get quotes from G2 and Capterra and compare cost against how many of your tracked prompts each cites.
4. For the competitor bucket, offer a reciprocal mention in one of your own comparison articles as the trade.
5. For the independent bucket, pitch the editor of the specific cited page with your segment and a differentiator.
6. Send your own written product snippet with every request so the wording is yours.
7. Re-run the prompt basket 30 days after each placement lands and record any change in mentions.
8. Drop any source that gets cited for none of your tracked prompts, however large its traffic.

**Tools:** G2, Capterra, Traqer

**Pitfall:** Asking every site in your industry for mentions wastes the budget. Grow and Convert say to focus only on articles LLMs already cite for your prompts.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should price up listings on the review platforms cited for practice management prompts and treat that as a GEO line item, not a review-site expense. Competitor swaps are also available through Pabau's own comparison articles.

**Apply anywhere:** Bucket your cited domains into paid review platforms, competitors open to a swap, and independent trade sites, then pursue each with the method that fits. Skip any source not cited for your tracked prompts.

### 4. Build a GEO topic map from every product angle that matters  `78.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 78*

Grow and Convert's opening move for any GEO client is to enumerate the full universe of conversations where an LLM could recommend the brand. They list categories, named competitors, use cases, target personas, the jobs the product does and the pain points it solves. Each of those becomes a branch that needs its own piece of content. Their point is that the map is not a keyword list: it is a map of buying conversations, and most companies never produce content at that granularity because no keyword tool surfaces those angles. Source 12 covered the topic-map idea for case studies; this source names the six specific axes to enumerate and makes the map the planning artifact for the whole content program, not just the case-study library.

> "We map every product angle that matters"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use this as step one for every GEO client, including Constitution Lending and InnovationCast.

**How to do it**

1. Open a spreadsheet with one row per branch and columns for angle type, customer type, pain point and the content asset that will cover it.
2. Fill six angle types: product categories you sit in, named competitors, use cases, target personas, jobs the product does, pain points it solves.
3. For each row write the specific scenario in the customer's words, not the category label.
4. Cross-reference the map against your published URLs and mark every branch with no dedicated page.
5. Rank empty branches by how close they sit to a purchase decision and work down from the closest.
6. Re-run the enumeration each quarter with new competitors and new use cases added.
7. Track coverage as a percentage of branches with a live page, and report that number rather than page count.

**Pitfall:** Teams build the map from keyword tools, so it only contains angles that already have search volume. The signal you have hit this is a map where every branch maps to a keyword, which means the specific scenarios that only exist in sales calls are missing.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should build a topic map for Pabau across aesthetic and healthcare practice types, named competitors, and the jobs the software does. Rows like 'single-room injector switching from paper notes' or 'multi-site med spa needing consolidated reporting' become blog and template briefs.

**Apply anywhere:** Build a spreadsheet of every angle where an LLM could plausibly recommend you: categories, competitors, use cases, personas, jobs and pain points. Treat empty rows as your content backlog and report coverage as a percentage of branches filled.

### 5. Build product-centric depth instead of targeting individual AI prompts  `87.4`
*core · best practices · source 87*

Grow and Convert's conclusion from the study is that a content strategy built around ranking for specific prompts or fan-out queries is impractical. You cannot see the true prompts users enter, the fan-out queries differ run to run, ChatGPT does not always search the web, and even when it does only about 40% of citations trace back to those SERPs. Their recommendation is to identify your product's genuine strengths, find Google keywords that map to those strengths, and write detailed content for those keywords. They explicitly reject the tactic layer they have criticized before: FAQ pages, bullet-point summaries and llms.txt files. The mechanism they credit instead is being available for training data and building domain-level topical authority.

> "identify your strengths and find Google keywords"

**Evidence:** Toro TMS, a young brand with no prior content marketing, saw ChatGPT visibility rise after publishing product-centric content for real Google keywords.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the three or four things your product genuinely does better than the alternatives.
2. For each strength, find the Google keywords buyers use when they want that thing, using normal keyword research.
3. Check each keyword has commercial intent, not informational drift.
4. Write thorough, product-centric pages for those keywords covering use cases, features and limits.
5. Put your own product first on any listicle you publish, as Grow and Convert recommend.
6. Skip the tactic layer: do not add llms.txt, bullet summaries or FAQ pages expecting citation gains.
7. Measure at the topic level over months, not per prompt.

**Pitfall:** Chasing prompt-level wins produces a scatter of thin pages that neither rank nor get cited. The signal is a growing page count with flat citation share.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's plan should map to what Pabau actually does well for aesthetic and healthcare practices, then find the keywords for those, rather than reverse-engineering ChatGPT prompts about practice management.

**Apply anywhere:** Map your content plan to your product's real strengths, find the Google keywords for those, and write thorough pages. Do not reverse-engineer AI prompts.

### 6. Build your cited-domain list from your own prompts, not published charts  `84.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 84*

Grow & Convert's whole argument is that the widely shared 'top cited domains by AI' infographics are useless for a specific brand. Those charts aggregate citations across thousands of random keywords - one they name is built on 150,000 citations from 5,000 randomly selected Semrush keywords - so they are dominated by general consumer queries like 'best washing machine' or 'restaurants in Chicago'. That is why Reddit, Wikipedia and Forbes top them. For a product-intent prompt in a defined vertical, the cited set is almost entirely industry domains. Their instruction is to stop labelling yourself niche or general, B2B or B2C, and instead start from the prompts that matter to your brand and read the actual source list each engine returns for those prompts.

> "don't waste time trying to label yourself as "niche" or "general,"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert analyzed 120 prompts across five clients and 2,440 cited domains; 86% of citations were industry or industry-adjacent, only 14% general.

**How to do it**

1. List the product-intent prompts a buyer would actually type, such as 'best dispatch software for trucking companies' or 'alternatives to Asana'.
2. Run each prompt in ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overview and log every cited URL and domain.
3. Group the domains into direct vendor, industry-adjacent publication, and general site.
4. Count citation frequency per domain across the whole prompt set, not per single prompt.
5. Rank the domains by how many of your prompts they appear in.
6. Take the top recurring domains as your off-site mention target list.
7. Ignore any published 'most cited domains' chart that was built from random keyword samples.
8. Re-run the prompt set quarterly, since the cited set moves with the underlying rankings.

**Tools:** Traqer, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overview

**Pitfall:** Checking one prompt once gives a sample too small to act on, because LLM citation sets are resampled per call. Build the list from a topic's worth of prompts.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run Pabau's real buyer prompts, like 'best practice management software for a medspa', through the three engines and record which domains get cited. That list, not a generic Reddit-and-Forbes chart, decides where Pabau chases mentions.

**Apply anywhere:** Run your real buyer prompts through ChatGPT, Perplexity and AI Overviews, log every cited domain, and let that list decide where you chase mentions. Generic 'top cited domains' charts are built from random keyword samples and will not match your category.

### 7. Category niche-ness, not B2B or funnel stage, decides general-site citations  `84.4`
*core · content insights · source 84*

Grow & Convert tested the obvious explanations and both failed. Funnel stage barely moved the number: general sites made up about 18% of citations on top-of-funnel prompts, about 11% mid-funnel and about 14% bottom-of-funnel. The B2C explanation also failed, because Climb Hire is B2C and still saw only about 15% general citations, close to the B2B clients. What separated them was Peloton, a mass-market consumer brand, where general sites made up just under 60% of citations from 542 domains across 20 prompts, industry-adjacent about 34%, and industry-specific only about 6%. Their conclusion is that niche verticals have niche content ecosystems, so vendors and trade publications are the only sources covering the topic, while mass-market categories are dominated by Wirecutter, PCMag, CNET, YouTube and Reddit.

> "it's the niche-ness of the category that determines which domains LLMs cite"

**Evidence:** Peloton: 542 domains across 20 prompts, just under 60% general, ~34% adjacent, ~6% industry. Climb Hire, B2C but niche: ~15% general.

**Tools:** Traqer

**Pitfall:** Applying a mass-market GEO playbook to a niche vertical wastes budget on Reddit and mainstream placements that supply a seventh of citations there, and the reverse fails too.

**Apply at Pabau:** Aesthetic practice management is a niche vertical, so Pabau should expect a Climb Hire style split. Mainstream press placements are a brand play, not a GEO play, and should be budgeted separately.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge your category by how niche it is, not by B2B versus B2C or funnel stage. Niche verticals cite vendors and trade press; mass-market categories cite Wirecutter, CNET, Reddit and YouTube.

### 8. ChatGPT repeats your article's exact value props back to buyers  `79.7`
*core · content insights · source 79*

Grow & Convert's clearest piece of evidence for careful positioning is client Constitution Lending, a private lender for real estate investors. Their differentiator is fast closings and high loan-to-value ratios, and the agency writes those into every article they produce for them. The mechanism this demonstrates is that how you position the product in the foundational article is how the model positions it to its user. Vague positioning gets summarized vaguely; specific numbers survive the summarization intact.

> "it cites the exact value props and features we state"

**Evidence:** Constitution Lending recommended by ChatGPT for 'fastest closing, highest LTV DSCR lenders USA', with the article cited and its 80% LTV, quick pre-approval and four-day closing claims repeated verbatim.

### 9. Classify every cited domain as industry, adjacent or general before acting  `84.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 84*

The taxonomy Grow & Convert used is simple enough to copy and it is what makes the citation data actionable. Industry means direct vendors or companies selling the product or service being discussed, such as wrike.com in a project management query. Industry-adjacent means publications, blogs or platforms in the same sector that do not sell the product, such as Transport Topics in a trucking software query. General means domains with little or no connection to the industry: mainstream outlets like Forbes and Wikipedia, community platforms like Reddit, Quora and YouTube, and occasional off-topic sources. Splitting your citation log this way tells you immediately whether your category is served by an industry ecosystem or by mass-market publishers, which is the thing that determines where mentions are worth buying or earning.

> "Domains with little or no direct connection to the industry"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert applied exactly this three-way classification to 2,440 domains across five client verticals.

**How to do it**

1. Export every cited domain from your prompt run into a single sheet, one row per citation.
2. Tag each domain 'industry' if the site sells the product or service being discussed.
3. Tag it 'industry-adjacent' if it publishes about the sector but sells nothing in it.
4. Tag everything else 'general', including Reddit, Quora, YouTube, Wikipedia and mainstream news.
5. Compute the percentage split across the three buckets for your whole prompt set.
6. If general is under about 20%, direct off-site effort at trade publications and comparison vendors.
7. If general is over about half, direct it at the mainstream review publishers ranking for those queries.
8. Repeat the split per funnel stage to see whether the mix shifts at the top of funnel.

**Tools:** Traqer

**Pitfall:** Tagging by brand familiarity instead of by whether the site sells the product blurs the industry and adjacent buckets and makes the split unusable.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau logs cited domains, treat competing practice management vendors as 'industry', aesthetic trade press as 'adjacent' and anything else as 'general'. The split tells David whether to invest in trade press or consumer publishers.

**Apply anywhere:** Tag every cited domain as direct vendor, sector publication, or general site, then compute the split. That ratio tells you whether trade press or mainstream publishers is the right off-site target.

### 10. Claude, ChatGPT, and AI Overviews need different citation strategies  `10.9`
*core · content insights · source 10*

Ranking in classic Google search and "ranking" inside an AI model's answers are two increasingly separate goals, because each AI model verifies claims differently. Google's AI Overviews has the loosest verification and can be tricked with fake information all day long, making it the easiest of the three to manipulate; ChatGPT sits in the middle. Claude is the most skeptical of product recommendations specifically — Charles says this skepticism is written into Claude's own system prompt — and is hardest to trick, especially on newer or less-established domains. Because of these differences, content and even the hosting domain should be structured per target AI: ChatGPT citations often just need blogs, press releases, and news-site placements, while earning Claude citations needs more traditional reference-style sites, wiki-style formatting, brand-level authority pages, and evidence of an actively maintained product such as changelogs, product-update pages, and developer documentation, because that reads as trusted, verifiable material to Claude's more skeptical evaluation.

> "is way more skeptical of product recommendations"

**Evidence:** Charles states Claude's product-recommendation skepticism is explicitly written into its system prompt, and contrasts it with AI Overviews ("you can trick AI Overviews with fake information all day long") and ChatGPT, which he places "somewhere in the middle."

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau's GEO strategy, don't treat all AI answer engines the same: winning Claude citations for Pabau as a product likely requires genuinely trustworthy reference material (a public changelog, developer or API docs, transparent product pages, third-party wiki-style profiles) rather than just more blog posts and PR placements aimed at easier targets like AI Overviews.

**Apply anywhere:** For your GEO strategy, don't treat all AI answer engines the same: winning Claude citations for your product likely requires genuinely trustworthy reference material (a public changelog, developer or API docs, transparent product pages, third-party wiki-style profiles) rather than just more blog posts and PR placements aimed at easier targets like AI Overviews.

### 11. Coin a named framework and repeat it consistently to earn AI citations  `19.5`
*core · best practices · source 19*

Rather than chasing every LLM-optimization tactic, such as schema markup or special files, which Kaleigh says people "get lost in the sauce" on, her actual strategy is to talk about one specific topic using the same specific, signature language over and over, inventing a named framework, her own example being "the source signal stack," that becomes consistent terminology for that topic. She pairs this with spotting an emerging topic or behavior shift early, drawing on her seven years as a journalist to sense what's new, and combining the named framework with that emerging trend, which she calls "rocket fuel" for earning citations, because it makes you the identifiable, named expert on that one specific thing rather than a generic voice repeating common industry terms. She frames this as the next evolution of SEO, layered on top of, not replacing, traditional keyword and competitor work, precisely because AI has made it trivially cheap to produce generic content, raising the value of genuinely distinctive framing.

> "talk about one thing with very specific language over and over"

**How to do it**

1. Pick one specific topic or angle you want to own as the identifiable expert on, narrow enough that a generic industry term doesn't already cover it.
2. Invent a distinct, ownable name for your point of view or framework on that topic, a two-to-four word named term such as "source signal stack," rather than reusing existing industry jargon.
3. Build a simple visual model or diagram to accompany the named framework so it's easy for others to explain and reference.
4. Look for an emerging trend, behavior shift, or new development in your industry that you can pair the framework with, to make it feel current rather than abstract.
5. Use the exact same named-framework terminology consistently across every piece of content, posts, articles, and talks, for at least several months rather than varying your phrasing.
6. Track whether the term starts getting picked up or referenced by others as a sign LLMs and readers are associating it with you specifically (inferred verification signal).
7. Keep doing normal keyword and competitor research in parallel, since the named-framework approach is a layer on top of standard SEO practice, not a replacement for it.

**Pitfall:** Getting "lost in the sauce" chasing every individual LLM or AEO tactic, such as schema or specific file formats, instead of committing to one consistent, ownable point of view is the most common failure mode she sees.

### 12. Collect real customer prompts and track those, not keyword strings  `80.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 80*

Grow and Convert asked a new client to share the actual ChatGPT conversation in which the agency was mentioned. The prompt was not a keyword. It was a full sentence carrying the industry, the outcome wanted and an opinion request. The response opened by naming the client's business type back to her and listing high-intent keywords for it before recommending anyone, which shows how much of the answer is driven by context the user supplied rather than by the nominal search term. The lesson is operational: ask customers for their conversations, and build the tracked prompt set out of that real language instead of inventing tidy keyword strings.

> "What are the best SEO companies in your opinion"

**Evidence:** A Grow and Convert client's real ChatGPT prompt, shared verbatim, produced an answer that named her business funding vertical and recommended the agency first.

**How to do it**

1. Add a question to onboarding or the first client call asking whether they used an AI tool and what exactly they typed.
2. Ask them to paste or screenshot the conversation, not to paraphrase it.
3. Store the verbatim prompts in a sheet with the customer's industry and situation.
4. Look for the context they volunteered that no keyword tool would have shown, such as business model or vertical.
5. Add those verbatim prompts to your weekly AI tracking set alongside your own invented ones.
6. Compare which set you win more often, and write content against the context terms that appear in the real prompts.
7. Refresh the collection every quarter, since prompt phrasing shifts as people get used to the tools.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What are the best SEO companies in your opinion who have driven massive results with endless leads for people in our kind of space?
```

**Pitfall:** Tracking neat keyword-shaped prompts you wrote yourself. They omit the situational context real users type, so your tracking looks healthy while you lose the conversations that actually produce leads.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should ask new signups which AI tool they used and what they typed, then add those exact sentences to the tracked prompt set. Clinic owners phrase things very differently from the keyword list.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask customers for the verbatim AI conversations that led them to you, and build your tracked prompt set from that real language rather than from invented keyword strings.

### 13. Common GEO tactics don't influence brand awareness at all  `12.2`

Devesh characterizes the prevailing online GEO advice - add FAQs, publish an llms.txt file, get your schema right, convert headings into questions, add bullet-point summaries - as all sharing one flaw: every one of these tactics is a tweak to existing content aimed at helping LLM crawlers better parse or digest a site that already exists, not a way to influence whether an LLM knows about or associates a brand with anything in the first place. > "None of it is about how to actually influence"

**Evidence:** Devesh's critique of the prevailing online GEO narrative (FAQs, llms.txt, schema fixes, question-format headings, bullet-point summaries), noting people recommend llms.txt even in posts admitting 'no major LLM vendor currently consumes this in their crawler index pipelines.'

### 14. Compare two adjacent topics' visibility to locate the content gap  `83.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 83*

Grow and Convert use their own data as the worked example. Their AI visibility for 'B2B content marketing services' and 'B2C content marketing services' looks completely different. Their process works on both and they have had clients in both, but they have had more B2B clients, written more about B2B challenges and published more B2B case studies. The industry, and by extension the LLMs, know them better in B2B. The conclusion is directly actionable: to win more B2C clients they must publish more content about their B2C work. This is the operational version of topic-based measurement. Prompts are invisible, but the difference between two neighbouring topics tells you exactly which body of content is thin.

> "looks completely different"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own visibility for B2B versus B2C content marketing services differs sharply, which they attribute to having more B2B clients, more B2B writing and more B2B case studies.

**How to do it**

1. Pick two adjacent topics you serve equally well, such as two customer segments or two product use cases.
2. Build a prompt basket of at least ten variants for each topic and run both across ChatGPT, Perplexity, AI Overviews and Gemini.
3. Score each basket as the share of prompts where your brand appears.
4. Where one topic scores far lower, count your published pages and case studies on each topic.
5. Confirm the visibility gap tracks the publishing gap before assuming anything else is wrong.
6. Commission case studies and articles specifically for the weaker topic, naming that segment explicitly.
7. Re-run both baskets monthly and watch whether the weaker score converges.
8. Repeat the comparison for the next pair of adjacent topics.

**Tools:** ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Traqer

**Pitfall:** Comparing topics you do not actually serve equally well produces a gap you should not close. Check that the weaker topic is a real commercial target before funding content for it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should run this comparison across its segments, for example aesthetics clinics versus physiotherapy or veterinary practices. If one segment scores far lower, the fix is case studies and pages for that segment rather than broader software content.

**Apply anywhere:** Run prompt baskets for two adjacent segments you serve equally well and compare the scores. A large gap usually tracks a publishing gap, so fund case studies and pages for the weaker segment and re-measure monthly.

### 15. Comparison listicles get picked up by LLMs more  `18.11`
*core · content insights · source 18*

Testing ChatGPT with 'who is the best SEO on Reddit' revealed it wasn't doing any independent evaluation — it pulled an existing Google results list, and 'sometimes only two of the results were even from Reddit,' meaning people who aren't even active on Reddit ended up on the 'best on Reddit' list because their content ranked elsewhere. This is offered as evidence that LLM answers for 'who/what is best' queries are really just repackaged SERP-list aggregation, reinforced by the observation that listicle rankings are themselves unstable across pages (page one might show 10 names, page two the same topic shows 10 with 8 different names, page three 7 different again), so no single list is authoritative. The recommended conclusion is that comparison-format content ('X vs Y vs Z', 'why is [X] the best') is more synthesizable by an LLM than a plain ranked list, because it gives the model verifiable, side-by-side facts to work from rather than requiring it to adjudicate a ranking itself.

> "makes it easier for LLMs to synthesize an answer"

**Evidence:** Asking ChatGPT 'who is the best SEO on Reddit' returned a list where 'sometimes only two of the results were even from Reddit,' showing the model repackaged a Google results list rather than truly assessing Reddit activity or expertise.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should format 'best X software' and competitor-comparison content as explicit head-to-head comparisons (Pabau vs. Competitor, feature-by-feature) rather than relying solely on ranked-listicle placement, since this format is what the source says LLMs can actually synthesize into a recommendation.

**Apply anywhere:** You should format 'best X software' and competitor-comparison content as explicit head-to-head comparisons (your site vs. Competitor, feature-by-feature) rather than relying solely on ranked-listicle placement, since this format is what the source says LLMs can actually synthesize into a recommendation.

### 16. Context seeding: make every mention say the same thing about you  `60.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 60*

This is the strongest single tactic in the episode. Joe's observation is that ChatGPT doesn't just list ten options like Google - it recommends a brand for a specific reason: this is the best CRM if you're a small business, this is the best if you want the most features, this is the cheapest. So it gathers context around the brand, not just the name. FATJOE's response is what they call context seeding: on every review or listicle they commission, they make sure the writer writes the specific context the brand wants to be known for. If the client is a CRM whose USP is having the most features, they want 10-20 reviews out there all saying this CRM has the most features. Repeat the USP enough and the AI will use that context as the basis of its recommendation. The second half of the argument makes it sharper: ChatGPT knows a great deal about the person asking - their chat history, whether they prefer expensive or cheap, whether they run a small business - so it matches the asker's profile against the context attached to brands. The related concept he names is brand salience: when you think of air fryers you think Ninja, when you think mobile phone you think iPhone - what is the thing your brand wants to be known for.

> "what we like to do is context seeding"

**How to do it**

1. Decide the single thing your brand should be recommended for - the context, not the category.
2. Write it as a short, repeatable phrase you can hand to any third-party writer.
3. Brief every commissioned review, listicle and mention to include that exact context, not just the brand name.
4. Aim for repetition across many sources - Joe's target is 10-20 reviews all saying the same thing.
5. Mix formats: some listicles, some standalone reviews, so it doesn't look like a single campaign.
6. In standalone reviews, include honest pros and cons and an explicit 'this is for you if / this is not for you if' - that's the context the engine reuses.
7. Test by asking the engines the qualified question ('best CRM for a small business') rather than the head term.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Joe deliberately mixes listicles with full reviews because if every mention is a listicle with your brand at number one, he assumes the model may treat that as suspicious - his words are that he mixes it up for his own sanity, but the diversification is sound either way.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau needs one agreed context phrase - the specific thing Pabau should be the recommendation for - and it should appear verbatim in every third-party mention, review and listicle, because that phrase is what the AI reuses when a clinic owner asks a qualified question.

**Apply anywhere:** Agree one context phrase - the specific thing you should be the recommendation for - and get it appearing verbatim in every third-party mention, review and listicle, because that phrase is what the AI reuses when someone asks a qualified question.

### 17. Count domain-level matches, not URL matches, when auditing AI citations  `87.2`
*core · content insights · source 87*

Grow and Convert re-ran their overlap analysis counting a match whenever any page on a cited domain ranked for the fan-out query, rather than requiring the exact cited URL to rank. The match rate jumped from 27% to about 50%. They read this as ChatGPT favoring domains it recognizes as topically authoritative even when it does not cite the page that ranks. The practical consequence is that a citation audit scored on exact URLs will understate how much your search presence is doing. Score both. A domain that ranks broadly across a topic gets cited on pages that never ranked for the specific query, which is the strongest argument in the study for depth of coverage over per-query targeting.

> "jumps from 27% to about 50%"

**Evidence:** Domain-match scoring took the overlap from 27% to ~50% across 81 prompts.

**How to do it**

1. Collect the cited URLs from 30 or more buying-intent prompts in the ChatGPT browser UI.
2. Capture the fan-out queries ChatGPT displays for each prompt.
3. Pull Google SERPs for each fan-out query with SerpAPI, going at least three pages deep.
4. Score each citation twice: once for exact-URL presence in the SERP, once for any URL on the same domain.
5. Report both rates side by side, and treat the gap between them as your domain-authority effect.
6. Where the domain rate is high but the URL rate is low, invest in topic coverage rather than in optimizing single pages.

**Tools:** SerpAPI, ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Reporting only the exact-URL rate makes AI visibility work look like it failed when the domain is in fact being recognized.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David audits Pabau's AI citations, score domain matches for pabau.com as well as exact URLs. A high domain rate says keep publishing across the aesthetic-practice topic; a low one says the domain is not yet recognized.

**Apply anywhere:** When auditing AI citations, score domain matches as well as exact-URL matches. The gap between the two tells you whether the engine recognizes your domain on a topic or only your individual pages.

### 18. Cut top-of-funnel prompts from your AI visibility target list  `180.3`
*core · best practices · source 180*

Grow and Convert make the funnel distinction explicit for prompt targeting. Top-of-funnel content does not drive traffic from AI tools, because users get their answer inside the tool and never click. So visibility on a top-of-funnel prompt is a vanity result. They treat the ability to discern between bottom and top of funnel prompts as the test of whether an agency's GEO plan is serious. This is consistent with their wider position that content should chase people already looking to buy, since for almost every software category there is meaningful volume of people actively searching to buy that type of product or to solve the pain it solves. They note this inverts the long-standing convention of starting top of funnel, capturing an email and dripping information until conversion.

> "users stay in the AI tool to get their answers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state plainly that top-of-funnel content does not drive traffic from AI tools because users stay in the tool to get their answers.

**How to do it**

1. List the prompts you want visibility on and label each one buy-stage or research-stage.
2. Delete any research-stage prompt whose answer a reader would accept inside the chat window and act on.
3. Keep prompts where the searcher must reach a vendor to proceed: pricing, comparisons, alternatives, integrations, 'best X for Y'.
4. Rank the surviving prompts by how close the asker is to a purchase decision, not by estimated prompt volume.
5. Build or refresh one owned page per surviving prompt, optimized to rank in classic search first.
6. Track referral sessions from AI engines per prompt cluster and drop clusters that produce impressions without visits.
7. Move genuinely top-of-funnel material to video, social and community rather than the blog.

**Pitfall:** Reporting AI citation counts on top-of-funnel prompts as a win. Impressions rise, referral traffic does not, and the program looks healthy while producing nothing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's AI visibility tracking should weight prompts like 'best practice management software for medspas' and pricing comparisons far above 'what is patient intake'. Drop the definitional prompts from the reporting basket.

**Apply anywhere:** Strip research-stage prompts out of your AI visibility targets. When the answer is complete inside the chat, a citation earns you nothing. Target prompts where the user still has to go and pick a vendor.

### 19. Do citation outreach to the domains cited for your prompts  `79.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 79*

Grow & Convert's headline replacement for generic Reddit marketing is what they call citation outreach. Rather than chasing sites that industry studies name as most-cited overall, you identify the specific domains and pages that get cited for the queries and prompts your brand targets, then pitch those publishers on why you belong in their list or article. They ran this for client Toro TMS in trucking management software: across five prompts on that topic, the most cited domains across ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overview were PCS Software, TruckingOffice, Connecteam, Wise Systems and Toro TMS itself. All trucking, transportation or dispatch sites. None were Reddit or Wikipedia. The same held for the far broader topic 'best project management software', where every top cited domain was a project management software site.

> "identifying the domains and pages that actually get cited"

**Evidence:** Toro TMS: five trucking management software prompts, top cited domains were PCS Software, TruckingOffice, Connecteam, Wise Systems and Toro TMS. Same pattern in 'best project management software'.

**How to do it**

1. Pick one bottom-of-funnel topic and write five prompt phrasings a buyer would actually type.
2. Run all five through ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overview and log every cited domain and URL.
3. Aggregate the citations and rank domains by how many of the five prompts they appear in.
4. Drop domains you cannot influence, then keep the listicles, roundups and comparison pages you could be added to.
5. Find the author or editor of each specific page, not a generic contact address.
6. Pitch inclusion with the concrete reason you fit that list: the segment you serve, a differentiator, a number.
7. Re-run the same five prompts monthly to see whether new citations changed the answer.
8. Ignore published charts of most-cited-domains-overall unless the underlying keywords match your category.

**Tools:** Traqer.ai, ChatGPT, Perplexity

**Pitfall:** Studies that rank citation sources are built on random keyword samples. One they quote used 150,000 citations from 5,000 randomly selected Semrush keywords, which says nothing about your category and pushes budget to Reddit that belongs on trade sites.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, run this on prompts like 'best practice management software for medical spas' and pitch the aesthetic and healthcare trade sites and software roundups that come back, rather than budgeting for Reddit seeding. The output is a target list for outreach, refreshed quarterly.

**Apply anywhere:** Run this on the prompts your buyers ask, then pitch the trade sites and roundups that actually come back, rather than budgeting for whichever community site a general study names.

### 21. Do not chase AI visibility on definitional queries, because no brand is named  `81.9`
*core · content insights · source 81*

Grow and Convert make a hard structural point about which queries can be won at all. Bottom-of-funnel topics are the only ones where LLMs mention brands, products or services in their responses. Ask a top-of-funnel question like 'what is a DSCR loan' and the model explains the concept without recommending or linking to any brand, because there is no buying intent to satisfy. They go further on the economics: the old convention of writing 'what is X' posts for traffic is being upended, since that content never converted well and now does not even generate the traffic it used to. Their bottom-of-funnel examples were 'best DSCR lenders in Connecticut', 'best LLC mortgage lenders', 'best fix and flip lenders' and 'fastest closing private lenders'.

> "it'll simply explain the concept without recommending"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show ChatGPT answering 'What is a DSCR loan?' with no brand recommendation, and report bottom-of-funnel keywords convert 10x to 25x better.

**How to do it**

1. List your planned topics and ask each one as a prompt in ChatGPT before commissioning anything.
2. Mark any topic where the answer names zero brands as unwinnable for AI visibility.
3. Keep those topics only if they earn organic clicks or serve an internal linking purpose, and cap the spend.
4. Move the budget to queries where the answer returns a list of vendors.
5. Build those queries from the 'best X for Y', 'X vs Y' and 'fastest or cheapest X' shapes.
6. Confirm buying intent by checking the query has a meaningful CPC.
7. Publish one page per vendor-list query and re-run the prompt after it ranks.
8. Audit the existing blog and demote definitional posts that neither rank nor convert.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Assuming a high-volume informational term is worth having because it feeds the funnel. If the model answers it without naming anyone, there is no mention available to win no matter how good the page is.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's 'what is' explainers on aesthetics and practice terminology will not earn AI mentions. The commissioning budget should tilt toward 'best software for X practice' and comparison pages, where the model has to name vendors.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask your candidate topic as a prompt first. If the model answers without naming any brand, there is no mention to win, so spend the budget on queries where the answer returns a vendor list.

### 22. Do the reasoning for the indexing LLM inside the sentence  `73.5`
*core · best practices · source 73*

Barnard calls this the biggest mistake people are making right now. Everyone watches a chatbot reason in a chat window and assumes the same intelligence applies everywhere. But at the two moments SEOs actually care about, indexing and grounding, there is no reasoning happening: the systems use the cheapest possible LLM, which he describes as a pretty stupid model that digests, categorizes and files. If understanding your entity description requires an inference step, that inference never happens and the conclusion never enters the index. So you write the conclusion yourself. His example: 'Jason Barnard is a digital brand engineer and has been doing SEO since 1998. Therefore he's an SEO expert.' Without the 'therefore' clause, the expert claim does not get indexed. He argues this applies across an entire SEO strategy, not just entity optimization.

> "you have to do the reasoning for them"

**Evidence:** Barnard states the 'therefore he's an SEO expert' clause is what gets the expert claim indexed, and that omitting it means the claim does not get in.

**How to do it**

1. Find every claim on your key pages that a reader infers rather than reads, such as expertise, seniority, market position or suitability.
2. Write the premise as a plain fact with a date or number attached.
3. Write the conclusion as its own explicit sentence, joined with 'therefore', 'so' or 'that makes', with the entity name as the subject.
4. Avoid pronouns in the conclusion sentence; repeat the name so the claim binds to the entity.
5. Apply the same treatment to platform descriptions, author bios and press boilerplate, not just the website.
6. Test the rewritten text in an entity-recognition tool and confirm the intended entities and types are picked up.
7. Repeat the pattern consistently across every mention, since consistency is what raises confidence.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Jason Barnard is a digital brand engineer and has been doing SEO since 1998. Therefore, he's an SEO expert.
```

**Pitfall:** Writing sophisticated copy that implies expertise through tone and detail. The chat model would infer it; the cheap indexing model will not, so the claim simply never enters the index.

**Apply at Pabau:** Rewrite Pabau's About page and author bios so conclusions are stated outright: name the years in market and the practice count, then a separate sentence stating what that makes Pabau. Do the same in the boilerplate used on guest posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Never leave a conclusion to inference. State the supporting fact, then state the conclusion in its own sentence with the entity name as the subject, because the models that index and ground do not reason.

### 24. Exact-match domains boost GEO citations and cut PPC costs  `16.1`
*core · general insights · source 16*

TJ Robertson and David Quaid argue that building a second or third exact-match/satellite domain, rather than publishing everything on one brand domain, increases the odds of appearing in the finite citation lists LLMs generate, since there is "a statistical factor" tied to how many of your domains are even eligible to be cited, on top of whether the LLM already has prior knowledge of the brand. Separately they make a PPC economics case for the same domains: for expensive commercial keywords, a dedicated domain can earn a near-perfect Quality Score (matching what a branded domain gets on its own brand terms), driving lower cost-per-click and higher CTR/relevancy match versus bidding a generic branded domain against a specific commercial term. A second domain also removes a single point of failure (DNS/host outage, expired registration, a hack) and lets a B2B brand launch campaign- or segment-specific messaging without fighting brand guidelines or slow internal approval chains.

> "around $540 a click. A hundred clicks is $50,000."

**Evidence:** Specific CPC estimate of ~$540/click for "personal injury lawyer" (so $50,000 per 100 clicks) and a projected 40-60% cost saving from higher Quality Scores on a dedicated domain; also cites Nike and Microsoft using satellite/EMD domains for events and campaigns, and references HouseFresh and RetroDodo as real single-domain businesses hit hard by the Helpful Content Update.

**Apply at Pabau:** For any high-CPC commercial keyword Pabau bids on, or any campaign/segment that doesn't fit the main brand voice, evaluate whether a dedicated domain would both improve GEO citation odds and pay for itself through Quality-Score-driven PPC savings, rather than defaulting to pabau.com for every initiative.

**Apply anywhere:** For any high-CPC commercial keyword you bid on, or any campaign/segment that doesn't fit the main brand voice, evaluate whether a dedicated domain would both improve GEO citation odds and pay for itself through Quality-Score-driven PPC savings, rather than defaulting to your main domain for every initiative.
