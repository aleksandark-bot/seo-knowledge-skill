### 1. Add an on-the-fly mini sample to the writer application form  `164.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 164*

Grow and Convert added a final application question that works as a miniature test project, after being frustrated by writers with strong portfolios who collapsed on the paid test. The reasoning is that nobody edits a form answer, so it shows the writer's real unaided ability, whereas a portfolio piece may have been rewritten by the client. Their own version asks the applicant to look at a client's SaaS website and write a short paragraph highlighting the software's key selling points. They say most writers should manage it in about 15 minutes, and they deliberately keep it short to respect applicants' time. If a candidate takes hours over it, they count that as dedication rather than cheating. The rule they give is to make the question as close as possible to the actual work, just a very short version of it.

> "it's unlikely anyone will be editing it"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert added the mini sample after repeatedly finding that experienced writers with awesome portfolio pieces did terribly on the paid test project.

**How to do it**

1. Add one final question to the writer application that mirrors your real work in miniature.
2. Scope it to roughly 15 minutes of effort so good writers do not skip the application.
3. Point applicants at a real product page or site and ask for a short paragraph selling it.
4. Do not supply a brief beyond what a working writer would get, so the answer shows unaided thinking.
5. Score the mini sample separately from the portfolio and advance only applicants strong on both.
6. Keep two saved examples, one pass and one fail, as the internal benchmark for scoring.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Look at this client's SaaS website and write a short paragraph highlighting the software's key selling points.
```

**Pitfall:** Making the mini sample long enough to feel like unpaid work. Grow and Convert cap it deliberately; a long unpaid exercise drives away the strong writers who have other options.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's writer application should end with one question: look at pabau.com and write a short paragraph selling the software to an aesthetic clinic owner. That single answer will filter faster than any portfolio review.

**Apply anywhere:** End your writer application with one short on-the-fly writing question modeled on the real job, capped at about 15 minutes. Nobody edits a form answer, so it shows what the writer can do alone.

### 2. Add an unedited mini sample question to the writer application  `167.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 167*

Grow and Convert used to move candidates from portfolio straight to the test project, and kept finding writers with years of experience and excellent portfolios who did badly once tested. Their explanation is that you cannot tell how much of a portfolio piece was written by the writer and how much was rewritten by the client. So they added a final application question that works as a miniature test project, which they call the mini sample. It asks for a few sentences written on the fly. If a candidate takes hours over it, that is fine and shows dedication, but nobody else can edit it. Their own version asks applicants to look at a client SaaS website and write a short paragraph highlighting the key selling points, a task most writers finish in about 15 minutes. They say it has helped a lot.

> "acts as a mini test project"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert added the mini sample after repeatedly seeing strong portfolios fail the paid test project, and say it has helped a lot.

**How to do it**

1. Add one final free-text question to the writer application form.
2. Make the task a very short version of the real work, so it takes roughly 15 minutes.
3. Point the applicant at a live URL, for example a product site, and ask for one short paragraph on its key selling points.
4. Do not give a word count or formatting spec; you are grading thinking and phrasing.
5. Grade for specificity: does the paragraph name a concrete mechanism and outcome, or does it shout adjectives and rhetorical questions?
6. Reject submissions built on borrowed statistics, all-caps emphasis and questions like are you still letting doubt stop you.
7. Advance only applicants who pass both the portfolio filter and the mini sample.
8. Keep every submission so you can benchmark new applicants against the ones who worked out.

**Tools:** Google Forms

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Look at this client's SaaS website and write a short paragraph highlighting the software's key selling points.
```

**Pitfall:** Relying on portfolio pieces alone. Grow and Convert had experienced writers with awesome portfolios do terribly on the test project, because the portfolio may reflect the client's editor as much as the writer.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's writer application should ask candidates to write one paragraph on a named competitor's product page selling points, which tests exactly the comparison skill the blog needs.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a short on-the-fly writing question to your application form. It is the only sample you know the applicant wrote alone, and it costs you nothing to grade.

### 3. Analyze the SERP before the interview and turn your gaps into questions  `126.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 126*

Grow and Convert put SERP analysis before the expert interview, not after it, and they use it as a question-generation tool rather than as a competitive audit. Before each interview they answer three things: what is the search intent, meaning what questions the searcher is trying to answer, what information they want and what they have likely tried before; what other people are already saying on this topic; and how the product will likely tie into the topic. Trying to answer those three questions inevitably exposes holes in their own understanding, and they list those holes out as the questions for the subject matter expert. Cameron Brown prepared a written question doc this way for the enterprise reporting tools piece. The output is an interview aimed at exactly what the SERP cannot tell you.

> "shed light on holes in our understanding"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert show Cam's prepared question document for the enterprise reporting tools piece that went on to rank on page one.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the top 10 results for the target keyword before booking the interview.
2. Write one paragraph on search intent: the question, the information wanted, and what the searcher has already tried.
3. List the claims and themes every competing page repeats, so you know what is already commodity.
4. Write one paragraph on how your product plausibly ties into this topic.
5. Mark every point where you cannot answer your own paragraph, and convert each into a direct question.
6. Put the questions in a shared doc and send it to the expert before the call.
7. Order the questions so the ones only an insider can answer come first, in case the call runs short.

**Tools:** Google Docs

**Pitfall:** Running the interview before the SERP work. You then ask generic questions, get generic answers, and cover ground the top 10 already covers.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO and /generate flows already pull the SERP. David should add one step: convert the SERP gaps into a written question list for the internal expert, and store it with the brief so the interview is not improvised.

**Apply anywhere:** Do the SERP analysis first and use it to find out what you do not know. Write the intent, the competitor consensus and the product tie-in, then turn every sentence you cannot finish into a question for your internal expert.

### 4. Answer search intent immediately to stop pogo-sticking  `44.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 44*

The core directive is to give the searcher their answer the moment they land on the page, with no scrolling required. The stated mechanism is "pogo sticking": if the answer isn't immediately visible, the searcher goes back to the search results and picks a different site, which signals to Google that the content wasn't satisfying and leads to lower rankings — separate from and in addition to the lost conversion opportunity. This is framed as the single most important thing to fix in SEO copywriting before anything else, since it damages both rankings and sales simultaneously.

> "This tells Google that your content is not satisfying"

**How to do it**

1. For every page, identify the single core question or intent behind its target keyword before writing anything else.
2. Place a direct, complete answer to that intent at the very top of the page, visible without scrolling.
3. Avoid burying the answer under introductory throat-clearing, backstory, or generic category explanation.
4. After the immediate answer, use the rest of the page to build trust and depth, then move the reader toward a call-to-action.
5. Audit existing underperforming pages specifically for this failure mode (making the reader scroll or read multiple paragraphs before reaching an answer) as a priority fix. (inferred audit step)

**Pitfall:** Making the searcher scroll or read through preamble before reaching their answer — this is framed as directly causing pogo-sticking, which signals dissatisfaction to Google and can lower rankings, on top of losing the conversion opportunity.

### 5. Apply the earns-its-way test to the first sentence of every product entry  `92.9`
*core · best practices · source 92*

Grow and Convert's single operational test for fluff is a rule borrowed from non-fiction writing: does every word in this sentence earn its way onto the page. They apply it hardest to the opening sentence of a product description, which they say is ironically where fluff most often appears and where it is least needed. You are on the first sentence, you have not reached the details yet, so just describe the product plainly. Running the test on the WPBeginner line about TranslatePress, full-fledged fails because nothing in the article defends what makes a plugin full-fledged rather than normal, and every aspect of your website fails because translating every word is what all these plugins do by definition. Both words sound like good sales copy and are not. The test is per word, not per sentence, and it is the cheapest quality gate in the process.

> "Does every word in this sentence earn its way onto the page?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's rewrite of the failing line: while most translation plugins are limited to just this thing, TranslatePress is a full-fledged WordPress multilingual plugin that also does the following.

**How to do it**

1. Isolate the first sentence of each product entry in the draft.
2. Read it one word at a time and ask what each word contributes that the sentence loses without it.
3. Delete any word that survives only because it sounds impressive.
4. For any evaluative adjective you want to keep, add the sentence that defends it with specific features.
5. Rewrite the opener as a plain description of what the product does and for whom.
6. Move the differentiating claim into sentence two, where you have room to back it.
7. Repeat the pass on the first sentence of every section heading, the second-worst fluff site.

**Pitfall:** Writers defend an adjective by saying it is true. Truth is not the test; earning its place is. If the article never explains what makes the product full-fledged rather than normal, the word is fluff regardless of accuracy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add the earns-its-way test to Pabau's sentence gate, applied specifically to the first sentence of every provider entry in a listicle. Those openers are where category-baseline adjectives cluster.

**Apply anywhere:** Add the earns-its-way word test to your editing checklist, applied specifically to the first sentence of every product entry in a listicle. Those openers are where category-baseline adjectives cluster.

### 6. Ask an agency whether its writers are staff or a freelance rolodex  `163.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 163*

Grow and Convert concede that an agency holding the whole process from A to Z has a structural advantage over a single contractor or in-house hire. Then they name the exception that undoes it: many agencies outsource content writing to freelance writers in their rolodex. That puts you back in the flawed briefing model the article criticizes, only with an extra layer of markup and one more handoff between the person who set the keyword strategy and the person writing the page. So the advantage only holds if the agency has in-house writers and a documented process for getting product expertise into drafts. Ask who physically writes the words, whether they are employed or contracted, how they are trained, and whether the same writers will stay on your account.

> "outsource content writing to freelance writers in"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite their own in-house writer team and multi-year training process as the reason their agency model avoids this failure.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the agency directly: are your writers employees or freelancers, and how many are on staff?
2. Ask how writers are trained and how long the training takes, since a real program implies real investment.
3. Ask whether the same writers stay on your account or rotate per piece.
4. Ask to speak to or see bylined work from the specific writer who would be assigned to you.
5. Ask what the writer does before drafting: whether they get an interview with your team or only a brief.
6. Treat a large freelance bench with no training process as equivalent to hiring freelancers yourself, and price it accordingly.
7. Put writer continuity in the contract if product depth matters to you.

**Pitfall:** You pay agency rates for a chain that ends in the same lightly briefed freelancer you could have hired directly. The signal is drafts whose voice changes month to month.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau commissions an outside agency, require named in-house writers with healthcare or SaaS experience and continuity across the engagement. Rotating freelancers cannot hold aesthetic-practice product detail.

**Apply anywhere:** If you commission an agency, require named in-house writers with sector experience and continuity across the engagement. Rotating freelancers cannot hold product detail.

### 7. Ask candidates for topic ideas and the lead story behind each  `101.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 101*

Step two of the Grow and Convert process: after stating the goal, ask the candidate to produce content ideas and narrate how each idea produces a lead or customer. They call ideation the single most important part of content marketing strategy. The judging rule is length of explanation. Their own answer is one sentence: they prioritize topics where the searcher shows buying intent for what the client sells. For a client selling remote executive assistant services that means 'best executive assistant services', 'how to hire an executive assistant', and 'executive assistant vs. virtual assistant'. The failing answer is the conditional chain: visitor lands, reads, remembers the brand, returns, downloads an ebook, opens nurture emails, and eventually buys. More steps means less likely to work.

> "the story of how that piece of content would bring in a new lead"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use this as their standard sales-call answer and cite the executive assistant services client topic set.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the candidate to bring five to ten specific topic titles, not categories.
2. For each topic, ask them to tell the story of how it produces one lead.
3. Count the conditional steps in the story and reject anything over about three.
4. Check that the topics name the product category the way a buyer would search it.
5. Look for the three bottom-funnel shapes: 'best X', 'how to hire X', 'X vs Y'.
6. Reject idea lists made entirely of introductory guides to the industry.

**Pitfall:** A polished multi-step nurture narrative sounds sophisticated and is the main tell that the topics will not convert.

**Apply at Pabau:** David can score any proposed pabau.com topic the same way: if the one-sentence lead story needs an ebook and a nurture sequence, drop it and pick a topic like 'best practice management software for medspas' instead.

**Apply anywhere:** Make candidates pitch actual topic titles and narrate the path from search to lead for each one. Prefer topics where the search itself shows buying intent, and reject any idea whose payoff needs a five-step nurture chain.

### 8. Ask each department a different set of best-customer questions  `122.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 122*

Grow and Convert do not ask everyone the same question. They give each stakeholder group its own question set, because each sees a different slice of the account. Account management and customer success get asked which customers see a ton of value in the product, which they have never had an issue with, and which they think are the best fit for the company. Sales get asked which accounts closed in the last six to twelve months were easiest to close and why, which accounts had a huge pain point the company solved, and what those pain points were. Marketing get asked which leads had the highest chance of converting to an opportunity. The answers form the qualitative side that the CRM sort is checked against.

> "Which accounts had a huge pain point"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publish these exact question sets as the ones they run in client sessions, noting conversations then branch by company.

**How to do it**

1. Split the room by function before asking anything: success, sales, marketing.
2. Ask success: which customers see a ton of value, which have never caused an issue, and which are the best fit.
3. Ask sales: which of the last six to twelve months' closes were easiest and why.
4. Ask sales: which accounts had a huge pain point you solved, and what exactly the pain point was.
5. Ask marketing: which lead types had the highest chance of becoming an opportunity.
6. Write every named company on a shared list as it is said, with the reason attached.
7. Compare the named list against the CRM top-band list and investigate any name that appears on one but not the other.

**Pitfall:** Asking one generic 'who is our ideal customer' question to the whole room. You get a job title, not a list of named accounts with reasons, and nothing downstream can be checked against it.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau runs this, the sales question about the pain point solved is the one to press hardest. Those answers become the H2s on template pages and the opening problem statement on blog posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Give each department its own question list in the research session, and capture named accounts plus the reason behind each name rather than abstract persona traits.

### 9. Ask for the outline first, then the article - never one-shot it  `64.4`
*core · ai workflows · source 64*

The mechanical detail that most improves output quality in this workflow. He prompts for a detailed blog post outline based on the source material first, and explains why: the outline gives the model the skeleton of what it's about to write, and if you try to one-shot the article it will not be as thorough or as well-defined as if you asked for the outline beforehand. Only then does he prompt for the article from that outline. Two practical observations from the demo: he uses Claude specifically because the larger token limit lets him supply much more source context than ChatGPT would accept for the same job, and the length instruction undershoots - asking for 2,000 words produced a 1,400-word article, which he verifies by pasting into a word counter.

> "give the AI the skeleton"

**How to do it**

1. Paste the target keyword and the full source material into a model with a large context window.
2. Prompt for a detailed blog post outline based on that source material, not for the article.
3. Review the outline against the coverage of the ranking pages and fix gaps at the outline stage.
4. Prompt for the article from the approved outline, with an explicit length target.
5. Expect the length to undershoot the instruction and check the actual word count.
6. For higher quality, write section by section against the outline rather than the whole piece at once.

**Tools:** Claude

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Write a detailed blog post outline based on the following text, about the keyword [target keyword].
```

**Pitfall:** The context limit is the reason for the tool choice here - if the source material won't fit, the model will refuse or silently truncate, and truncated source material produces a thinner article than no source material would suggest.

**Apply at Pabau:** Outline-then-write should be the standing rule for any Pabau article generated with AI, with the outline reviewed by a person before drafting - that review is the cheapest quality gate in the process.

**Apply anywhere:** Outline-then-write should be the standing rule for any AI-assisted article, with the outline reviewed by a person before drafting - that review is the cheapest quality gate in the process.

### 10. Ask product experts what the product replaces and why features were designed that way  `182.13`
*core · concrete actions · source 182*

Grow and Convert explain why the expert interview matters specifically for bottom-funnel pieces: those topics are product-centric, so the differentiating detail exists only inside the client's company. They are explicit that this is not grabbing a few quotes to drop into an article. It is hour-long recorded interviews, with the whole article shaped around the expert's viewpoint, so the writer is never asked to pretend to be the expert. The detail they name as the payoff is unusually specific: how the product is differentiated, what it replaces, and why features were designed in certain ways. Those three questions are the ones a writer researching on Google cannot answer, and they are what stop a comparison page reading like every other comparison page.

> "why features were designed in certain ways"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe hour-long recorded interviews as the mechanism behind the product expertise in their bottom-funnel pieces.

**How to do it**

1. Book a one-hour recorded call with a product owner or senior implementer for each bottom-funnel piece, not once per engagement.
2. Ask what the customer used before buying, tool by tool, and what they stopped paying for.
3. Ask why each relevant feature was designed the way it is, and what the rejected alternative was.
4. Ask which competitor the deal was against and what the buyer said tipped it.
5. Transcribe the call and build the outline from the transcript, not from the SERP.
6. Keep the expert's framing as the spine of the article and use search research only to fill gaps.
7. Send the draft back to the interviewee for a factual pass before publishing.

**Tools:** Zoom, Otter.ai

**Pitfall:** Interviewing a marketer rather than someone who builds or implements the product produces the same generic positioning the article was meant to escape. The signal is a transcript with no design decisions or displaced tools in it.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau comparison and best-software pages, interview an implementation specialist and ask what systems new practices switch off, and why the booking or consent flow works the way it does. That answer belongs in the page.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a recorded hour with a product owner per bottom-funnel article and ask three questions: what the product replaces, how it differs, and why each feature was designed that way.

### 11. Ask whether expert interviews happen per piece or only at kickoff  `178.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 178*

Grow and Convert give buyers a specific way to grade an agency's product-expertise process: ask what shape it takes. The options they list are a single one-off interview at the start of the engagement, a handful of one-hour calls, or interviews with your internal experts on a piece-by-piece basis. They say the last one is both extremely rare among outside agencies and the most effective, because product and domain expertise sits in your staff's heads and has to be pulled out for each topic. Their own model runs it that way. The question generalizes to any writer you hire, in-house or freelance. If the process is a kickoff call only, the writer is working from memory of one conversation by article ten and has drifted back to generic research.

> "doing interviews on a piece-by-piece basis"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say per-piece interviewing is extremely rare among outside agencies and is the approach they use themselves.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the agency to describe the exact shape of its expertise process, not whether it has one.
2. Force the choice: one-off kickoff interview, a few one-hour calls, or a fresh interview per article.
3. Ask who inside your company they expect to interview and for how long per piece.
4. Ask to see one article next to the interview it came from, so you can check the transfer.
5. Ask what happens when a topic falls outside the kickoff conversation's scope.
6. Budget your own experts' time honestly: a per-piece model needs an internal hour per article.
7. Make per-piece interviews a contract line so it survives account-team turnover.

**Pitfall:** Accepting 'yes we interview your team' without asking the frequency. A single kickoff call degrades into generic research within a few articles, and the drop is invisible until a customer notices the article is wrong.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles on clinical workflow or compliance need an interview with a Pabau product or support person per topic, not one onboarding call reused across the blog. Book that hour before the outline, not after the draft.

**Apply anywhere:** Ask any agency or writer whether expert interviews happen once at kickoff or per article, and insist on per article. Then reserve an internal expert hour for each piece so the process can actually run.

### 12. Ask whether the SEO writes the content or only hands over briefs  `163.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 163*

Grow and Convert flag a common split that wastes budget: the SEO does keyword research, focuses the valuable buying keywords on the homepage and a few landing pages, then relegates blog content to general top-of-funnel traffic and hands briefs to writers without worrying much about the articles. Their argument is that most businesses have dozens of high-buying-intent keywords, and a homepage plus a few landing pages cannot rank for all of them. Each variation needs its own new page, made carefully enough to rank and convert. So ask directly whether the scope includes creating the content or stops at the brief. They recommend hiring a service that does both, so the same party is accountable for the page ranking. The question applies to in-house candidates too: ask whether they would write it themselves, and if not, what process keeps freelancers on the keyword strategy.

> "They create a giant keywords list and hand content briefs to writers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert describe this brief-only pattern as a huge waste of SEO resources, based on client accounts of previous vendors.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the candidate whether the engagement includes producing the finished pages or ends at the keyword list and brief.
2. If it ends at the brief, ask who writes, who edits, who publishes, and who is accountable if the page does not rank.
3. Count your own high-buying-intent keywords and confirm the plan creates a dedicated page per variation, not extra sections on the homepage.
4. For an in-house candidate, ask whether they will write the content themselves.
5. If they plan to use freelancers, ask for their specific process for making a freelancer execute the keyword strategy.
6. Prefer a single vendor accountable for both strategy and the finished page, so ranking failure cannot be blamed across a handoff.

**Pitfall:** Paying for a keyword list and a stack of briefs, then discovering there is no writing capacity to execute them. The signal is briefs aging in a folder past 60 days.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau already has the writing pipeline, so any outside help should be scoped to keyword strategy and briefs only, with in-house execution. Do not pay a vendor for briefs that duplicate what /SEO already produces.

**Apply anywhere:** If you already have writing capacity, scope outside help to strategy and briefs only. If you do not, do not buy briefs at all — buy finished pages from one accountable party.

### 13. Audit thin landing pages that rank nowhere and rebuild them as posts  `131.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 131*

Grow and Convert's first move on the Smartlook engagement was auditing the existing high-intent pages. Smartlook had well-designed competitor landing pages targeting terms like 'Hotjar Alternative' and 'FullStory Alternative', plus sharp feature pages. Almost none ranked on page one and most were not in the top 100. The diagnosis was word count under 500 per page, thin against what already ranked, plus missing search-intent elements. The alternative pages never compared features against the competitor or named the competitor's shortcomings. The feature pages had no room for use cases with examples. Their fix was not to expand the landing page but to build a dedicated in-depth blog post for the same keyword, which gave space to cover nuances and show expertise. They name too little content and not meeting intent as the two reasons most agencies fail to rank high-intent pages.

> "the total word count per page was less than 500"

**Evidence:** Smartlook's 'Hotjar Alternative' and 'FullStory Alternative' landing pages were under 500 words and mostly not in the top 100; the replacement post '12 Hotjar alternatives for website and mobile app analytics' ranked.

**How to do it**

1. List every landing page and feature page on the site that targets a buying keyword.
2. Check each one's rank for its target keyword and flag any that is outside the top 100.
3. Count the words on each flagged page and compare against the word counts of the current top 10 results.
4. Read the ranking pages and list the elements they contain that your page lacks, such as feature-by-feature comparison, competitor shortcomings, or worked use cases.
5. Decide per keyword whether the SERP wants a landing page or an in-depth article; if the ranking results are articles, build an article.
6. Write the new post to cover the comparison nuances the landing page skipped, not just a longer version of the same copy.
7. Keep the landing page for paid traffic and direct navigation, and point the new post at it with an internal link.
8. Track rank and conversions on the new post separately from the landing page so you can compare the two.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Teams keep padding the landing page instead of changing format. A CRO-optimized page under 500 words will keep losing to article-format results, because the missing thing is the analysis searchers came for, not the length.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should audit Pabau's competitor and feature pages the same way. Any page targeting a term like 'Pabau vs Aesthetics Pro' that sits outside the top 100 with thin copy should get a full comparison article on /blog/, with the feature comparison and the honest limitations of the competitor, while the landing page stays for ads.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your competitor and feature pages for rank and word count. Any page targeting a buying keyword that sits outside the top 100 with under 500 words should be answered with a full in-depth article that does the comparison work, while the landing page stays for paid traffic.

### 14. Audit whether your content supplier is actually a writing service  `182.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 182*

Grow and Convert's third screening question is whether the agency promotes what it produces. Their finding is that most content marketing agencies do not distribute anything and are, in their words, glorified writing services. At best they will share a piece through the client's own social accounts, and asked directly how they promote, they say they publish it and wait for it to rank in Google. The cost of that is time: search takes months to work, so the article sits doing very little while the retainer runs. The test is therefore not whether promotion is mentioned in the pitch deck but whether a named channel, a named owner and a named budget exist for the weeks immediately after publication.

> "We publish it and wait for it to rank"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say this pattern came up repeatedly across clients who had previously used other content agencies.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the supplier to describe, for the last article they published, every distribution action taken in the first 30 days.
2. Count how many of those actions used a channel the supplier controls rather than your own social accounts.
3. Ask who pays for paid distribution and out of which budget line.
4. Require a promotion plan per piece in the statement of work, with channel, owner and spend named.
5. Set a checkpoint 30 days after each publish to compare traffic from promotion against traffic from search.
6. If the answer is publish-and-wait, either renegotiate the scope to include distribution or move that budget to a supplier who does it.

**Pitfall:** Sharing a post through the client's own LinkedIn page gets counted as promotion. It reaches an audience you already own, so it adds nothing the client could not do themselves.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat publication as the midpoint, not the end, of an article's job: assign each new pabau.com piece a named distribution step, whether that is paid social to aesthetics practice owners, a newsletter placement or outreach for links.

**Apply anywhere:** Make distribution an explicit line in any content contract, with channel, owner and budget per piece, and audit the last published article against it.

### 15. Ban the Google research paper brief given to freelance writers  `153.8`
*core · best practices · source 153*

Grow and Convert name a specific failure mode in outsourced content. Hand a freelancer a brief and let them do their own research, and the writer reads the top Google results and produces something similar to what already exists. They call this a Google research paper, and describe it as the human version of what AI does. It is the same argument they make against AI writing: both synthesize existing material, so neither can produce something unique. The fix is that the uniqueness has to come from a source the top 10 does not contain, which means the brief must supply it rather than asking the writer to find it.

> "We call this a "Google research paper"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's practice across 80+ clients: they refuse both AI-written drafts and freelancer self-research briefs on the same reasoning, that both synthesize existing results.

**How to do it**

1. Stop issuing briefs whose research instruction is to read the top-ranking results.
2. Before commissioning, decide which source of uniqueness the piece will carry: original data, a different angle, an expert interview, or your own product experience.
3. Supply that raw material in the brief itself: the dataset, the interview transcript, the internal numbers, or the support tickets.
4. Name in the brief the specific claims the writer must make that no ranking page makes.
5. Reject drafts whose substance could have been assembled from the current top 10, regardless of how well written they are.
6. Run one subject-matter-expert interview per piece where you have no proprietary data, and give the writer the recording.
7. Keep the SERP reading in the brief for format and intent only, never for substance.

**Pitfall:** A well-written, on-brief, complete draft that passes every editorial check but contains nothing the top 10 does not already say. It reads fine and never ranks.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau brief should carry its own originality nugget as raw material, not as an instruction. David should attach practice-owner interview notes, anonymized Pabau usage data or support-ticket themes to the brief before a writer starts.

**Apply anywhere:** Never brief a writer to research a topic by reading the top results. Decide the source of uniqueness first, put the raw material in the brief, and reject drafts that could have been assembled from the current top 10.

### 16. Book one hour with sales and one with product, on four fixed questions  `134.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 134*

After the desk research, Grow and Convert run kickoff interviews of at least one hour with one or two experienced salespeople and another hour with one or two experienced product people. The questions are follow-ups from the research, and they say every question should fall into four categories: who is the target audience, what are that audience's pain points, how does the product solve each pain point and specifically which feature does it, and what are the product's differentiators. They claim two or three extremely focused hours with internal experts replaces a college-level 101 course in the subject. They also say this pair of steps gets you enough to build the content strategy and pick the first topics, but not enough to write every article, which still needs its own per-topic expert interview.

> "you need to spend two or three extremely focused hours with subject matter experts"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say two to three focused hours with internal experts is enough to build a content strategy in an unfamiliar industry.

**How to do it**

1. Schedule two separate hours: one with one or two experienced sales reps, one with one or two experienced product people.
2. Sort every prepared question into the four buckets before the call and drop anything that fits none.
3. Ask sales for the target audiences and the pain points that show up on live calls.
4. For each pain point, ask which specific feature resolves it, and refuse a general 'the platform handles that'.
5. Ask product for the differentiators and what a competitor cannot do.
6. Record both calls and keep the unanswered items as the seed of the per-article interview list.
7. Use the output to build the topic strategy, then book a separate expert interview for each article you commission.

**Pitfall:** Interviewing external industry experts instead of internal ones. Grow and Convert say external experts know the field but not the product, which is almost completely useless for bottom-of-funnel content where you must explain feature nuances and differentiators.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should hold a standing two-hour version of this with a senior sales rep and a product manager, refreshed each quarter, and store the answers as a shared brief that every writer of a /blog/ or /templates/ page starts from.

**Apply anywhere:** Run two one-hour internal interviews, one with sales and one with product, structured around audience, pain points, the specific feature that fixes each, and differentiators. Refresh it quarterly and make it the starting brief for every writer.

### 17. Break a broad topic into pain points before writing a single post  `98.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 98*

Grow and Convert's core process. Benji Hyam starts from the high-level concept, then lists every pain point someone hits while solving that problem, and writes one detailed post per pain point. Their worked example: the high-level concept was 'finding and hiring blog writers'. The pain points were deciding between in-house, agency or freelancers; where to find writers and how to evaluate them; and how much to pay and how to manage a team. Those became three separate posts, published in the order a reader hits them. He is explicit that he knew the pain points from having done the job, and says that if he had not, he would have run user research first rather than guessed. The rule he gives: do not guess the pain point, and if you do not know it, go find out.

> "First, know the high level pain point. Don't guess."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's three posts on hiring writers were later compiled into a mega guide of 5,000+ words on the topic.

**How to do it**

1. Write the high-level concept down as a phrase, not a title, for example 'finding and hiring blog writers'.
2. List every decision or obstacle a reader faces between having the problem and solving it, in the order they hit them.
3. If you do not personally know those obstacles, run user research interviews or a survey before listing anything.
4. Corroborate the list against Google suggested searches and Q&A forums such as Quora for the same topic.
5. Cut any item you cannot write 1,500 detailed words about, and split any item that has two distinct answers.
6. Assign one post per surviving pain point and sequence them in the order a reader encounters them.
7. Write each post at a level of detail that would be impossible if the topic were broader.
8. Once the sequence covers the whole process, compile the posts into one mega guide and add content that fills the gaps between them.

**Tools:** Quora, Google

**Pitfall:** Teams guess the pain points from the marketing team's assumptions rather than from customers, and end up with a list of sub-topics that sound sensible but that nobody actually searches for. The signal is that the posts get no comments and no long-tail impressions.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog planning should start from a practice-owner pain point list, not a topic. Take 'running an aesthetic practice' and break it into the actual decisions: choosing between paper and digital consent, handling no-shows, pricing packages, hiring a second injector. One article each.

**Apply anywhere:** Plan content by decomposing a broad subject into the sequence of decisions a reader must make, then write one deep post per decision. Get the decision list from customer research rather than from internal assumptions, and compile the finished posts into a guide afterwards.

### 18. Brief writers on argument and emphasis, not on H2 counts  `167.13`
*core · best practices · source 167*

Grow and Convert argue that most content briefs fail in one of two directions. Some give almost no information and expect the writer to research the topic, which produces googled and regurgitated content. Others give excessive instructions on minute formatting: number of paragraphs, word count, header types, and which keywords to place where. They accept that the second kind checks SEO boxes, but say those details do not fix the actual problems, which are that the article teaches the customer nothing new, does not discuss the product the way you want, and emphasizes the wrong topics. Those are writing problems, and specifying the number of H2s is not going to fix them. Their alternative is a process that helps a talented writer get the substance right: what the arguments are, which to emphasize, and how to word the piece.

> "Specifying the number of H2s is not going to fix that."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name over-specified formatting briefs as one of five common mistakes companies make when hiring and managing writers.

**How to do it**

1. Open the brief with the argument the article must make, in one sentence.
2. List the arguments in priority order and mark which ones get the most space.
3. State the product positioning explicitly: what the product does differently and against which alternatives.
4. Name what the reader should learn that they could not get from the current top-ranking pages.
5. Supply the expert interview transcript as the source of that new information.
6. Keep formatting instructions to the minimum your CMS and on-page process genuinely need.
7. Move keyword placement and header structure to the editor's on-page pass, not the writer's brief.
8. Review the writer's outline against the argument list before drafting starts.

**Pitfall:** A brief heavy on word counts, header counts and keyword placement feels rigorous and produces fluent, on-spec articles that say nothing new. The signal is an article that passes every SEO check and still needs a full rewrite for substance.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should lead with the argument, the differentiator against a named competitor and the expert source, and leave block structure and keyword placement to the on-page pass David runs afterwards.

**Apply anywhere:** Brief on substance: the argument, what to emphasize, the product positioning and the expert source. Leave header counts and keyword placement to the editor's on-page pass.

### 19. Budget 60 cents to a dollar per landing page view, optimized down to 15 to 30  `128.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 128*

Grow and Convert publish their actual Facebook numbers. At launch, cost per landing page view, which is the metric they optimize for on cold and lookalike campaigns, runs from about $0.60 to over $1.00. After working through audience testing, article testing and creative optimization, they usually get it into the $0.15 to $0.30 range, though they say it varies by audience and can land outside that band either way. Two things matter operationally. They buy landing page views, not clicks, so bounced or abandoned loads are not counted as delivery. And the launch number is not the number to plan against or to kill a campaign on: the whole point of the three-phase process is that the first month's cost is expected to be three to five times the settled cost.

> "we're usually able to get the costs down in the range"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report $0.60 to $1.00+ per landing page view at campaign launch, falling to $0.15 to $0.30 after audience, content and creative optimization.

**How to do it**

1. Set the Facebook campaign objective to optimize for landing page views, not link clicks, on cold and lookalike sets.
2. Plan the launch month at $0.60 to $1.00 per landing page view and size the test budget from that, not from the target cost.
3. Do not judge or kill an audience on week-one cost; give the audience test the full one to two months.
4. Once audiences are settled, target $0.15 to $0.30 per landing page view as the optimized benchmark.
5. Split reporting by audience type, since a narrow cold interest set can sit well above the band.
6. If cost will not fall below about $0.30 after creative optimization, treat the audience as unqualified rather than the ad as bad.
7. Recheck cost per landing page view monthly, since audience fatigue pushes it back up.

**Tools:** Facebook

**Pitfall:** Reporting on link clicks instead of landing page views. Click costs look far better than they are because a large share never render the page, and the number is not comparable to these benchmarks.

**Apply at Pabau:** David can use $0.15 to $0.30 per landing page view as the pass mark for any Pabau content promotion test, and should treat a first month at $0.80 as normal rather than a failed test.

**Apply anywhere:** Buy landing page views rather than clicks when promoting content on Facebook, expect $0.60 to $1.00 at launch, and treat $0.15 to $0.30 as the optimized benchmark. A campaign that will not come below about $0.30 after creative work usually has an audience problem, not an ad problem.

### 21. Budget for heavy coaching instead of hunting for a perfect-fit writer  `164.7`
*core · best practices · source 164*

Grow and Convert say the belief that you can find writers who happen to write exactly the way you want from day one is false, and they call it out as something people online repeat. Their model is that you find a talented writer who can ace your projects after coaching. In practice an experienced content strategist works with each writer at every stage, reviewing the questionnaire, giving guidance and giving feedback, and all their content strategists were previously writers for the agency, so they know the standard. Coaching is heaviest early and tapers as the writer gains experience, and almost every writer on their team has been heavily coached. The three things they coach toward are clarity of thought in structuring a piece and reasoning through arguments, a simple style without fluff, and compelling sales copy that is direct without being over the top.

> "almost every writer on our team has been heavily coached by us"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report that almost every writer on their team has been heavily coached, and all their content strategists started as writers for the agency.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the three or four qualities you coach toward, in plain language, before onboarding anyone.
2. Assign each new writer a named editor who reviews their brief or questionnaire before drafting starts.
3. Hold a kick-off call for each of the writer's first pieces rather than sending a brief and waiting.
4. Give feedback at every stage of the first few articles, not only on the finished draft.
5. Taper the coaching deliberately as the writer's drafts stop triggering the same corrections.
6. Promote your best writers into the editor seat so the standard is held by people who wrote to it.
7. Track which correction repeats across writers and move it into the written style guide.

**Pitfall:** Firing writers who miss the voice on their first pieces. If almost everyone needs coaching, a no-coaching process just churns through applicants and blames the market.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should treat the factcheck-flow guides as the coaching curriculum and pair each new writer with an editor for their first pieces, rather than expecting the guides to be absorbed unaided.

**Apply anywhere:** Assume every writer needs coaching and staff for it. Name the two or three qualities you coach toward, assign a named editor to each new writer, and taper the support as their drafts stop repeating the same errors.

### 22. Build a content brief that classifies the keyword before listing headings  `71.1`
*core · ai workflows · source 71*

Devesh Khanal of Grow and Convert argues most content briefs are worthless because they only carry word count, paragraph length and headings scraped from page-one results by SEO software. His replacement, which he calls a super brief, opens instead by naming the keyword type. In the demo the keyword 'AI content brief generator' is classified as a category keyword on Grow and Convert's Pain Point SEO framework, meaning the searcher wants a specific type of tool and the query sits bottom of funnel. That single classification then drives everything downstream: how much of the piece is a product walkthrough versus teaching, and whether the angle is a comparison or a teardown. He says a brief that skips this leaves the writer guessing.

> "This is a category keyword"

**Evidence:** Devesh's demo brief for 'AI content brief generator' opened with the category-keyword classification and bottom-of-funnel label, which he then used to decide the article would be an opinionated teardown with a full product section.

**How to do it**

1. Before writing any brief, classify the target keyword into a type such as category, comparison, jobs-to-be-done, best-of listicle or informational.
2. Record where the keyword sits in the funnel, and say bottom, middle or top explicitly in the brief.
3. Write one line stating what that classification means for the piece, for example 'searcher is shopping for a tool, so lead with a detailed product walkthrough'.
4. Set the pitch level from the classification: a bottom-funnel category keyword earns a full product section, an informational keyword gets a passing mention.
5. Only after the classification is fixed, add the structural details like headings and length.
6. Reject any brief that arrives with headings but no keyword type or funnel position.

**Tools:** Wave Writer

**Pitfall:** Briefs that copy the headings of page-one results tell the writer what everyone else already did, which guarantees a me-too article. The signal you have hit it is a brief whose only unique content is a word count.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should add a keyword-type and funnel line to the top of every Pabau generation brief in /generate and /SEO, because it decides whether an article like a diagnostic-code page teaches or whether a template page pitches Pabau properly.

**Apply anywhere:** Add a keyword-type and funnel line to the top of every content brief you issue. It decides how much of the piece teaches and how much of it sells, and no writer can make that call from a heading list alone.

### 23. Build a disruption story from the founder interview in four fixed sections  `125.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert describe the disruption story as the article that lays out the problem the founders lived through and the unique solution they built for it. They walk through their own client example: a founder from the Stanford Research Design program who spent hours sifting raw footage, realized filmmakers and marketers had the same pain, and concluded that editing video should be as easy as editing text. Their piece opened by describing the problem, then presented the unique idea, then walked step by step through how the product solved each problem named in the introduction. Crucially, when describing each feature they harked back to the old pain, with lines like no hunting for timestamps, no manual cutting and pasting, just highlighting text. That contrast is what keeps the differentiation in front of the reader. They say this usually yields one piece, but a powerful one that produces a steady drip of conversions.

> "editing video should be as easy as editing text"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client disruption piece for a text-based video editing product, structured as problem, unique idea, then step-by-step solution with per-feature contrast; their own disruption story article generates consistent leads.

**How to do it**

1. Interview the founders about the specific pain they personally lived through before starting the company.
2. Push until they state the belief in one sentence, in the shape editing video should be as easy as editing text.
3. Open the article with the problem and its pain points, not with the company.
4. Name every distinct pain point in the introduction so the body has something to answer.
5. Present the one-sentence unique idea as its own section immediately after the problem.
6. Walk step by step through how the solution resolves each pain point in the order introduced.
7. When describing each feature, restate the old way it replaces, such as no hunting for timestamps, no manual cutting and pasting.
8. Close by restating the core assertion rather than listing features.
9. Expect one piece from this, and drive paid traffic to it rather than waiting on rankings.

**Pitfall:** Writers drift into a feature tour and drop the contrast with the old process. Without the harking back, the piece reads as product marketing and the differentiation disappears.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish one disruption story covering the spreadsheet-and-disconnected-tools reality that aesthetic practices ran before practice management software, told by the founders, with each Pabau feature described against the manual step it replaces.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish one founder-led disruption story: the pain they lived through, the one-sentence belief that came out of it, then a step-by-step walkthrough of the solution where every feature is described against the old manual process it replaces.

### 25. Build a topic map of hyper-specific case studies  `12.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 12*

Devesh's content-planning device is a 'topic map': branch out, subway-map style, every specific situation, pain point, and competitor-switch scenario where you'd want your brand mentioned if you could wave a magic wand. For each branch, the content has to be highly specific, not generic - concretely, for tier-one owned content this means building a library of detailed case studies that each name a specific customer type, their specific situation and issues, the exact solution applied, and direct quotes, so that later, when an LLM is helping a user in a closely matching situation, it can point to a brand that published a case study of someone in exactly that situation. He contrasts this explicitly with generic product pages, noting a plain category page alone isn't enough; the granular situational detail has to be layered on top.

> "We call this a company's topic map"

**How to do it**

1. Brainstorm every distinct buying situation, pain point, and competitor-switch scenario relevant to your product, laid out as branches from a central topic map, such as a mind-map or spreadsheet with one row per situation.
2. For each branch, write down the specific customer type, their exact situation or problem, and the outcome your product delivered, not a generic category description.
3. Produce a detailed case study for each major branch that includes the specific customer type, the specific issues they had, the specific solution applied, and direct quotes from the customer.
4. Build a searchable, well-organized case-study library on your site, rather than burying entries in a single generic case-studies page, so both users and LLM-triggered web searches can find the specific, relevant one.
5. Cross-reference the topic map periodically against your published content to find branches that still have zero dedicated case study or content, and prioritize filling those gaps.

**Pitfall:** Generic bottom-of-funnel product content alone is not enough - the framework requires layering highly specific situational detail, such as exact customer type, exact pain point, and exact competitor switched from, on top, since LLMs pattern-match a user's specific stated situation, not a broad product category.
