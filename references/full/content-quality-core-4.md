### 1. FAQ schema is dead; Reddit ranks because blogs buried answers  `13.13`
*core · content insights · source 13*

Edward states plainly that Google deprecated FAQ schema and it has not worked for the last two to three years, despite this still being commonly recommended advice, a concrete example of outdated SEO folklore still circulating. He connects Google's Helpful Content Update to Reddit's prominence in results with a specific causal explanation: many niche blogs were optimizing for dwell time by padding content with fluff and burying the direct answer deep in the page, so searchers had to hunt for it and trusted the page less because of the excess promotional padding around it, and Google's response was to prioritize sources like Reddit where a searcher gets a fast, trustworthy, undiluted answer instead. This reinforces the separate TL;DR insight in this transcript: the mechanism explaining Reddit's rise is the same one that punishes buried answers generally.

> "Google deprecated FAQ schema, and FAQ schema hasn't worked"

**Evidence:** Direct claim that FAQ schema has not worked for two to three years, plus the explanation that niche blogs padded content with fluff and buried the answer, prompting Google to prioritize Reddit for fast, trustworthy answers instead.

**Apply at Pabau:** Stop treating FAQ schema as a ranking tactic, and audit Pabau's own long-form articles for buried answers, restructuring any page that makes readers scroll past fluff to reach the direct answer, since that is the exact pattern the Helpful Content Update targeted.

**Apply anywhere:** Stop treating FAQ schema as a ranking tactic, and audit your own long-form articles for buried answers, restructuring any page that makes readers scroll past fluff to reach the direct answer, since that is the exact pattern the Helpful Content Update targeted.

### 2. Find the value props no page-one competitor mentions and build the article on them  `92.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 92*

Grow and Convert's differentiation method is a value-prop gap analysis of the SERP. For website translation plugin they read the page-one results and found that while competitors emphasized language counts and filled space with fluff, almost none of them emphasized or even mentioned the two value props that mattered most to buyers: automation and accuracy. That gap became the spine of their client's article. The mechanism is that competing writers all research the same way, from vendor marketing pages, so they all inherit the same wrong emphasis. Anything only reachable through customer conversation is therefore systematically absent from the SERP, and is available to whoever does the interviews. This is a repeatable originality source: not a new topic, a different set of buying criteria on an existing topic.

> "almost none of the other posts on page one of the SERP emphasize"

**Evidence:** Automation and accuracy were absent or unemphasized across page-one results for website translation plugin, and became the top two criteria in the article that reached #2.

**How to do it**

1. List the top three to five pain points from your customer-facing interviews.
2. Open every page-one result for the target keyword and search each for the language of those pain points.
3. Record, per pain point, how many competitors mention it and how many lead with it.
4. Pick the highest-ranked pain point that nobody leads with; that is the article's angle.
5. Build the criteria section so that pain point is criterion one.
6. Judge every product in the list against it explicitly, including competitors that fail.
7. Note which value props all competitors over-emphasize and deliberately demote those in your piece.
8. Re-run the gap analysis when refreshing, since a competitor may have closed it.

**Pitfall:** Doing this from the SERP alone just produces a longer version of the same article. The gap is only visible if you already hold interview-derived pain points to check the competitors against.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau's competitor roundups, list the pain points sales hears, then check which ones the page-one articles never mention. That gap is the originality nugget the article is built on, not a novel topic.

**Apply anywhere:** List the pain points your sales and support teams hear, then check which ones the page-one articles never mention. That gap is your article's angle, not a novel topic.

### 3. Fix historically high-converting posts first, park the rest  `116.3`
*core · best practices · source 116*

Grow and Convert deliberately narrowed the audit to two of the four buckets: posts that once converted well and have since dropped out of the top three, and posts still in the top three whose conversions fell. Their reasoning is speed of return. Those posts have already proved they convert, so the job is only to restore what they had. Posts that rank well but never converted much are limited by traffic ceiling, so improving them moves the total very little. Posts that never ranked at all need entirely new rankings, which takes longest. Both of those go into a low-priority pile to revisit after the first two buckets are done. The point is not that the other buckets are worthless, it is that they cannot reverse a conversion dip quickly.

> "we decided to focus on these two categories of posts first"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert reversed a client's conversion dip to an all-time high by working only these two buckets first, starting February 2023.

**How to do it**

1. Take the four audit buckets and rank them by proven conversion history, not by traffic.
2. Start with posts that converted well historically and have dropped out of the top three.
3. Then take posts still in the top three whose conversions fell, because their secondary keywords are the likely cause.
4. Defer posts that always ranked top three but converted little; their ceiling is traffic, not optimization.
5. Defer posts that never ranked, since earning a first-time ranking takes longest.
6. Set a review date to revisit the deferred buckets once the first two are re-optimized.
7. Measure the recovery against the pre-decline conversion baseline for each post, not against site totals.

**Pitfall:** Teams start with the worst performers because they look like the biggest problem. Those are the slowest to fix, so the conversion dip continues for months while effort goes into pages with no proven demand.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau's organic demo requests dip, David should refresh the articles that used to bring demos and slipped, not the ones that never worked. A page that never ranked for aesthetic practice software terms needs a new page, not an edit.

**Apply anywhere:** When conversions dip, refresh the articles that used to convert and slipped, not the ones that never worked. Pages that never ranked need rebuilding, which is slower.

### 4. Fix or consolidate best-of pages that cannot name a case where a rival wins  `66.28`
*core · concrete actions · source 66*

Pages that target purchase-decision queries without providing credible decision support: they rank the publisher first, hide the method, repeat the same template at scale, or cannot show a situation in which another option wins. The framework's instruction is to consolidate coverage and keep only comparisons with transparent criteria, evidence and meaningful trade-offs. It covers self-serving best-of lists, thin X-vs-Y comparisons and alternatives pages created at scale without disclosed criteria, testing, evidence, trade-offs or audience-specific recommendations. It scores Low on click resilience, citation potential, brand mention potential and proprietary advantage, at Medium effort — with Medium business value, the only value it retains, which is precisely why these pages survive audits. The four listed failures are diagnostic and any one of them is enough. The fourth is the sharpest test available: a comparison that cannot name a scenario in which a competitor is the right choice is not evaluating anything.

> "it ranks the publisher first, hides the method, repeats the"

**How to do it**

1. List every best-of, versus and alternatives page on the site.
2. Run the four failure tests on each: does it rank you first by default, is the method undisclosed, is it one of many from the same template, and can it name a case where a rival wins?
3. Count how many pages fail. Any single failure puts the page in this type.
4. Decide coverage first: how many comparison pages the category genuinely needs, which is almost always far fewer than exist.
5. Consolidate the surplus into that smaller set, merging the useful content and redirecting the rest.
6. For each page you keep, publish the criteria and their weighting before the verdict, and date every figure.
7. Add the evidence per criterion — first-hand testing where possible, the vendor's own documentation otherwise — rather than asserting the comparison.
8. Write the scenario in which each competitor is the better choice, specifically enough that a reader in that scenario would recognize themselves.
9. Disclose your commercial position plainly near the top.
10. Put a maintenance owner on the retained set, since stale pricing and feature claims are what turn a fixed comparison back into this type.

**Tools:** Screaming Frog, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Keeping the whole estate because it converts. The Medium business value is real, but it comes with Low scores everywhere else and it is the reason these pages accumulate — the framework's instruction is to consolidate coverage down to the pages that can pass the four tests, not to keep them all and improve the copy.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's alternatives and versus pages need the four tests run honestly. Consolidate the surplus, then for each retained page publish the criteria, date the competitor figures, and name the practice profile for which the competitor is the better fit — and remember figures for Pabau pricing come from pabau.com only.

**Apply anywhere:** Run the four tests honestly on your alternatives and versus pages. Consolidate the surplus, then for each retained page publish the criteria, date the competitor figures, and name the customer profile for which the competitor is the better fit — sourcing your own pricing from your own site only.

### 5. Fix the topic before you test the CTA on a non-converting post  `114.8`
*core · content insights · source 114*

Grow and Convert open with a case Devesh repeats often. Doing conversion optimization for a SaaS blog, he had a post ranking for a very high volume keyword that barely converted. The team ran test after test: CTAs in different positions, different messages, popups, sliders. Nothing moved. Their conclusion was that the topic simply did not attract customers, and no CTA placement can fix that. They state the principle as the biggest determinant of whether a post converts being the idea behind the post, not the call to action inside it. The consequence is a different order of operations for any business selling a product: when a page has traffic and no conversions, the first hypothesis is topic and audience mismatch, and CTA experiments are wasted effort until that is settled. They contrast this with the volume-first approach where high traffic is assumed to become leads eventually.

> "They ran test after test, putting CTAs in different places"

**Evidence:** A SaaS blog post ranking for a very high volume keyword resisted every CTA position, message, popup and slider test because the topic did not attract customers.

**How to do it**

1. When a post has traffic and no conversions, stop and read the query it ranks for before touching the page.
2. Ask whether the person searching that query needs your product now, in one sentence.
3. If the answer is no, mark the page as a topic problem and stop running CTA tests on it.
4. Check whether the traffic is arriving from a term any of your buyers would type, or only from a broad informational phrase.
5. Rewrite or retire the page against a query tied to something your product does.
6. Reserve CTA and offer testing for pages whose visitors already match your buyer.
7. Report conversions per post alongside traffic so topic failures are visible before six months of testing.
8. Feed the failed topic back into the ideation process as an example of what not to commission.

**Pitfall:** Conversion optimization budget gets spent on placement, message and popup tests for a page whose readers were never buyers. The signal is a high-traffic post where every variant tests flat against control.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before David commissions CTA or demo-block changes on a Pabau /blog/ page with traffic and no demo requests, he should check whether the ranking query belongs to a practice owner buying software. If not, retire or repoint the page.

**Apply anywhere:** When a page has traffic and no conversions, treat it as a topic problem first. Read the query it ranks for and ask whether that searcher needs your product now. Only test CTAs on pages whose readers are already buyers.

### 6. Fold standalone commodity definitions into the hub they belong to  `66.23`
*core · concrete actions · source 66*

Pages that define a common term or acronym using information already available everywhere. Search features and AI can answer these without a click, so the framework's instruction is not to delete them but to keep them only inside a relevant product, service or topic hub — or as standalone pages when the definition is genuinely brand specific. It covers dictionary-style definitions, basic explainers and acronym pages compiled from common knowledge with no distinctive brand, product, audience or next-step context. It scores Low on click resilience, citation potential, business value and proprietary advantage at Low effort, with Medium brand mention potential — the one dimension that keeps the content alive at all, and the reason the definition belongs inside a hub where it can carry brand association rather than being deleted outright. The exception is worth taking seriously: a definition of a term your product created, or a term your industry uses differently, is brand specific and is not this type.

> "defines a common term or acronym using information already available everywhere"

**How to do it**

1. Export every glossary, definition and acronym URL on the site with its clicks, conversions, inbound internal links and external links.
2. Separate the genuinely brand-specific definitions — terms your product coined, or terms your industry uses differently from common usage — and keep those as standalone pages.
3. For the rest, find the product, service or topic hub where the term is actually used, and move the definition into that page as a section.
4. 301 the standalone URL to the hub section so rankings, internal links and any external links carry over.
5. Update internal links that pointed at the old definition URL to point at the hub, rather than relying on the redirect chain.
6. Where a definition earns meaningful clicks, add what the commodity version lacks before deciding: your own data on the term, the audience-specific next step, or an example from your operating context.
7. Remove only the definitions with no traffic, no links and no hub that needs them.
8. Stop commissioning new standalone definitions, and add definitions to hub pages instead.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Screaming Frog, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Bulk-deleting the glossary. These pages are often long-standing internal-link hubs and occasionally hold the site's only external links on a topic, and Low value at Low effort is not the same as negative value — consolidation with redirects keeps what they have while removing the fragmentation.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's glossary terms mostly belong inside the feature or treatment page that uses them, with a redirect. Keep standalone only where the definition is genuinely Pabau- or sector-specific, and add the practice-facing next step rather than deleting a page that still earns clicks.

**Apply anywhere:** Glossary terms mostly belong inside the product or topic page that uses them, with a redirect. Keep standalone only where the definition is genuinely brand- or sector-specific, and add the audience-specific next step rather than deleting a page that still earns clicks.

### 7. Follow up on every unbacked claim with four fixed challenges  `134.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 134*

Grow and Convert's rule for blog quality is that bad posts make claims while good posts back claims with details, examples and impact. They warn that experts in interviews mostly just make claims: our product makes it easier to write tests, our product automates time-consuming manual processes. So whenever a claim arrives without backup, the interviewer asks a follow-up immediately, and they give the exact wording: how does your product do that, what problem is that solving, what happens if the person just ignores that problem or uses a workaround. They also recommend playing devil's advocate: why is that easier than the status quo or the competitor, and why can't you just do that with a spreadsheet or whatever the free default is. The effect is to break the expert out of the elevator pitch, at which point they usually describe a specific use case where the value is obvious.

> "you have to ask a follow-up question whenever you hear a claim without any backup"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say these questions typically make the expert switch from the standard pitch to a specific use case where the product's value is clear.

**How to do it**

1. Flag every sentence in the interview that asserts a benefit with no mechanism attached.
2. Ask 'how does your product do that?' and wait for the feature name.
3. Ask 'what problem is that solving?' to get the pain point behind the feature.
4. Ask 'what happens if the person ignores that problem or uses a workaround?' to get the cost of inaction.
5. Play devil's advocate: 'why can't you just do that with a spreadsheet?' or with whatever the free default is.
6. Ask why it beats the named competitor, not why it is good in general.
7. Keep the use case they describe under pressure; that is the paragraph that goes in the article.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
How does your product do that? What problem is that solving? What happens if the person just ignores that problem or uses a workaround?
```

**Pitfall:** Accepting the claim because it sounds reasonable. You then write a paragraph that any competitor could also publish, which is the definition of a bad post under their standard.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's articles should never state a benefit like 'reduces no-shows' without the mechanism, the cost of not fixing it, and why a spreadsheet or a paper diary does not do the same job. Add these four questions to the interview template used before writing a feature-led article.

**Apply anywhere:** Never let a benefit claim through an interview unchallenged. Ask how, what problem, what happens if ignored, and why not a spreadsheet, then write from the use case that surfaces.

### 8. Generic content wins citations but gets you described like everyone else  `82.9`
*core · content insights · source 82*

Grow and Convert make a claim that cuts against pure ranking-first GEO advice. If you publish generic content that echoes what others are already saying, even ranking in LLMs will not help, because the model will describe you the same way it describes everyone else, making readers far less likely to convert. So visibility is necessary but not sufficient. The content has to carry positioning the model can repeat, or you buy your way into a list of five names that all read identically. This is the reason they insist on thorough content articulating who the product is for, its use cases, features and benefits, the pain points it solves, and the differentiators against competitors. The conversion outcome, not the mention count, is what the specificity is protecting.

> "even ranking in LLMs won't help because it will describe you"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated principle, supported by ChatGPT reproducing Level AI's specific semantic-understanding and emotion-detection claims verbatim.

**Pitfall:** Teams optimizing for share of voice will hit the number with commodity roundup content and see no pipeline change, because every recommended name is described identically.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's comparison and category pages should never restate the same feature list every competitor publishes. Each needs the specific claims about all-inclusive subscriptions and aesthetic-practice fit that a model can quote back.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not settle for being mentioned. Publish claims specific enough that a model repeating them separates you from the other names in the same answer.

### 9. Get originality from one hour-long expert interview, not a quote roundup  `128.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 128*

Grow and Convert draw a hard line here. Their Originality Nuggets principle means interviewing a subject-matter expert who can supply insightful original ideas, concepts, tactics and details that distinguish the piece from the other results, and they explicitly say this is not collecting quotes from half a dozen so-called experts for the purpose of getting shares from them. What they mean is an hour-long interview with a single expert whose thinking guides the arguments of the entire piece. The distinction is structural. A roundup decorates an existing outline with attributed sentences and changes nothing about the article's argument. A single deep interview changes the outline itself, because the expert's view of the problem determines what the sections are and what the piece claims.

> "an hour long interview with a single expert who guides the arguments"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert specify an hour-long interview with a single expert who guides the arguments of the whole piece, and reject multi-expert quote roundups gathered for shares.

**How to do it**

1. Pick one subject-matter expert per article who has actually done the thing, not a name with an audience.
2. Book a full hour and treat it as an interview, not a request for a quote.
3. Interview before the outline exists, so their framing can set the sections.
4. Ask for specific tactics, numbers and cases rather than opinions and principles.
5. Build the outline from the interview's arguments and write the piece around them.
6. Attribute their specific claims inline so the originality is visible to a reader skimming the page.
7. Refuse the shortcut of emailing six people for a paragraph each, since that does not change the article's argument.
8. Record which articles were interview-led so you can compare their ranking and link performance against the rest.

**Pitfall:** Sourcing experts for their reach rather than their knowledge. You get quotes and some shares, but the outline stays generic and the piece still reads like every other result.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles should be built on one long interview with a practitioner or an internal expert per piece, conducted before the outline, rather than sprinkling quotes into a finished draft.

**Apply anywhere:** Build originality from one hour-long interview with a single subject-matter expert, conducted before the outline so their framing sets the sections. Multi-expert quote roundups gathered for shares decorate a generic outline without changing what the article argues.

> "nobody was actually answering the question or taking a"

**Evidence:** The article, titled "What is the best position for the PS5?", reached the number-one Google ranking and captured the featured snippet within a few days despite competing against huge media outlets, large Reddit threads, and big competitor sites already ranking for the same query, none of which gave a definitive answer, including Sony's own ambiguous statements.

**Apply anywhere:** Actively hunt for "debated rumor" or ambiguous-answer situations in your own sector — contested claims about a regulation, a competitor feature, or an industry best practice where existing content hedges — and be the source that states a clear, confident, well-supported answer, since search volume already exists around these questions and current top-ranking pages are demonstrably failing to satisfy it.

### 11. Give away the template, then argue why the spreadsheet fails  `132.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 132*

Grow and Convert describe exactly how they made a template keyword convert for Geekbot. The target was 'daily standup Excel template', a search that explicitly wants a non-software answer, which on the face of it is the worst possible fit for selling software. Their handling was to satisfy the intent completely by including a genuinely downloadable Excel template, then to discuss Excel's downsides and argue why Geekbot is the better option. Both halves matter. Withholding the template or gating it fails the intent and the page will not hold rankings. Providing the template and stopping there produces a page that converts nothing. The argument section has to name concrete limits of the manual approach, which is what gives the reader a reason to consider software they were not searching for.

> "we also discuss Excel's downsides, and argue why Geekbot is a better option"

**Evidence:** Geekbot's 'daily standup Excel template' page, part of the top-funnel set that produced 397 conversions in the case study.

**How to do it**

1. Find the template and spreadsheet keywords in your category where searchers want a manual answer.
2. Build the actual asset and make it downloadable on the page without a form gate, so the intent is fully satisfied.
3. Write the how-to-use section for the manual asset first, in enough detail to stand alone.
4. Add a section naming the concrete limits of the manual method: what breaks at scale, what gets missed, what has to be done twice.
5. Map each named limit to the specific product capability that removes it, rather than making a general pitch.
6. Place that comparison after the download, not before it, so the page still reads as delivering what was searched for.
7. Close with a next step that suits someone who has just realized the spreadsheet will not hold, such as a demo or a feature walkthrough.

**Pitfall:** Gating the template behind a form to capture leads is the common instinct and it kills the page. Rankings fall because the intent is not met on the page, and you lose the traffic that would have read the argument.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau already publishes template pages, so this is a direct audit item. Every template page should have the asset downloadable on the page, followed by a section naming what the spreadsheet or paper form cannot do, mapped feature by feature to Pabau. David should check that the download is not gated and that the limits section exists.

**Apply anywhere:** On template and spreadsheet keywords, publish the real asset ungated so the search intent is met, then add a section naming the concrete limits of the manual method and mapping each one to the product capability that removes it. Put the comparison after the download, never in front of it.

### 12. Give every target keyword its own page instead of stacking them on existing pages  `94.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 94*

Grow and Convert's second principle is one unique page per target keyword. They describe a client who had hired three SEO vendors and had a keyword sheet where the homepage was assigned to rank for 'time clock app', 'online time clock', 'employee time tracking' and 'online time and attendance' at once. Those four terms have different search intents and different page-one results, so one page cannot satisfy them, and a homepage is the hardest page to add SEO text to. The same failure showed on blog posts: a post titled '10 tips for helping employees do...' was assigned keywords that were not in the title and whose intent the body did not satisfy, even though the keywords appeared several times in the copy. Their fix is a dedicated page built for one keyword's intent and nothing else.

> "These keywords all have different search intents!"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client had used three SEO vendors and still had a handful of pages assigned to rank for many different target keywords, with very few actually optimized for any one of them.

**How to do it**

1. Export your keyword tracking sheet and add a column for the assigned URL.
2. Flag every URL that appears against more than one target keyword.
3. For each flagged group, search all its keywords and compare the page-one results; different result sets mean different intents.
4. Split the group so each keyword gets its own page, keeping at most one keyword on the existing URL.
5. Check that the assigned keyword actually appears in that page's title; if it does not, the assignment was never real.
6. Write each new page to satisfy only its own keyword's intent, and do not reuse a generic body with the keyword sprinkled in.
7. Leave the homepage out of the keyword map entirely, since you cannot restructure it around a search intent.
8. Re-run the sheet after publishing to confirm no URL is carrying two keywords again.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Vendors who only optimize existing pages produce these stacked maps, because writing new content is outside what they do. The signal is a keyword sheet where the homepage and two feature pages carry a dozen terms.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should audit its keyword map for pages carrying several terms. Each of those terms needs its own /blog/ or /templates/ page written to that intent, rather than another keyword bolted onto an existing feature page.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your keyword map for any URL assigned more than one target keyword. Compare the page-one results for each term, and split them into dedicated pages whenever the results differ.

### 13. Give the product half or more of the body copy on buying-intent posts  `144.2`
*core · best practices · source 144*

Grow and Convert state that when writing content for their clients, significant portions of the copy, over 50 percent, go to describing the features and benefits of the product or service, naming the key value propositions and showing how the client solves the customer's pain points. They argue most blog content only glancingly mentions the product, partly because blogs pick top-of-funnel topics and partly because of a content marketing culture that says do not be salesy. They call that a massive conversion mistake. On buying intent keywords the product discussion is not an intrusion, it is what satisfies the search intent, because the searcher is trying to learn how the product works, whether it fixes their problem and how it differs from alternatives. They add that if you do not discuss your product, someone else will.

> "significant portions of our copy (50%+) are dedicated"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report over 50 percent product copy as their standing practice across client content, contrasted with typical blogs that mention the product only glancingly.

**How to do it**

1. Set a working target for every bottom-funnel article: at least half the word count describes your product's features, benefits and value propositions.
2. Before drafting, write down the customer pain points the article addresses and which specific features answer each one.
3. Structure the body so each pain point section ends in the concrete product mechanism that solves it, not a generic tip.
4. Name the differentiators explicitly, saying how you work differently from the alternatives the reader is also considering.
5. Count product-focused words against total words after drafting; if the product content is under half, expand it rather than trimming the rest.
6. Treat any editorial note asking you to make the piece less salesy on a buying-intent keyword as a request to change intent, and push back with the keyword's SERP as evidence.

**Pitfall:** Applying the same 50 percent rule to a genuinely informational keyword. There the product-heavy copy reads as an advert and drives pogo-sticking, so the rule belongs to buying-intent posts only.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau bottom-funnel pages, especially competitor alternative and comparison articles, should devote at least half their length to how Pabau's scheduling, charting and payments actually work for an aesthetic practice. David should treat a comparison article that mentions Pabau only in the CTA block as unfinished.

**Apply anywhere:** On bottom-funnel pages, especially comparison and alternatives articles, devote at least half the length to how your product actually works for the reader's situation. Treat a comparison article that mentions your product only in the closing CTA as unfinished.

### 14. Google demotes AI content via behavior, not detection  `20.17`
*core · content insights · source 20*

Google reportedly cannot reliably detect AI-generated content directly (detection has significant false positive/negative rates), which is why model providers are pursuing watermarking instead — but Google still effectively penalizes low-value AI content indirectly, through user behavior signals rather than content forensics. The mechanism: when a site suddenly publishes at large scale, Google temporarily applies a provisional site-quality score based on similarity to other known sites, then observes real user behavior after ranking it and demotes the site if engagement is poor. Generative AI content typically shows low time-on-site and high bounce rates by default — but this is a UX/value problem, not an AI-authorship problem, since AI content with genuinely good UX and real value doesn't trigger this pattern and performs fine long term.

> "they leverage the human signals they leverage across everything"

**Evidence:** 'A lot of people just assume that because you use a bunch of AI, Google can detect that and automatically demote you. That's not true... Generative AI content typically has a low time on site and a high bounce rate — but if you have better UX, you won't have this problem.'

**Apply at Pabau:** This reinforces that Pabau's AI-assisted content should be judged and QA'd purely on real user engagement outcomes (time on page, bounce rate, task completion) rather than AI-detection risk — David should treat weak on-page UX/value as the actual mechanism that gets scaled AI content demoted, and prioritize fixing that over any 'sounds too AI' concern.

**Apply anywhere:** This reinforces that your AI-assisted content should be judged and QA'd purely on real user engagement outcomes (time on page, bounce rate, task completion) rather than AI-detection risk — you should treat weak on-page UX/value as the actual mechanism that gets scaled AI content demoted, and prioritize fixing that over any 'sounds too AI' concern.

### 15. Google penalizes thin content, not AI-written content  `07.3`
*core · content insights · source 07*

The creator directly refutes the belief that Google detects and penalizes AI-generated text, calling AI detectors 'complete nonsense.' ### 23. Judge content on the second read, not the first  `174.3`
*core · content insights · source 174*

Khanal's test for mirage content is a reading procedure. AI output, and the human-written content he criticized in 2016, sounds articulate and well-written on the first read. The failure only shows on the second read, when you stop and dissect the arguments and realize the piece is stating the same generic arguments in different ways. Grammatical polish is therefore useless as a quality signal, and it is the signal most editors actually use. His original 2016 diagnosis is that first-page results have interesting titles but, once clicked, all regurgitate versions of the same few talking points. The practical version is to review a draft twice with different questions: does it read well, then separately, what does it actually assert that is not already on page one of the SERP.

> "stop to really dissect the arguments, you realize it's just stating"

**Evidence:** Khanal's Mirage Content diagnosis, first published in 2016 and applied to AI output seven years later: pieces that look well-written on the surface but are high-level fluff on inspection.

**How to do it**

1. Read the draft once for flow, then set it aside without approving it.
2. On the second read, write down every distinct claim the piece makes, one line each.
3. Open the top five ranking results for the target keyword and check each claim against them.
4. Strike any claim that appears in three or more of those five results as non-differentiating.
5. Count what survives; if fewer than two claims survive, send the draft back for original input rather than line editing.
6. Record the surviving claims in the brief so the next writer on the topic does not repeat the same ground.

**Pitfall:** Editors approve drafts because the prose is clean. Clean prose is exactly what AI and inexpert freelancers both produce, so it correlates with nothing. The signal you missed it: the piece is live and you cannot summarize its point without describing the topic.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a second-read step to Pabau's editorial pass. After the fact-check, list the article's claims and compare them with the top five ranking pages for the keyword. If nothing survives that comparison, the piece needs an originality nugget from someone who works with practices, not another line edit.

**Apply anywhere:** Review drafts twice. Read once for flow, then on a second pass list every claim the piece makes and check each against the top five ranking pages. Strike anything three or more of them already say. If fewer than two claims survive, the draft needs original input, not line editing.
