### 1. Settle promotion scope, price and mechanics before signing  `178.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 178*

Grow and Convert treat writing as one facet of content marketing and promotion as the part that actually drives traffic to a new article. So they tell buyers to establish three things during evaluation: whether promotion is included in the service or sold separately, what the promotion concretely consists of, and what will be left for the client to do. They call out the cheapest version specifically. If the agency's promotion is posting the article to your own brand's social channels, that is something you could easily do yourself and should not be priced as a service. The other question is timing. If the agency relies strictly on SEO, ask what drives traffic during the months a page takes to rank. Whatever the agency does not cover becomes your workload, so the answer sizes your internal effort as well as the invoice.

> "to help drive traffic to articles during the time it takes for"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert note that sharing content on the client's own social channels is something the client could easily do themselves, so it should not count as agency promotion.

**How to do it**

1. Ask whether content promotion is inside the retainer or billed separately, and get the number.
2. Ask them to list the specific promotion actions per article, not the category.
3. Flag any answer that amounts to posting on your own social accounts and price it at zero.
4. Ask what drives traffic to a page in the months before it ranks.
5. Ask who pays for any paid amplification: the agency's budget or an extra ad spend line from you.
6. Write the leftover promotion work into your own team's plan before signing.
7. Set a per-article promotion checklist in the contract so it is auditable later.

**Pitfall:** Discovering after signing that promotion was excluded. The blog fills up, nothing ranks yet, nobody is driving traffic, and the engagement looks like a failure when it is a scope gap.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, any external content spend should be quoted with promotion named as a line item, and David should assume the first three months of a new cluster need non-organic traffic from Pabau's own channels.

**Apply anywhere:** Before signing, get promotion in writing: included or extra, the exact actions per article, who funds any paid amplification, and what drives traffic while pages are still climbing.

### 2. Share every new piece internally and by email, but expect only a spike  `102.12`
*useful · best practices · source 102*

Grow and Convert split content promotion into three categories, existing audience, paid channels and SEO, and say the most effective strategies use all three. For the existing audience they prescribe a fixed publish-day routine: share the piece throughout your own company, send it to your email list if you have one, and post it organically on your social pages. These are the quickest and cheapest ways to get eyes on a piece. Their caveat matters more than the tactic: this does not produce sustainable traffic growth. You get a spike when you promote and very little afterward. Treating that spike as evidence a piece is working is a common misreading, and it is why they keep SEO as the channel that carries long-term traffic and conversions.

> "you will get a spike when you promote the piece"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state plainly that audience promotion will not produce sustainable traffic growth, only a spike.

**How to do it**

1. Write a publish-day checklist covering internal share, email list send and organic social posts.
2. Send the internal share to sales and support with a line on which customer question the piece answers.
3. Post organically to the pages where your audience actually is, rather than all of them.
4. Log the promotion date so the traffic spike can be excluded when you later judge organic performance.
5. Read the piece's traffic again 30 and 90 days after publication, with the spike excluded, to judge it on search.
6. Do not commission more of a format because its launch spike was large.

**Pitfall:** Judging a piece on its launch week. Audience promotion produces a spike with almost no tail, so a strong launch says nothing about whether the piece will rank or convert.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep a publish-day share routine but judge every article on its 30 and 90 day organic numbers with the launch spike excluded, so editorial decisions are not driven by newsletter traffic.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep a fixed publish-day routine of internal share, email send and organic social posts, but log the date and judge the piece on its 30 and 90 day organic numbers with the spike removed.

### 3. Ship an MVP blog on a cheap theme before the custom build  `123.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 123*

Hyam describes the sequencing at ThinkApps as staged rather than waiting for perfect. They first built an MVP version of the blog on WordPress, bought a $40 theme, and started publishing content, while a full-site redesign and a custom-designed blog were being built in the background. The custom property, BuildBlog by ThinkApps, then launched with the mega project. His side note adds a specific warning: you can install a free theme from WordPress.com to start, but be cautious about which web hosting company you pick, because migrating to a new provider later can be a huge hassle. So the cheap decision that is safe is the theme, and the decision worth care up front is the host.

> "we bought a $40 WordPress theme and started publishing some content"

**Evidence:** ThinkApps published on a $40 theme while the custom BuildBlog design was in development, then used the finished design's launch as the moment for the Apple Watch mega project.

**How to do it**

1. Stand up WordPress with a free or cheap theme and start publishing rather than waiting for a design.
2. Choose the hosting provider carefully at this stage, since Hyam flags migrating hosts later as a major hassle.
3. Publish enough content during the MVP phase that the custom launch has an archive behind it.
4. Run the redesign in the background on a staging site rather than pausing publication.
5. Keep the same domain and URL structure across the MVP and the custom build so the archive keeps its equity.
6. Save the flagship launch asset for the custom design's go-live rather than spending it on the MVP.
7. Redirect any URLs the redesign changes, and check indexing after the switch.

**Tools:** WordPress, WordPress.com

**Pitfall:** Locking into a host chosen on price during the MVP phase. Hyam warns migrating providers later is a huge hassle, and the cost shows up exactly when traffic is growing and downtime matters.

**Apply at Pabau:** This maps to how Pabau should treat new page types: publish a small set on the existing template and only invest in a custom template once the format proves out, keeping URLs stable across the change.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish on a cheap or free theme while the real design is built in the background, pick the hosting provider carefully because migrating later is painful, keep URLs stable across the switch, and save the flagship launch asset for the finished design's go-live.

### 4. Skip education and nurture on differentiator keywords, sell immediately  `94.9`
*useful · content insights · source 94*

Grow and Convert make a specific claim about who is searching a differentiator-qualified term. If you sell codeless, visual, automated QA testing for websites and mobile apps and someone Googles exactly that, they do not need to be made problem aware or solution aware, they do not need educating about the space, and they do not need dripping with content on an email list for months. The act of typing the term proves they are actively looking for a solution like yours right now. This changes what the page has to do. The standard content-marketing structure that opens with the problem, explains the category and eventually mentions the product is wasted length here, and it delays the answer that would keep the searcher on the page.

> "need to be made "problem aware" or "solution aware"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert argue searchers on terms like 'codeless test automation' are ready to buy, and report their QA client ranking in positions 1-3 across a set of such terms.

**How to do it**

1. Classify each target keyword by whether the qualifier proves the searcher already knows what they want.
2. For keywords that pass, cut any planned section that explains what the category is or why the problem matters.
3. Open the page with the direct answer, then move straight to product fit.
4. Replace the email-capture lead magnet CTA with a demo or trial CTA on these pages.
5. Reserve the problem-aware, educational structure for genuinely broad terms, and do not mix the two on one page.

**Pitfall:** Writers keep a long educational introduction because the brief template has one. On a differentiator keyword that intro pushes the product section below the fold and reads as if you did not understand the query.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's bottom-of-funnel articles should not open with what practice management software is. On a term that already names the buyer's situation, answer and move to the product.

**Apply anywhere:** When the keyword's qualifier proves the searcher already knows what they want, cut the category explainer, answer immediately and move to product fit with a demo CTA rather than an email capture.

### 5. Sort the content job into brand-building or lead-driving before hiring  `101.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 101*

Grow and Convert open by splitting outsourcing needs into two buckets. Bucket one uses content as a marketing channel measured on traffic, leads and sales. Bucket two uses it for thought leadership and brand building, with no metric attached: CEO ghostwriting, story articles for social. Their point is that the two need different hires. Bucket two needs a good writer who can carry your perspective in your brand voice, and no SEO strategy at all. Bucket one needs ideation, SEO, promotion and attribution alongside writing. Sorting first stops you from applying a lead-generation vetting process to a ghostwriting role, or hiring a stylist to run a revenue channel. They add that even for bucket two, finding a good writer is hard and comes down to having a filtering process.

> "they typically fall into one of two buckets"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert filtered 1000+ writer applicants and tested hundreds over seven years.

**How to do it**

1. Write down which bucket the work belongs to before drafting a job description.
2. For bucket two, test only for voice: commission a paid sample in your founder's voice.
3. For bucket two, drop SEO and keyword requirements from the spec entirely.
4. For bucket one, run the five-step vetting process on goal, ideas, expertise, promotion and reporting.
5. Never let one hire carry both buckets unless they demonstrably have both skill sets.
6. Report each bucket against its own measure: brand work on reach and reception, marketing content on leads.

**Pitfall:** Judging a ghostwriter on keyword strategy, or a content marketer on prose style, gets you the wrong person and a bad review of good work.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's founder or clinical thought-leadership posts should be commissioned separately from the search-driven template and blog pages, with different writers and different success measures.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide whether a piece of content work is brand building or a measured marketing channel before you write the job spec. The first needs a voice-matching writer; the second needs strategy, SEO, promotion and attribution too.

### 6. Source head-to-head comparison content from sales and competitive intelligence interviews  `130.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 130*

Grow and Convert write their 'our client vs competitor' pages as long-form pieces walking through the client's strengths and unique differentiators against that specific competitor, and they name the source: interviews with the client's sales, product, or competitive intelligence teams. That is a deliberate choice over reading the competitor's website or its review profile. Sales staff know which objections come up in live deals, product staff know which limitations are real rather than marketed, and competitive intelligence teams hold the battlecards. The result is a comparison that names differences a competitor's own marketing would never surface, which is also what makes the page hard for a third-party listicle to outrank on depth.

> "based on interviews with their sales, product, or competitive intelligence teams"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: 11 comparison pages in the top 3 and 14 on page 1, with significant demo signups despite low search volume.

**How to do it**

1. Ask sales which competitors they lose to and win against most often, and take that list over a tool-generated one.
2. Run a 30-45 minute interview per competitor with a salesperson who has run deals against it.
3. Ask specifically which objections the competitor raises about you, and which of its limitations kill its deals.
4. Interview a product person to check each claimed limitation is currently true, not fixed in the last release.
5. Request any existing battlecards from competitive intelligence and reconcile them with the interviews.
6. Write one head-to-head page per competitor from those notes, organized by the differentiators sales actually uses.
7. Send the draft back to the interviewee for a factual check before publishing.
8. Re-interview annually, since competitor limitations get fixed and the page becomes wrong.

**Pitfall:** Writing comparisons from the competitor's website produces a feature table anyone could build, and it goes stale silently. The signal is a comparison page sales never sends to prospects because it does not match how they pitch.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's versus pages should come from interviews with Pabau's sales team, not from competitor websites, and each should be re-checked yearly because competitor limitations get fixed. Route drafts back to sales for a factual check before publishing.

**Apply anywhere:** Build each head-to-head comparison from interviews with your own sales and product teams rather than from the competitor's website. Ask what objections come up in live deals and which limitations actually lose them business, verify the limitations are still true, then have the interviewee check the draft.

### 7. Split an interview subject into 10-15 posts before booking the interview  `98.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 98*

Hyam's fix for an interview-format content plan. Luke Ryan of Mokriya, an enterprise software developer, proposed 'How SanDisk Created The Best File Management Software In The World' as an interview post with a marquee client. Hyam liked the idea as a read but said the interview itself would be difficult to conduct, because there are too many pieces and no obvious starting point. So they took the high-level concept and broke it into 10-15 separate post ideas: how SanDisk conducted user research and what it led to, each of the seven core principles of UX and how they approached them, how the designer built wireframes with the user in mind, how to design an app to a large company's brand guidelines, and how SanDisk ran user testing and what they changed. Narrowing first made the interview questions easy to write, made each post solve one internal pain point, and made each piece promotable to a different audience.

> "We took the high-level concept and broke it out into 10-15 different ideas"

**Evidence:** Mokriya's SanDisk interview premise broke into 10-15 post ideas covering user research, the seven core UX principles, wireframing, brand guideline constraints and user testing.

**How to do it**

1. Write the broad interview premise down, then refuse to book the interview against it.
2. Break the premise into 10-15 sub-topics covering each stage of the process the subject went through.
3. Check each sub-topic against a pain point another company in your audience would have internally.
4. Write the interview question set per sub-topic, which is now easy because the scope is defined.
5. Run one interview session but structure it as a sequence of sub-topic blocks, not a free conversation.
6. Publish one post per sub-topic rather than one long interview write-up.
7. Match each published post to a different distribution audience, for example UX groups, UI groups and product development groups.

**Pitfall:** A broad interview premise produces vague questions, which produce vague answers, which produce a post that reads as a company profile rather than something a reader can act on. You cannot fix that in the edit.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's customer interviews should be scoped this way. Instead of one 'how this med spa grew' piece, plan separate posts on how they set up their consent workflow, how they cut no-shows, and how they moved from paper records, and write the interview questions per post.

**Apply anywhere:** Before booking a customer or expert interview, split the premise into 10-15 process-stage sub-topics and write your questions per sub-topic. One interview then yields several specific posts, each promotable to a different community.

### 8. Split jobs-to-be-done keywords into buying and non-buying halves  `104.11`
*useful · concrete actions · source 104*

Grow and Convert define jobs-to-be-done keywords as queries where the searcher wants to solve a problem but is not yet searching for the solution by name, examples being 'how to transfer money securely', 'how to get signatures remotely', 'track invoices and payments' and 'meet CMMC compliance standards'. These are almost always long-tail. But in their conversion study the JTBD category split into two clearly different halves. 'Best tools for building a deck' carries buying intent because the searcher has accepted they need a product. 'How to build a deck' does not, because the searcher intends to do it themselves. When they compared conversion rates across those two buckets, the buying-intent half converted at a much higher rate. So JTBD is not one priority tier; it is two, and only one of them belongs near the front of the calendar.

> "'best tools for building a deck' vs 'how to build a deck'"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's comparison across 95+ articles found JTBD posts with high buying intent converted at a much higher rate than JTBD posts without it.

**How to do it**

1. Collect JTBD candidates as action and 'how to' phrases describing the problem your product solves.
2. Take the exact wording from sales and customer success interviews rather than paraphrasing it.
3. Split the list into two buckets: queries implying the searcher will buy a tool, and queries implying they will do it themselves.
4. Treat 'best tools for', 'software for', 'services for' and 'how to X without Y' as buying-intent markers.
5. Treat pure 'how to' and DIY instructions as the non-buying bucket.
6. Verify each classification against the SERP, checking whether tool listicles or step-by-step tutorials rank.
7. Schedule the buying-intent bucket first and hold the DIY bucket for later or move it to video and social.
8. When you do write a DIY post, name your product as one route through the job rather than treating it as a lead page.

**Tools:** Google Search

**Pitfall:** Treating the whole JTBD category as one tier because it is high-volume and easy to rank for. The DIY half fills the blog with traffic that never asks for a demo, which is exactly the outcome pain-point SEO exists to avoid.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, 'how to run a clinic consultation' is the DIY half and 'best software for clinic consultations' is the buying half. David should front-load the second and let the first sit lower on the calendar.

**Apply anywhere:** Divide jobs-to-be-done keywords into queries where the searcher expects to buy a tool and queries where they expect to do the work themselves. Build the first bucket first; the second converts far worse even at higher volume.

### 9. Split the writer role from the strategist role before you hire  `167.14`
*useful · best practices · source 167*

Grow and Convert say expecting a writer to be a content marketing strategist is a mistake they see constantly: companies hire a content writer and then ask them to take on every role from SEO to data analysis. They define the split. A content strategist, usually titled content marketing manager or SEO manager, owns long-term organic traffic: SEO, keyword research, rankings and analytics. A writer owns the article itself. Most people are not good at both, and if you find someone who is, do not expect to pay them a writer's rate. They enumerate the strategist's four duties: picking keywords and topics, coordinating subject-matter expert interviews, being the final pass on article quality, proofreading and on-page SEO, and measuring results as leads ascribed to each piece. Almost no freelance writer will open content as a channel single-handedly.

> "Being a final pass on article quality, proofreading, and on-page SEO."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert list the strategist's four duties and say most people are not good at both roles, and those who are cost more than a writer rate.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the four strategist duties: keyword and topic selection, expert interview coordination, final quality and on-page pass, and results measurement.
2. Decide who owns each before posting a writer role, and accept that the answer may be you.
3. Write the writer job description to cover drafting only, and price it accordingly.
4. If you need both roles in one person, budget significantly above a writer rate and expect a long search.
5. Hire or assign a content marketing manager or SEO consultant for the strategy half rather than stretching the writer.
6. Measure the strategist on leads attributed to each piece, not on articles published.
7. Revisit the split when output grows, since the strategy work scales faster than drafting.

**Pitfall:** Hiring one writer and calling it a content channel. Grow and Convert are blunt that almost no freelance writer opens content as a channel alone, and the symptom is published articles with no keyword rationale and no measurement.

**Apply at Pabau:** David already holds the strategist duties for Pabau, so any writer commissioned for pabau.com should be scoped to drafting from a supplied keyword and interview, with measurement staying in house.

**Apply anywhere:** Separate the strategist role from the writer role before hiring. Someone must own keyword selection, expert interviews, the final on-page pass and lead measurement, and it is usually not the writer.

### 10. Staff content with three to five roles, not one generalist hire  `180.8`
*useful · best practices · source 180*

Grow and Convert argue against the single content hire on capability grounds. Effective content marketing needs strategy, writing, promotion and design, and one person rarely holds all four. Companies typically hire just a writer or just a strategist and discover later that they need more than one person to execute. Their own client accounts run three to five people with different specialisms: content strategists, writers, a paid ads specialist, a project manager and a designer. They also flag a cultural obstacle to hiring in-house for signup-driven content: most content marketers believe content exists for traffic and brand awareness, so that is how they operate, which makes it hard to find one who has developed a strategy aimed at product signups and can write pieces that both rank and sell.

> "3 to 5 people with different areas of expertise"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert deploy content strategists, writers, a paid ads specialist, a project manager and a designer per client account.

**How to do it**

1. List the four functions your content program actually needs: strategy, writing, promotion, design.
2. Score any single candidate honestly against all four before writing the job spec.
3. If one hire is the only option, decide which two functions they own and where the other two come from.
4. Contract the missing functions out rather than assuming the hire will grow into them.
5. When interviewing for the strategy seat, ask specifically for a program they built around product signups, not traffic.
6. Treat 'content drives awareness' as the default answer and probe past it, since most of the field holds that belief.
7. Add a project manager once you are publishing more than a few pieces a month, since scheduling is what fails first.

**Pitfall:** Hiring a strong writer and expecting strategy, distribution and design to follow. The output is good prose that nobody sees and that does not map to a keyword plan.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content work already spans strategy, writing, visuals and technical SEO. David should treat those as four named roles in the process, even when automated, and check no stage is silently skipped because one person owns everything.

**Apply anywhere:** Content programs need strategy, writing, promotion and design. One hire rarely covers all four, so define which functions a single hire owns and source the rest rather than hoping they grow into the job.

### 11. Staff five roles before committing to an SEO content strategy  `107.18`
*useful · best practices · source 107*

Grow and Convert's fourth strategy decision covers who creates the content, in-house or outsourced. The takeaway they emphasize is a resourcing one: an SEO strategy needs a team covering keyword research, content writing, technical SEO, analytics and web development. They say it is unrealistic to expect one or even two marketers to handle all of it, especially at volume. The decision that matters is therefore not the in-house versus outsourced label but whether all five functions are actually covered by someone competent. This connects directly to their cadence advice: a thinly staffed team that commits to high volume will drop the technical, analytics and research work first, because writing is the only part with a visible deliverable.

> "keyword research, content writing, technical SEO, analytics, and web development"

**How to do it**

1. List the five functions and name the person responsible for each, in-house or contracted.
2. Mark any function with no named owner as a gap and either hire, contract or cut scope.
3. Set publishing volume to what the thinnest-staffed function can support, usually research or technical.
4. If one marketer owns three or more functions, reduce cadence rather than expecting all five to happen.
5. Review the assignment whenever volume is increased, because the gaps only appear under load.
6. Judge an agency proposal by which of the five functions it actually covers, not by its deliverable count.

**Pitfall:** When one or two people own everything, keyword research and analytics quietly stop happening because writing is the only function with a visible weekly output. The result is volume against unvalidated keywords.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the technical and analytics functions sit outside the writing process. David should confirm each of the five has a named owner before raising the publishing cadence.

**Apply anywhere:** Name an owner for keyword research, writing, technical SEO, analytics and web development before you set a publishing cadence, and set the cadence to what the weakest-staffed function supports.

### 12. Stop ad spend on articles that lose on Facebook and let them live on SEO  `128.3`
*useful · concrete actions · source 128*

Grow and Convert are explicit that paid promotion is a filter, not a subsidy. Once an article has been through the test slot and shows neither conversions nor low cost, they stop spending on it entirely rather than trying to rescue it with more creative. Their reasoning is that those pieces can still earn their keep through channels that cost nothing per visit: organic search over the longer term, organic sharing, or the client's email list. The discipline this creates is that ad budget concentrates on a small set of proven articles in an ongoing campaign, while the test slot keeps cycling new pieces to look for the next winner. It also means a poor Facebook result is not treated as a verdict on the article's SEO value, only on its paid economics.

> "If the articles don't perform well on Facebook, we don't spend advertising dollars on them."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert stop advertising articles that fail the Facebook test and rely on SEO, sharing or email for those pieces instead.

**How to do it**

1. Define the conversion event per client up front, such as free trial signup or lead form fill, and make sure it attributes to the article.
2. Give each new article a fixed test budget in the rotation slot rather than an open-ended one.
3. At the end of the test, keep only articles that produced conversions and a low cost per landing page view.
4. Move those into a continuous promotion campaign and leave the budget there.
5. Cut spend on the rest completely instead of iterating creative on them.
6. Route the cut articles to email newsletter sends and to the organic and link-building track.
7. Keep the test slot filled with new pieces every month so the winner pool keeps growing.
8. Re-test a cut article only if you have rewritten it or the SERP has changed.

**Tools:** Facebook

**Pitfall:** Treating a poor paid result as proof the article is bad. Some pieces convert well from search and badly from a cold feed, so cutting the ad spend should not mean cutting the SEO investment.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau promotes blog and template pages with paid social, David should cut spend on non-converters after the test window and keep them in the internal-linking and refresh queue rather than pushing more budget at them.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat paid content promotion as a filter. Give each article a fixed test budget, keep spending only on the ones that convert cheaply, and let the rest earn traffic through search, sharing and email instead of iterating ad creative on losers.

### 13. Stop agonizing over in-house versus outsourced content  `101.9`
*useful · general insights · source 101*

Grow and Convert's second misconception is the amount of energy companies spend on whether to hire in-house or outsource. Their position is that the decision barely matters next to three questions: does the person or agency have a good strategy, are they a fit for the type of content you want, and do they have a proven track record of driving the results you want. They say finding great writers, strategists and agencies is genuinely hard, so if you find a good one, take them, whichever side of the line they sit on. The one real difference they concede is that outside agencies usually have workflows ready and will get production running faster. For a long-term investment like content, they call that difference trivial compared to whether the hire is actually any good.

> "It doesn't actually matter that much whether they're outsourced or in-house"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert have tested hundreds of writers over seven years of running their agency.

**Pitfall:** Months spent debating the employment model while the real constraint, finding someone with a proven strategy and track record, goes unaddressed.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau needs more content capacity, David should run one search for proven strategy and track record and accept whichever comes first, in-house or agency, rather than settling the model first.

**Apply anywhere:** The in-house versus outsourced question matters far less than strategy quality, content-type fit and track record. Agencies ramp faster, but over a multi-year investment that head start is trivial.

### 14. Stop expecting the founder to write unique details into the brief  `126.18`
*useful · best practices · source 126*

Grow and Convert answer their own objection about whether you could just put original details in the brief. Yes you could, they say, but will you? Are you including that level of detail now, and if you are the writer, are your clients including it? Definitely not. The reason is that it takes time and effort, and most managers or founders do not want to spend it, figuring this is what they are hiring writers to do. They list the questions that would actually produce the good details: what do we have to add that is interesting here, do we have anything original to say, does our software help the customer do their tasks in a unique way versus competitors, does our agency do paid social differently, is our creative better, what is our stance. Their point is that answering these takes real mental effort and sometimes a lot of time to explain the nuance, so it almost never lands in a brief. An interview extracts it instead.

> "most managers or founders don't want to spend that time"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say this level of detail is almost never in content briefs because it takes work to provide the arguments, and moved to interviews for that reason.

**How to do it**

1. Stop asking the founder to write detail into a document.
2. Book a recorded call instead and ask the questions out loud.
3. Ask what we have to add that is interesting to this discussion, and whether we have anything original to say.
4. Ask specifically whether the product helps the customer do the task differently from every competitor, and how.
5. Ask what the company's stance is on the topic, and push until you get a claim rather than a description.
6. Have someone else turn the answers into the brief so the founder's only cost is the call.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
What do we have to add that's interesting to this discussion? Do we have anything original to say? Is it that our software helps the customer do their tasks in a unique way vs. all of our competitors? What is our stance here?
```

**Pitfall:** Sending the founder a brief template to fill in. It comes back thin or not at all, and the team concludes the founder does not care rather than that writing is the wrong extraction method.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not ask its founders or product leads to fill in brief fields. Book a 30-minute recorded call per cluster and ask the four questions above, then have the content team build the briefs from the transcript.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not ask executives to write unique detail into a brief; they will not. Book a short recorded call and ask what you have to add, whether you have anything original to say, how the product does the task differently, and what your stance is.

### 15. Stop losing ideas that die for lack of a designer or developer  `88.8`
*useful · content insights · source 88*

The author points at a specific waste pattern: great ideas that died in a Google Doc because there was no way to build them. Creative marketers had the ideas but needed other stakeholders, so anything costly or resource-intensive never shipped. He now treats this as solved, if you can dream it you can build it. The useful part for a content team is diagnostic. Most teams have a written record of these dead ideas in old briefs, Slack threads and planning docs, and that archive is now a build queue rather than a graveyard. It is cheaper to mine than to generate new ideas, because those ideas were already judged interesting by someone who knew the audience.

> "the great ideas would die in a Google Doc"

**How to do it**

1. Search old planning docs, briefs and Slack for ideas marked as too expensive, parked or needs design.
2. List each with the reason it was killed and mark the ones killed purely on resource.

5. Ship the two cheapest prototypes first to prove the pipeline works.
6. Add the pattern to the intake process so future ideas are logged with their blocking component named.

**Pitfall:** Reviving an old idea without rechecking demand. An idea parked two years ago may now be answered directly in AI results or covered by three competitors, so re-validate intent before building.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's brief archive and rejected outlines are a build queue. Check each against current SERPs before reviving, since the two-bar rule still applies: it must fit searcher intent and carry a unique angle.

**Apply anywhere:** Mine your archive of ideas that were killed for lack of design or dev time, name the one component that blocked each, and prototype it with AI. Re-validate demand before building.

### 18. Test content tools on matched publisher groups before buying  `29.7`
*useful · best practices · source 29*

When Raptive needed to pick a content-optimization tool for its 5,000-plus publisher network, Hank ran a controlled three-product study rather than choosing by reputation: he tested Surfer, a product called Topic, and a third contender, assigning 10 publishers to use each tool exclusively when updating existing content. The critical design choice was the comparison baseline - performance was measured against similarly-situated peer publishers who used none of the tools, not just a before/after on the same pages. Topic won the test outright, and the result was strong enough that Raptive acquired the product and had Hank's team lead its integration. The lesson generalizes to a single-site team like Pabau's: even without a publisher network, you can approximate this by assigning matched sets of articles to competing tools (or a no-tool control group) before committing budget to one platform.

> "I had 10 publishers using each one, sending me back"

**How to do it**

1. Shortlist 2-3 competing content-optimization tools you're evaluating (Hank's case: Surfer, Topic, and a third product).
2. Assign a matched group of pages, articles, or sites to each tool so every tool gets a comparable test sample (Hank used 10 publishers per tool).
3. Have each group use only its assigned tool's workflow when updating existing content during the test window.
4. Define your comparison baseline as similarly-situated peers using none of the tools, not just a before/after on the same pages.
5. Measure each tool's actual content-update performance lift against that no-tool peer baseline, not against the vendor's own claimed benchmarks.
6. Collect results centrally from every participant rather than relying on self-reported success.
7. Select the winning tool based on measured lift versus the peer baseline.
8. Roll the winning tool out fully only after the controlled test concludes, rather than committing budget upfront on reputation alone.

**Tools:** Surfer SEO, Topic

**Pitfall:** Choosing a content-optimization tool by reputation or vendor demo instead of a controlled test against a real peer baseline risks paying for a tool that doesn't actually outperform doing nothing.

### 19. The daily-content format problem: tips, then case studies, then reviews  `57.14`

### 20. Tie every feature in the body back to the pain in the intro  `91.8`
*useful · best practices · source 91*

Grow and Convert say that once angle, title and intro are right, the rest of the post is straightforward: complete the story and show how the product is designed to solve the problems you laid out. Somewhere in the body you get into the details of the product, its features, how it works, how it helps and results it has produced for customers. They are explicit that there is always a heavy sales aspect to a disruption story, which is the whole point of the format. But the constraint is that it remains a story, so features and benefits have to be constantly tied back to the problems set out in the introduction. They point at InnovationCast's and LAWCLERK's stories as examples of a body that keeps the story tone while selling. They call it an art and a finesse rather than a template, which is also why they warn against handing it to a generalist writer.

> "there is always a heavy "sales" aspect to disruption stories"

**Evidence:** InnovationCast's 'Innovation Management at most companies is broken. Here is how we are fixing it.' and LAWCLERK's story, cited as bodies that keep story tone while selling.

**How to do it**

1. List the pains named in your intro as a checklist before writing the body.
2. Introduce product details, features and mechanics only in service of one of those pains.
3. Open each feature section by restating the pain it answers, in the same words used in the intro.
4. Include customer results and outcomes as proof that the pain was actually removed.
5. Delete any feature paragraph that cannot be mapped to a listed pain.
6. Keep narrative voice throughout: first person, what we did and why, rather than spec-sheet phrasing.
7. Do a final pass reading only the section openers to confirm they still read as one story.

**Pitfall:** The body drifts into a product tour halfway down, and the reader who was nodding along at the intro stops. The signal is section headings that name features instead of problems.

**Apply at Pabau:** On Pabau positioning pieces, head each body section with the practice problem rather than the Pabau module name, then show the feature underneath. This also fits the house rule that a Pabau feature image caption must say how the feature helps the reader.

**Apply anywhere:** Sell hard in the body, but restate the pain at the top of each feature section so the piece stays a story. Cut any feature paragraph you cannot map back to a problem named in the intro.

### 21. Tier your calendar into light, medium and heavy originality  `100.16`
*useful · best practices · source 100*

Grow and Convert's practical contribution is a difficulty ladder rather than a single quality bar. Light originality needs a dash of creativity and a willingness not to be a me-too marketer: a contrarian angle, or a fresh way of arranging a list. Medium needs solid original ideas of your own, or an interview with someone who has them. Heavy means the work happens before the writing, like a scientist writing a paper after two years of lab work, and by definition those pieces also qualify as Skyscraper or 10X articles. They say the versatility is what makes the framework teachable to agency writers, because a writer can ask 'how can I add some light originality' on a niche term without setting off on a six-month endeavor. Planning a calendar in these tiers keeps the mix honest and stops every piece being budgeted the same.

> "this framework let's you think of content pieces in tiers of difficulty"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own tiering: light examples ranking #2 and #4, a medium interview post drawing 6,000 visitors, and a heavy study ranking #1 for two terms.

**How to do it**

1. Label every planned piece light, medium or heavy at the point it enters the calendar.
2. Assign light pieces to a contrarian angle or a new arrangement of an existing format.
3. Assign medium pieces either an in-house original take or a booked interview.
4. Reserve heavy pieces for a dataset, a study or a coded tool, and schedule the work before the writing.
5. Cap the number of heavy pieces in flight so they do not stall the calendar.
6. Match the tier to keyword competitiveness rather than to the writer's preference.
7. Review quarterly whether the heavy pieces earned their extra cost in leads and links.

**Pitfall:** Treating every piece as heavy stalls output; treating every piece as light means nothing ever earns links. The signal is a calendar where all pieces have the same budget.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should tag each Pabau brief with a tier. Template and code-reference pages sit in light, practice-management guides in medium with a clinic interview, and one heavy piece a quarter such as a clinic-website study or a calculator.

**Apply anywhere:** Tag every planned piece light, medium or heavy and budget accordingly. Match the tier to keyword competitiveness, and cap how many heavy pieces run at once.

### 22. Transcribe the client interview with AI but keep the argument yourself  `124.4`
*useful · ai workflows · source 124*

Grow and Convert surveyed 50+ marketers on how they use AI in writing and found improving writing was the second most common use case. Their own AI recommendation for the research stage is narrow: record the client interview and upload it to an AI tool for summarization, letting it return a structured outline or summary of key topics. The point is to remove the re-watching and manual transcription, not to hand over the thinking. They are explicit that you keep control over the article's argument and structure. They also note the rest of their tips survive AI use, because you can upload a draft and ask the tool to apply a specific edit, such as reorganizing thoughts into bullet points or getting to the value of the sentence faster.

> "consider recording your client interview and uploading it into an AI"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's survey of 50+ marketers on AI writing use found improving writing was the second most common use case.

**How to do it**

1. Record every client interview with the subject's permission.
2. Upload the recording or transcript to an AI tool and ask for a structured summary of key topics only.
3. Do not ask the tool for the outline or the angle; write those yourself from the summary.
4. Pull direct quotes from the transcript rather than from the AI summary, so the wording stays the expert's.
5. After drafting, upload the draft and ask for one named edit at a time, such as reorganize this section into bullet points.
6. Run a second pass asking the tool to move the value earlier in each sentence.
7. Review every AI edit against the questionnaire, and revert anything that flattens the specific detail.
8. Keep the transcript on file so a fact-check can trace each claim to the interview.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Summarize this client interview and give me a structured summary of the key topics discussed.
```

**Pitfall:** Letting the tool produce the outline as well as the summary. You then edit inside a structure the model chose, and the specificity the interview bought gets averaged away.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's interview recordings should go through AI only for transcription and topic summary. The outline for a Pabau article stays human, and the AI passes are limited to named edits such as bulleting a section or tightening sentences.

**Apply anywhere:** Use AI to transcribe and topic-summarize your expert interviews so you never re-watch a recording, but write the outline and argument yourself. Then use it for single named edits on the draft, one instruction at a time.
