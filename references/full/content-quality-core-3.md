### 1. Decide ranking first, then conversion, when picking a page format  `146.4`
*core · best practices · source 146*

Grow and Convert's ordering rule is blunt: if your page does not rank, its conversion rate is irrelevant. So format selection starts with which page type has a realistic shot at the top three, not which type converts best in theory. A blog post gives more room to include relevant keywords and answer the searcher's intent, so it is usually easier to rank than a thin product landing page. That is why they almost always target bottom-of-funnel keywords with posts. The conversion argument only becomes decisive between two formats that can both plausibly rank. They also note that positions one to three take roughly 60% of organic clicks, so even a lower-converting post in position one can produce more conversions than a better-converting landing page sitting further down.

> "And ranking needs to take priority first"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite roughly 60% of organic traffic going to positions one to three as the reason a top-ranked list post can beat a better-converting landing page on total conversions.

**How to do it**

1. For each bottom-of-funnel keyword, first ask which page format can realistically reach the top three on that SERP.
2. Rule out any format your site has no authority to rank in that position, regardless of its conversion record.
3. Among the formats that can rank, then pick the one with the better conversion record.
4. Estimate conversions as expected traffic times conversion rate, using roughly 60% of clicks going to positions one to three.
5. Accept a lower-converting format when it wins a top-three position and the higher-converting one would land on page two.
6. Revisit the decision once the page ranks, since a ranked page can be restructured for conversion later.

**Pitfall:** Optimizing format for conversion rate on a keyword you cannot rank for. The page ships polished, sits at position 14, and converts almost nobody.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau targets a term like practice management software for a vertical, David should check whether pabau.com can realistically rank a product page there. If not, the /blog/ article is the right vehicle and the product page can come later.

**Apply anywhere:** When targeting a competitive product term, check whether your domain can realistically rank a product page there. If not, publish the article version and add the product page later.

### 2. Declare that your listicle is vendor-written rather than faking neutrality  `72.22`
*core · best practices · source 72*

Khanal reviews a SERP pattern where ranking pages lean heavily on first-person testing and personal experience, and refuses to imitate it dishonestly. His rule is do not be deceitful and do not pretend this is some unbiased thing: say you are the vendor and say what you like. He pairs this with the honest framing of the intro, where he states his view of the space and lets readers self-select. He also names the objection to self-promotional listicles directly, that people say they get you penalized, and gives his counter: his agency has published them for ten years with stable rankings, and nobody being penalized is writing intros at that level of authenticity. The declaration is what makes the format defensible, not a disguise of neutrality.

> "don't pretend this is some unbiased thing"

**Evidence:** Khanal reports Grow and Convert has run self-promotional listicles for ten years with stable rankings and no client penalized.

**How to do it**

1. Decide whether you have genuinely tested every product you list, and do not claim testing you did not do.
2. State in the intro that you make one of the products listed, in plain words.
3. Frame the evaluation criteria as your view of the space rather than as objective standards.
4. Place your own product in the list where the criteria honestly put it, not automatically first.
5. Keep verifiable facts about competitors sourced to their own sites, and label opinions as opinions.
6. Invite the reader to disagree with your framing and leave, rather than hiding the bias.
7. Track rankings on these pages over time rather than assuming the format is either safe or doomed.

**Pitfall:** Copying the SERP's first-person testing convention without doing the testing. Khanal saw heavy emphasis on personal experience across the ranking pages and explicitly refused to fake it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's 'best software' listicles should state up front that Pabau is one of the options and that the criteria reflect Pabau's view, rather than presenting as a neutral roundup. This is also the honest reading of the existing rule against deceptive framing.

**Apply anywhere:** When you publish a listicle that includes your own product, say so in the intro and frame the criteria as your view, rather than imitating the neutral testing convention you did not follow.

### 3. Default to a list post for bottom-of-funnel product and service keywords  `146.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 146*

Once Grow and Convert have decided a blog post is the right vehicle for a bottom-of-funnel keyword, they most often write a list post. The reason is intent: on a category keyword like a software term, the searcher wants to view options and weigh them against each other, and a list satisfies that better than a single product page. They show client examples ranking above product landing pages this way, including Buddy Punch at position one for 'clock in clock out app' and for 'mobile time clock app' with seven landing pages and home pages below, Bigtincan at position two for 'sales onboarding software' with product landing pages in three and four, Circuit at one for 'delivery mapping software', and Reduct at one for 'video to text transcription software'. They describe these as a sample from dozens of clients.

> "We'll often do a list post because as we mentioned above"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite position one for Buddy Punch on 'clock in clock out app' and 'mobile time clock app', position two for Bigtincan on 'sales onboarding software', and position one for Circuit and Reduct on their category terms, all above product landing pages.

**How to do it**

1. Confirm from the SERP that the searcher's intent is to view and compare options rather than to buy one named product.
2. Write a list post naming real competing products, not a single-product page dressed as a list.
3. Order and frame the list so your own product's value props are stated up front relative to the alternatives.
4. Give each entry enough substance that the post satisfies the comparison intent on its own, without a click out.
5. Target the exact category phrasing in the title and H1, for example 'clock in clock out app' rather than a broader synonym.
6. Track the position of the post against the product landing pages it competes with, and refresh when one overtakes you.
7. Feed the post's competitor framing to sales, since it doubles as a positioning document.

**Pitfall:** Publishing a fake list that is really one product plus filler. It reads as vendor spin, fails the comparison intent, and does not hold a top-three position.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau category terms such as aesthetic clinic software or practice management software, David should commission list posts that name real competitors rather than product pages, and put Pabau's differentiators in the framing.

**Apply anywhere:** For category terms in your market, commission list posts that name real competitors rather than product pages, and put your own differentiators in the framing.

### 4. Delete AI's argue-example-argue-again loop wherever it appears  `71.5`
*core · best practices · source 71*

Devesh names a specific structural tic in AI writing: it makes an argument, adds an example, then makes the same argument again. His reaction while editing is that this is not grade school and the point does not need restating. He cut several of these during the session, including a whole section that repeated the intro almost verbatim and a paragraph that restated a point he had already made in one sentence. He is careful to say this is not an AI-only problem. Over ten years at Grow and Convert he has read countless human submissions that do it worse, and he traces it to an anxiety that if you do not say something enough times the reader will not get it. The instruction is the same either way: say it once, in one place, and move on.

> "makes an argument, adds an example, and then it makes the argument again"

**Evidence:** Devesh cut an entire post-intro section during the session, saying it was literally the intro again, and separately cut a duplicate mention of the brand summary that appeared both in prose and as step one.

**How to do it**

1. Read the draft looking only for repeated claims, ignoring style on this pass.
2. For each claim, note every place it appears, including the intro and any closing paragraph.
3. Keep the single strongest instance, normally the one with the concrete example attached, and delete the rest.
4. Delete any post-example sentence that restates the argument the example just proved.
5. Check specifically for a section near the top that re-explains what the intro already said, and cut it whole.
6. Check that a supporting artifact, such as a brand summary or a data source, is described in exactly one place rather than mentioned then re-introduced as a step.
7. Re-read the intro and body together after editing, since a section only reads as repetitive in context.

**Pitfall:** Reviewing sections in isolation hides the repetition. Devesh only spotted that a whole section duplicated the intro once he read them together after editing the intro.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial pass should include a dedicated repetition sweep that reads the intro, the Key takeaways block and the body together, since AI drafts routinely restate the takeaways as their own section.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a dedicated repetition pass on every AI draft. Find each claim, keep the single strongest instance with its example, and delete every restatement, including the section that quietly repeats your intro.

### 5. Delete AI-written 'best for' and 'standout feature' columns you cannot verify  `72.8`
*core · concrete actions · source 72*

The AI draft produced a comparison table with 'best for' and 'standout feature' columns. Khanal deletes it, and is careful to say the reason is not only his own time pressure. He calls the content AI interpolation played off as fact: Jasper described as best for enterprise teams needing brand governance is an assertion with an arbitrariness to it, and there may be no right answer at all. He notes AI always comes across extremely confident and that confidence is not accuracy. Two risks follow. A named vendor can email you asking what you are talking about, which matters more the bigger your brand is. And he does not think a page full of confident unverified assertions puts you in a good spot for Google's quality assessment. He allows objective columns such as starting price and feature set.

> "this is a lot of AI interpolation being played off"

**Evidence:** Khanal deleted a full comparison table on air, citing 'Jasper is best for enterprise teams needing brand governance' as the example of confident but arbitrary AI interpolation.

**How to do it**

1. Scan any AI-generated comparison table for judgment columns: best for, standout feature, ideal user.
2. For each cell, ask what source would prove it, and mark it unverified if the answer is the model's guess.
3. Delete the judgment columns rather than rewording them.
4. Keep only columns you can source from the vendor's own site: starting price, named features, integrations.
5. Open each vendor's product and solutions pages to confirm the retained facts before publishing.
6. If a judgment column is genuinely load-bearing, replace it with a sourced quote or your own tested opinion, labelled as opinion.
7. Weigh brand exposure: the larger your company, the lower your tolerance for an unsourced claim about a named competitor.

**Pitfall:** Publishing a confident-sounding table because competitors on the SERP all have one. Khanal notes the AI justified the table by SERP analysis, and he overrode it. The signal you have hit this is a cell you cannot trace to any source.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau listicles must not carry AI-written 'best for' verdicts about named competitors. Keep the pricing table, sourced from each provider's own website as the house rules already require, and cut judgment columns unless Pabau has actually tested the product.

**Apply anywhere:** Delete AI-written judgment columns such as 'best for' from comparison tables, keep only facts sourced from each vendor's own site, and treat confident phrasing as no evidence of accuracy.

### 6. Delete pages with no search intent and no traffic data behind them  `74.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 74*

David Quaid says that if you are doing SEO, a page with no intent behind it should raise the question of why it is on the site at all. He tells a story from rebuilding his own company site: to show the extended leadership team they were being emotion-driven rather than data-driven, he looked at the last 240,000 visits over 30 days and found that fewer than four people, not four percent but four people, had visited the about-us page. His broader point is that companies copy structure from other companies. Google does not look for an about-us page or a contact page. Those exist because firms think they should. He allows that starting a page on a hunch is fine, because that is how humans discover things. The rule is what happens next: if it stops working, get rid of it.

> "less than four people, not 4%, four people went to the about us page"

**Evidence:** David: fewer than four visits to the about-us page across 240,000 visits in 30 days on his own company site.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the last 30 days of pageviews for every URL and sort ascending.
2. For each near-zero page, name the search query it targets; if you cannot name one, mark it for removal.
3. Check whether the page earns anything else measurable: conversions, assisted conversions, or backlinks.
4. Keep pages with a real job even at low traffic, and delete or merge the rest.
5. 301 each deleted URL to the closest relevant page, server-side.
6. Re-run the report quarterly so hunch-started pages get judged on data rather than left indefinitely.
7. Present the raw visitor count, not the percentage, when arguing a page's removal internally; David found the absolute number changed the conversation.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Keeping pages because a competitor has them. David's warning is that the decision then gets defended with data found afterwards, which is emotion-driven reasoning wearing a report.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this ascending-pageviews report across pabau.com once a quarter and either merge, refresh or remove the dead tail. Corporate pages that exist only because other software companies have them should be judged on the same numbers as blog posts.

**Apply anywhere:** Sort every URL by 30-day pageviews ascending, require each low-traffic page to name the query or conversion it earns, and delete or merge the rest.

### 7. Delete the topic-is-important opener from every bottom-funnel intro  `126.5`
*core · best practices · source 126*

Grow and Convert's clearest quality rule from this article is that the searcher already knows the topic matters, so telling them wastes the opening. Someone Googling enterprise reporting tool already knows reporting is important; that is why they are searching for a tool. Their analogy: if someone walks into a car dealership and asks about the Tesla Model S, the salesperson does not open with a line about electric cars growing in popularity, because that would be insane, yet marketers do it in blog posts constantly. Their own client piece for TapClicks opens with almost zero fluff sentences and instead names the incumbent software the reader is probably using, then goes straight to pain points. Naming the incumbent is the hook: the reader recognizes their own situation and keeps reading.

> "Electric cars have really been growing in popularity"

**Evidence:** The TapClicks piece opening with incumbents and pain points ranks on page one for 'enterprise reporting tool'; the generic 'what is' competitors sit alongside it undifferentiated.

**How to do it**

1. Read the first two sentences of the draft and ask whether they tell the reader something they already knew before searching.
2. Delete any sentence asserting that the topic or category is important or growing.
3. Replace it with the names of the tools or methods the reader is most likely using today.
4. Follow immediately with the specific pain those incumbents cause, sourced from the interview.
5. Do not define the category term unless the SERP shows searchers are asking what it means.
6. Re-check the opener against the dealership test: would a salesperson say this to a buyer who just named the product?

**Pitfall:** Defining the term in the intro. Grow and Convert show three page-one competitors all opening with 'what is enterprise reporting', which is exactly why none of them differentiates.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's intros should never open with a line about how aesthetic practices are growing or why patient records matter. Open by naming the systems the reader is on now, then the specific pain that setup causes. This is a concrete rule to add to the editorial pass on /blog/ and /templates/ pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Cut the topic-is-important opener. Open bottom-funnel articles by naming the tools or methods the reader is most likely using now, then the specific pain that causes, and never define the category term the searcher already knows.

### 8. Delete thousands of weak, cannibalizing pages to improve overall performance  `17.6`
*core · best practices · source 17*

One of the most underrated SEO tactics described is simply deleting content: some sites remove thousands of weak, outdated, or keyword-cannibalizing pages and end up performing better overall, because those pages were wasting crawl budget, indexation allocation, and topical-relevance signal on content nobody was actually searching for. The host connects this to Lars Lofgren's account, from a separate episode about the Helpful Content Update, of cutting entire sections of websites that were hit by HCU, or that simply weren't performing, because the site had extended its topical authority too far or accumulated too many weak pages dragging down overall user signals. This means pruning is framed as a way to condense and rebound a site's topical focus and quality score, not merely a housekeeping exercise.

> "remove thousands of weak, outdated, cannibalizing pages and end up performing better"

**How to do it**

1. Export a full list of your site's indexed pages along with organic traffic, impressions, and ranking data for each, for example from Google Search Console or a crawler.
2. Identify pages with near-zero organic traffic and impressions over a meaningful lookback period, such as the last 12 months.
3. Separately flag pages that are cannibalizing each other, competing for the same query with split relevance signal.
4. Cross-check flagged pages against whether they sit within a topic area that's core to your site versus a tangential area the site drifted into.
5. For confirmed low-value or cannibalizing pages, decide between merging content into a stronger page with a redirect, or outright deleting the page and returning a proper status or redirect.
6. Prioritize pruning entire weak sections of the site rather than a handful of scattered pages, if the underlying problem is topical drift or an HCU-style broad quality hit.
7. Monitor sitewide crawl stats and rankings for retained pages over the following weeks to confirm removing the weak pages redirected crawl budget and improved performance elsewhere (inferred verification step).

**Tools:** Google Search Console

### 9. Delete top-of-funnel definition headings from bottom-of-funnel articles  `124.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 124*

Grow and Convert use the heading-only read to catch one specific failure they say they see often: a top-of-funnel heading sitting inside bottom-of-funnel content. Their demonstration inserts What is Content Writing as an H2 into their own outline about improving content writing through an interview process. The test they apply is whether it makes sense to assume that someone clicking this post, from Google search or from an ad, does not already know what content writing is. It does not, so the heading goes. The reasoning generalizes: the click itself is evidence of what the reader already knows, so any heading that explains a term the searcher used is padding that pushes the answer down the page.

> "a top-of-funnel heading that we often see in bottom-of-the-funnel content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own outline demonstration, inserting What is Content Writing into a piece whose readers arrive from search or ads already knowing the term.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the heading list and mark every heading of the form what is X or why is X important.
2. For each marked heading, name the query the page targets and ask whether someone searching it already knows X.
3. If they do, delete the heading and its section rather than shortening it.
4. Check for the same failure inside intros, where the definition often hides as the first paragraph.
5. Move any genuinely needed definition into a single clause on first mention.
6. Reallocate the deleted word count to the sections that answer the actual query.
7. Repeat the check on refreshes, since definition sections often get added during optimization passes.
8. Keep a note of the deleted section in case a separate top-of-funnel page should target it.

**Pitfall:** Keeping the definition section because a keyword tool shows volume for what is X. That volume belongs to a different page and a different intent, and hosting it here delays the answer for the buyer who actually landed.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog and template pages should not open with a definition of the procedure or the software category when the keyword implies the reader already knows it. David should strip these on refresh and, if the definition has its own demand, give it a separate page.

**Apply anywhere:** Mark every what is X heading in your outline and ask whether someone who searched your target keyword already knows X. If they do, delete the section and give that demand its own page instead.

### 10. Deprioritized never means deleted: improve, consolidate or remove  `66.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 66*

The framework is explicit that a low score is not a delete instruction. Deprioritizing these content types shouldn't mean automatically deleting every existing page: depending on its current performance and role, the right action might be to improve it with original value, consolidate it into a stronger relevant page, or remove it. That makes three distinct outcomes, chosen from the page's current performance and the role it plays, not from the score alone. A commodity definition that ranks and feeds internal links gets consolidated into a topic hub; a rehashed explainer with genuine demand behind it gets original testing, data or expert contribution added; only a page with no performance, no role and no path to original value gets removed. Skipping this triage is how a content audit destroys rankings and internal link equity while claiming to raise quality.

> "shouldn't mean automatically deleting every existing page"

**How to do it**

1. For every deprioritized page, pull its current performance first: clicks, impressions, conversions, assisted conversions, internal links pointing in, and external links pointing in.
2. Route to 'improve' when there is real demand and a credible way to add original value — first-hand testing, proprietary data, an expert contribution, a tool, or distinctive media. Schedule the addition, do not just rewrite the prose.
3. Route to 'consolidate' when a stronger, relevant page can satisfy the same need: merge the useful content into that page, then 301 the old URL to it so links and history carry over.
4. Route to 'remove' only when the page has no meaningful performance, no role in the internal link graph, no external links, and no realistic route to original value.
5. Before removing, check whether the URL is cited anywhere — AI answers, other sites, documentation, or paid campaigns — and redirect rather than delete if it is.
6. Record the decision and the date per URL, so a later audit does not re-litigate pages already triaged.
7. Re-check performance 8-12 weeks after the pass, and treat any consolidation that lost more than it gained as a signal to restore the standalone page.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics, Screaming Frog, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Running the removal pass without checking inbound internal links and external links first. Deleting a low-value page that happens to be a hub in the internal link graph, or that carries the site's only links from a strong domain, costs more than the page was ever worth.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau audits its glossary and older explainer posts, split them three ways rather than deleting: consolidate thin definitions into the relevant feature or template hub with a redirect, add original value to the explainers that still earn clicks, and remove only the pages with no traffic, no links and no route to first-hand data.

**Apply anywhere:** When auditing a glossary and older explainer posts, split them three ways rather than deleting: consolidate thin definitions into the relevant product or topic hub with a redirect, add original value to the explainers that still earn clicks, and remove only the pages with no traffic, no links and no route to first-hand data.

### 11. Diagnose duplicate content as slug, title and H1 overlap, not body text  `74.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 74*

David Quaid calls the duplicate content penalty one of the biggest myths in SEO and says Google does not care about duplicate content in the sense people mean. He rejects the 'Google is saving index space' argument, pointing out Google indexes hour-long 4K videos nobody watches, and that storage costs keep falling. What people are actually seeing, he says, is cannibalization: two pages with the same or very similar slug, or a word in one slug that matches another target keyword, compete with each other and one gets blocked. He is specific about where the collision happens. It is not caused by indexing, the algorithms or search; it is caused by the post-index retrieval system that assembles the final answers. He also notes Google's duplicate-content reporting looks at the page title and the H1 rather than all the body content, while the slug is usually the real culprit. He cites a 2009 Matt Cutts video as still relevant.

> "it's caused by the post index retrieval system that presents the final answers"

**Evidence:** David: Google indexes hour-long 4K videos of little interest, which he uses to debunk the storage-saving theory; he cites Matt Cutts's 2009 video on this as still accurate.

**How to do it**

1. Export every URL with its slug, title tag and H1 into one spreadsheet.
2. Sort by slug and flag any pairs whose slugs share the head keyword or differ only by a modifier.
3. Do the same on title tags and H1s, since those are the fields Google's duplicate reporting reads.
4. For each flagged pair, run the two target queries and check whether both URLs appear or whether they trade places over time.
5. Pick the winner by current rank, clicks and referring domains, merge the loser's unique content into it, and 301 the loser.
6. Where both pages must stay, rewrite one slug, title and H1 to a genuinely different query, not a synonym.
7. Stop rewriting body copy to reduce text overlap; David says that is not where the collision happens.

**Tools:** Semrush, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Spending weeks rewording body paragraphs to fix 'duplicate content' while two competing slugs stay in place, so the pages keep blocking each other in the retrieval layer.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau publishes several posts around the same treatment or software category, David should check slug, title and H1 overlap before publishing rather than after. On pabau.com the risk is highest across /blog/ posts and the matching /templates/ page targeting the same head term.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit duplicate-content complaints at the slug, title and H1 level, merge or re-target the loser, and stop rewriting body copy to fix it.

### 12. Discuss your product in depth because the query demands it  `143.3`
*core · best practices · source 143*

Grow and Convert reject the standing advice not to be salesy in blog content. Their position is that for a query like 'best social media management software' the searcher wants to understand value propositions, how options compare and which suits whom. Discussing your own product in that context is not selling, it is fulfilling search intent. They say most SEOs and content marketers barely mention the product, glance at a link to a service page, and then rely on popups, opt-in forms and CTA graphics to take care of conversions. They tie this directly to the keyword problem: teams that chase high-volume low-intent terms have to avoid the product, because the product genuinely does not belong in that content. Fix the keyword and the product discussion becomes mandatory rather than awkward.

> "it's just fulfilling search intent"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert base this on conversion measurement across hundreds of client blog posts and set it out in their Pain Point Copywriting post.

**How to do it**

1. For any buying-intent target, write the page as you would a sales page, with the product argued in the body, not linked in passing.
2. State what your product does, who it fits and where it differs from the named alternatives on the page.
3. Put the product discussion inside the section that answers the reader's comparison question, not in a block at the end.
4. Delete the reflex disclaimer voice that treats mentioning the product as an intrusion.
5. Keep design-element CTAs, but stop treating popups and opt-in forms as the conversion mechanism.
6. On genuinely top-funnel posts, still weave in one compelling product passage rather than none.
7. Review published posts for the glancing-mention pattern and rewrite those product passages first.

**Pitfall:** Writers trained to sound neutral produce a product paragraph that reads as a disclosure rather than an argument. The signal is a post that ranks for a comparison query but never says why a reader should pick you.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau comparison and alternatives articles should argue the product inside the body sections, not only in the Pabau section before the Conclusion. On a 'best practice management software' page, the Pabau entry needs the same depth of reasoning as any competitor entry.

**Apply anywhere:** On buying-intent pages, argue your product in the body copy at the depth a comparison query demands. Treating the product as off-limits and delegating conversion to popups is what makes those pages fail.

### 13. Do not cover the beginner question your bottom-funnel audience already knows  `96.5`
*core · best practices · source 96*

Analyzing the 'paid search dashboard' SERP, Grow and Convert found most ranking pages were thin and overweighted beginner material, especially a 'What is a paid search dashboard?' section. They judged that obvious to anyone in the target audience for that term, who already know what it is and are looking for options to purchase. So they deliberately left the definition section out of their piece, even though it recurred across page one, and it still reached position 2. This is a deliberate exception to their own rule that recurring subtopics need covering. The test they apply is whether someone typing that specific query would already know the answer. For super bottom-of-funnel keywords, the reader's real question is which of these products is the best fit for me.

> "we felt this was obvious to anyone in their target audience"

**Evidence:** The TapClicks post omitted the 'what is' section and ranks at position 2 for 'paid search dashboard'.

**How to do it**

1. List the recurring subtopics from the SERP analysis as usual.
2. For each, ask whether a person typing this exact query already knows the answer.
3. Cut the definitional sections that fail that test, even when competitors all include them.
4. Reallocate that space to the differentiation question the searcher actually has.
5. On bottom-funnel terms, answer 'which option fits me' explicitly rather than 'what is this'.
6. Check after publishing that the omission did not cost ranking; restore a short answer if it did.

**Pitfall:** Cutting the definition on a mixed-intent keyword where part of the audience is genuinely new. The signal you got it wrong is a high bounce rate from the query variants that include 'what is'.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's bottom-funnel pages, like software comparison and pricing pages, should skip 'what is practice management software' and lead with which fit which practice type. Keep definitions on genuinely informational /blog/ posts.

**Apply anywhere:** On bottom-funnel keywords, cut the definitional section your buyers already know and spend the space on which option fits whom.

### 14. End each bottom-funnel post with a screenshot walkthrough of the feature  `121.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 121*

Grow and Convert say selling the product in practice takes the form of a product walkthrough: screenshots of the relevant features, a description of how they work, and an explanation of how they solve the customer's pain better or differently than other solutions. Their example is an article for a video editing platform targeting 'video logging software'. They did not write that video logging is tedious and the tool makes it easier. They established that they understood the tools the reader uses, the specific challenges in that workflow and the consequences of those challenges, then walked through exactly how the transcript-based editing feature removes the need to scrub through hours of footage, with a screenshot for each step. They are explicit that this level of detail only comes from interviewing product experts who understand both the pain and the software.

> "walk through exactly how the product's transcript-based editing feature eliminates"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite their 'video logging software' article, where the walkthrough of transcript-based editing replaced a generic ease-of-use claim; that keyword converted at 3.7% in their reported data.

**How to do it**

1. Interview the product expert about the one feature that addresses the article's target query, and record it.
2. Ask them to screen-share the workflow end to end and capture a screenshot at each step.
3. Open the product section by naming the tools the reader currently uses and the specific step that costs them time.
4. State the consequence of that step in concrete terms, such as hours spent or errors introduced.
5. Walk the feature through in numbered steps, one screenshot per step, in the order the user would perform them.
6. Say how this differs from how competing tools handle the same job, naming the difference rather than claiming superiority.
7. Place the walkthrough where the reader has already been given the neutral answer to the query, not before it.
8. Caption each screenshot with what the reader is looking at and why it matters for the job in the title.

**Pitfall:** Writing the assertion instead of the walkthrough. 'This is tedious and our tool makes it easier' reads as a claim, converts poorly, and is the default output of a writer who never saw the product.

**Apply at Pabau:** Every Pabau bottom-funnel page should end with a numbered, captioned walkthrough of the specific Pabau feature that does the job in the title, sourced from a screen-share with product, not from the feature page.

**Apply anywhere:** Close each buying-intent post with a numbered walkthrough of the feature that does the job, one screenshot per step, sourced from a screen-share with someone who built it. Show the workflow instead of asserting it is easier.

### 15. Establish the normal approach first, then show how yours differs  `134.12`
*core · best practices · source 134*

Grow and Convert say every article they write, wherever it sits in the funnel, has to make a compelling pitch for the client's product, because the purpose is product sign-ups, demo requests or qualified leads. But a genuinely differentiated product still needs education before the difference lands. Their sequence for Rainforest was three moves in order: establish what the normal approach to software testing is, explain the downsides of that approach, then explain how Rainforest does it differently. Without the first two moves the differentiator is a feature nobody can evaluate. They pair this with a plain-English description of the difference between two technical products, which is the part the interview process produces. The structure works precisely because the reader supplies the comparison themselves once the normal approach is on the page.

> "establish what the "normal" approach to software testing is"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert used this three-move structure in the Rainforest QA article that became the client's top converting piece over 12 months.

**How to do it**

1. Write one short passage describing how the job is done today without your product.
2. Name the specific downsides of that default, using the consequences the expert gave you in interview.
3. Only then introduce your product's approach, as a direct contrast to the default just described.
4. Keep the whole comparison in plain English, avoiding the industry terms the expert used.
5. Include a concrete example of the difference in practice rather than a feature list.
6. Repeat the pattern in every article, including ones you consider top of funnel.

**Pitfall:** Leading with the differentiator. Readers who do not already know the default cannot see what is different about it, so a genuinely unusual product reads as another feature bullet.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles should describe how practices handle the task now, with paper forms or split systems, name what that costs, and then show the Pabau approach. Introduce it as practice management software like Pabau on first mention.

**Apply anywhere:** Describe the default way the job is done, name its costs, then contrast your approach. Skipping the first two steps makes real differentiation invisible.

### 16. Expect AI to have no stance, because averaging is its design  `174.5`
*core · content insights · source 174*

Khanal gives the mechanism behind generic AI output rather than treating it as a quality problem to be prompted away. AI writers are biased towards saying the most average thing because that is how they are built. They are programmed to produce a response as similar as possible to the information they trained on, which by definition is things people have already said, by finding the most likely word that goes after the word just written, over and over. The consequence he draws is that AI cannot take a strong stance and has no answer on topics where there is no right answer. This is not fixed by a better prompt or a newer model, because it is the objective function, so any strategy that depends on differentiated argument has to source that argument from a human.

> "AI writers are biased towards saying the most average thing"

**Evidence:** Khanal's mechanism: models are trained to reproduce their training data and predict the most likely next token, so their output is the most common existing statement on a topic.

**How to do it**

1. Treat any request for an opinion, a ranking judgment or a contrarian take as out of scope for AI generation.
2. Use AI instead for tasks where the average answer is the correct answer: definitions, summaries, formatting, code references.
3. When you do need a stance, capture it from a human first in a recorded interview, then hand the transcript to AI for structuring.
4. Check drafts for hedging language such as 'it depends' and 'both approaches have merits', which marks an averaged position.
5. Replace each hedge with the actual call your team would make, and say why the alternative is worse.
6. Do not expect a stronger prompt or a newer model to solve this; the averaging is the training objective.

**Pitfall:** Prompt engineering to force a 'bold' opinion produces a confident-sounding average position rather than a real one, which is worse because it reads as a stance while still saying what everyone says.

**Apply at Pabau:** On pabau.com, any article that ranks options, recommends a workflow or takes a side, such as comparison and template pages, must get its judgment from someone who has run or supported a practice. AI can structure the piece but cannot decide which approach is better.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not expect AI to hold a position. Next-token prediction returns the most common existing statement, so hedged, balanced output is the design working as intended. Source any ranking, recommendation or contrarian take from a human, then use AI downstream for structure. A stronger prompt only makes the average position sound more confident.

### 17. Expect SERPs to drift by target customer and by solution type  `116.7`
*core · content insights · source 116*

Grow and Convert name the two ways a SERP changes under a page that has not moved, and both explain lost secondary keywords. The first is a changing target customer: they use 'vacation tracker', where the SERP was once software for companies tracking team vacations and later shifted to individuals tracking their own trips. The page is unchanged but now answers the wrong person. The second is a changing solution type: 'employee onboarding systems' once returned listicles teaching small businesses to build a system out of spreadsheets, then dedicated HR SaaS products emerged and replaced them, and Google stopped distinguishing 'system' from 'software' so the two SERPs merged. They say they have seen shifts like this many times. The diagnostic value is that both are visible by reading the current top 10 against what you remember publishing into.

> "Interpretations and meanings of search terms shift and evolve"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's worked examples: 'vacation tracker' shifting from company software to personal tracking, and 'employee onboarding system' converging with 'employee onboarding software' as dedicated HR SaaS emerged.

**How to do it**

1. For each declining post, pull the current top 10 for its target keyword.
2. Classify each ranking page by who it is written for and what solution type it recommends.
3. Compare that classification against the SERP you wrote into, using notes or archived SERP data.
4. Label the change as a target-customer shift, a solution-type shift, or neither.
5. For a target-customer shift, re-angle the piece toward whoever the SERP now serves, or move the page to a keyword that still matches your buyer.
6. For a solution-type shift, check whether the near-synonym SERPs have merged and consolidate rather than maintaining two pages.
7. Record the diagnosis in the audit sheet so the rewrite brief starts from the actual change.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Assuming a ranking drop means the page got worse. In both of these patterns the page is unchanged and still good; it now answers a question the SERP stopped asking.

**Apply at Pabau:** Aesthetic industry terms drift the same way. A Pabau page written when a term returned manual how-to guides will lose once software vendors take the SERP, so David should re-read the top 10 before assuming the article needs more depth.

**Apply anywhere:** Terms drift the same way in every industry. A page written when a term returned manual how-to guides will lose once vendors take the SERP, so read the current top 10 before assuming the article needs more depth.

### 19. Expect a couple of hours of Google research to fail experienced readers  `162.13`
*core · content insights · source 162*

Grow and Convert's opening argument is that unless your customers are genuine beginners, a writer, freelancer or marketer doing two hours of Google research cannot produce something useful or interesting to people who have worked in the field for years. They note that almost all content is produced exactly that way, which is why so much of it is what they call beginner-level fluff. Their answer is to route the substance through subject matter experts and industry influencers instead, and to build the whole production process around sourcing those people rather than around briefing writers. The corollary for SEO is that the differentiator is not word count or structure; it is a fact only the interviewee could supply, which is also what makes the piece worth a link and worth citing.

> "produce a piece that is actually useful or interesting"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert built their whole content model on subject matter experts after concluding that a couple of hours of writer research cannot serve readers who have been in the field for years.

**How to do it**

1. Decide whether your reader is a beginner or a practitioner before commissioning anything.
2. For practitioner audiences, refuse to commission desk-research-only drafts.
3. Attach a named expert to every practitioner-level article at brief stage, not at review stage.
4. Build the outline around the questions only that expert can answer.
5. Have the writer interview the expert and write from the transcript.
6. Keep at least one detail per section that does not appear anywhere in the existing SERP.
7. Credit the expert by name and link to their site, which is part of what you promised them.

**Pitfall:** Adding a token expert quote to a desk-researched draft. Practitioners spot the surrounding generic material immediately, and the article earns neither links nor trust.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog serves practising clinicians, not beginners, so any article about treatments, compliance or clinic operations should be built on an interview with a practitioner and carry at least one detail that is not already on the SERP.

**Apply anywhere:** If your readers are practitioners rather than beginners, attach a named expert to the article at brief stage and build the outline around what only that person can tell you.

### 21. Expect scaled AI content to fail differently on new and established sites  `180.5`
*core · content insights · source 180*

Grow and Convert report seeing the consequences of fully AI-generated content across several sites, including clients who arrived with it already published. The failure mode splits by site authority. On established sites with existing authority, large volumes of AI content may go unnoticed at first, but Google has explicitly named this scaled content abuse and a penalty follows eventually. On newer sites without established authority, the consequences are more immediate and more severe: they saw most of these pages fail to get indexed at all, and in some cases entire domains became invisible in search. That gives two different diagnostic signals. A quiet, slow decline on an old site and a wall of unindexed URLs on a young one are the same underlying problem at different authority levels.

> "entire domains become invisible in search results"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert observed both patterns across several websites, including some of their own clients before the engagement began.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the full URL list of anything published with little human editing and tag it by publication date.
2. On a young site, check Google Search Console coverage first: a high share stuck in 'Discovered' or 'Crawled - currently not indexed' is the signal.
3. On an established site, check the same URLs for impressions decay rather than indexing, since they will likely have indexed fine.
4. Compare the site-wide organic trend against the AI publishing start date to see whether the decline is sitewide or page-level.
5. Stop the publishing pipeline before doing anything else, since velocity is what triggers the scaled-content classification.
6. Rewrite or consolidate the strongest pages with genuine source material, and delete or noindex the rest.
7. Expect recovery only at the next core update, and do not read a flat few weeks as a failed fix.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Reading indexing as a verdict on the old site and rankings as a verdict on the new one. On a new site the pages never index, so a rank check shows nothing and the owner assumes the content is fine.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau publishes on an established domain, so the risk is the slow version. David should track the ratio of lightly-edited to interview-sourced articles per month and keep the lightly-edited share low rather than watching for an indexing problem that will not appear.

**Apply anywhere:** Diagnose scaled AI content by site age. On a young domain the pages simply never index and the whole site can disappear. On an authoritative domain everything indexes and the penalty arrives later, at a core update.

### 23. Express originality through features if you sell a product, process if you sell a service  `113.8`
*core · best practices · source 113*

Grow and Convert split originality by business model. If you sell a product, originality comes from discussing your features and the way they solve customer pain points differently or better than competitors. If you sell a service, originality usually comes from what is different about your strategy or process compared to competitors, and how that gets better results for clients. They apply this to their own agency content and to the service businesses they work with. The worked example is a remote executive assistant service whose differentiator was its hiring process: the founders used backgrounds in behavioral science to design a rigorous methodology for vetting candidates. That became the main point of differentiation expressed in every article they wrote for the client.

> "comes from discussing what's different about your"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's remote executive assistant client: a behavioral-science-based hiring methodology became the differentiator expressed in every article.

**How to do it**

1. Decide whether the offering is a product or a service, because it changes what counts as an original point.
2. For a product, list the features that solve a named customer pain point differently or better than named competitors.
3. For a service, document the strategy or process step that competitors do not run, and the reason it produces better results.
4. Write that differentiator into a reusable paragraph and keep it in the brief template so every article carries it.
5. Back it with a result: what the process or feature changed for a specific client or customer.
6. Vary the wording per article but keep the substance identical, so the same claim compounds across the site.
7. Reject drafts where the differentiator is missing or replaced with a generic quality claim.

**Pitfall:** Service businesses default to describing outcomes rather than method, which reads identically to every competitor. The signal is a draft where swapping the company name would not change a sentence.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sells a product, so originality in Pabau articles should come from named features and how they solve a practice's specific pain, with the caption rule about naming how the feature helps the reader doing the job the article covers.

**Apply anywhere:** If you sell a product, make originality about features against named competitors. If you sell a service, make it about a process step nobody else runs. Keep the differentiator paragraph in the brief template.
