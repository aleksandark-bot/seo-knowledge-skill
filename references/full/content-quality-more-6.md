### 1. Reject general target-market statements as a substitute for positioning  `159.17`
*useful · best practices · source 159*

Grow and Convert list the statements companies mistake for market fit: our new CRM targets sales managers, our dev shop targets series A and B startups, our ecommerce store caters to millennial women, my consulting practice offers copywriting services to small businesses that need copywriting. They say plainly this is not good enough. These describe a target market but say nothing about perception, which is what positioning is. The four questions that replace them are: who do customers think your product is for, what do customers think makes you unique, what pain points do they think you solve, and how credible do they perceive you to be. The distinction is that all four are answered by customers, not by the company, so any statement written entirely from internal knowledge fails by definition.

> "But what about how your offering is perceived by the market?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert reject four typical target-market statements as insufficient and replace them with four perception questions answered only by customers.

**How to do it**

1. Take your current audience statement and check whether it says anything about how customers perceive you. If not, it is a target market, not positioning.
2. Write the four perception questions as a survey and send it to recent buyers and recent non-buyers.
3. Record the answers in the customers' own words rather than summarizing them.
4. Compare each answer with the internal assumption it was meant to test.
5. Rewrite the positioning statement using only claims that at least two customers made unprompted.
6. Keep the target-market line for internal targeting and media buying, but never let it stand in as positioning.
7. Repeat the survey after every significant product or market change.

**Pitfall:** Writing the positioning statement in a workshop with no customer input. It reads fine internally and predicts nothing about how the market responds.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's internal ICP definition is not positioning. David should get the four perception answers from recent clinic sign-ups and recent lost deals before rewriting any high-traffic page.

**Apply anywhere:** An ICP statement is not positioning. Get the four perception answers from recent buyers and non-buyers before rewriting any high-traffic page.

### 2. Reject phrases you cannot define, even in your own draft  `92.13`
*useful · best practices · source 92*

Grow and Convert take apart the phrase more complex language implementations from HubSpot's description of Lingotek. They ask what it actually means: more complex languages, a length problem, something else. Language is language, you translate from X to Y each time, so why would one time be more complex. If they mean length, say length. The phrase sounds smart and resolves to nothing, which is their definition of fluff at the phrase level. They pair it with a second objection about the growing into framing: these tools are not oversized clothes a company grows into, and it is not the case that small companies only need machine translation while big ones need human. A five-page site may need extremely accurate copy for legal reasons. Their test is simple and applies to your own writing as much as a competitor's: if you cannot state plainly what a phrase means, it does not go on the page.

> "That sounds like a smart phrase but what does it mean?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's counter-example: even a small site with only a few pages may need extremely accurate copy, say for legal reasons, so accuracy is not a function of company size.

**How to do it**

1. Highlight every abstract noun phrase in the draft: implementations, solutions, capabilities, workflows, transformations.
2. For each, write a one-line plain-English definition without using the phrase itself.
3. If you cannot write the definition, delete the phrase and say the concrete thing instead, such as longer documents or legal copy.
4. Check whether the phrase is doing segmentation work by company size; if so, ask what the real axis is.
5. Rewrite by the real axis, naming the outcome the buyer needs rather than the stage of company they are at.
6. Have a reader outside the team read flagged lines and say what they think each means; mismatched answers mean it goes.

**Pitfall:** Sizing framing quietly disqualifies buyers. Telling readers a feature is for companies growing into complexity loses the small buyer who needs it today, and you never see the loss in analytics.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau copy should avoid framing features as being for practices that grow into them. A single-room clinic can have the same compliance need as a ten-location group, and size framing tells the smaller reader the feature is not for them.

**Apply anywhere:** Avoid framing features as being for companies that grow into them. A small buyer can have the same compliance or accuracy need as a large one, and size framing tells them the feature is not for them.

### 3. Reject the anti-selling rule: talk about the product more, not less  `112.19`
*useful · content insights · source 112*

Grow and Convert take direct issue with a widespread content marketing belief. The common school of thought is that you should not be pushy, so you avoid selling or talking much about your product and instead offer helpful subject-area advice, on the theory that people will grow to like the brand and buy later. They say the opposite holds in their data: top-of-funnel content built on that theory converts far worse than bottom-of-funnel content, and the more you talk about your product, appropriately, the more sales you get from content. The qualifier matters. They are not arguing for advertising copy in a blog post, but for writing that demonstrates specifically how the product solves the problem the article is about, which is a different craft from how-to writing.

> "The more you talk about your product (appropriately) the more sales you get"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite their own published case studies showing top-of-funnel content converting far worse, and their pain-point copywriting work on selling inside blog posts.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles already require a product section before the Conclusion, so the rule is in place structurally. The gap is usually depth: David should make the Pabau section show how the specific feature solves the specific problem the article covers, rather than a generic product paragraph reused across pieces.

**Apply anywhere:** The advice to avoid mentioning your product in content is wrong for conversion. Talk about the product more, tied specifically to the problem the article addresses, which is a different skill from writing a neutral how-to.

### 4. Run a periodic rankings audit to build the update queue  `119.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 119*

Grow and Convert describe a rankings audit as the discovery mechanism that fills the update queue. During one for a SaaS client, they saw a post targeting an industry-specific keyword around estimating software had climbed to page one, plateaued around positions #9 and #10, then started to dip, which is both signals at once. The SERP analysis showed page one had shifted to list posts comparing software options, while their post only covered the client's product. That was a fair match for intent when it was written. They rebuilt the post with a list of competing tools and changed the title to signal the wider coverage. The post recovered and climbed to position #1, and since then has not dropped past #3, so they leave it alone until rankings fall again.

> "During a rankings audit for a particular SaaS client"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's SaaS client post on an estimating-software keyword: plateaued at #9 to #10, dipped, then after the list rebuild and title change reached #1 and has not fallen past #3.

**How to do it**

1. Export every tracked keyword with its current position and its rank history from Ahrefs Rank Tracker.
2. Mark each page as climbing, plateaued or dipping by reading the trend line, not the latest number.
3. Shortlist every page showing plateau plus dip together, since that pair is the strongest update signal.
4. Open the live SERP for each shortlisted keyword and record the dominant format now ranking, such as listicle, landing page or narrative.
5. Compare that format to your page and note the gap in one sentence.
6. Where the SERP has turned into comparisons, add a genuine list of competing options rather than only expanding your own product section.
7. Rewrite the title so it matches the new format and tells searchers you cover all the main options.
8. After recovery, remove the page from the active queue and do not touch it again until rankings fall.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Adding a token competitor list while keeping the piece a product pitch. Searchers see through it, the format still does not match, and the position does not recover.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's software comparison and feature articles are exposed to exactly this shift. When a SERP for an aesthetic-practice tool query turns into listicles, the Pabau post has to genuinely review competing options, with Pabau as one entry, not a product page with a list bolted on.

**Apply anywhere:** Run a periodic rankings audit and shortlist pages that plateaued and then dipped. Where the SERP has turned into comparison listicles, rebuild the page as a genuine review of competing options and change the title to match.

### 5. Run extensive customer research before producing any content  `160.10`
*useful · best practices · source 160*

The first item on Grow and Convert's list of advice for early-stage companies starting content is to do extensive customer research before starting to produce content, which they call customer-content fit. Paired with their closing advice, the standard is concrete: write on specific topics you know your customers care about, back up your claims with detail, and include as much originality as possible. The research is what makes the topic list defensible when positioning is still fluid, because it replaces internal opinion about which pain points matter with what customers actually said. It also front-loads the material that lets a non-expert writer produce something a long-time industry buyer will not dismiss.

> "Make sure to do extensive customer research before starting to produce content"

**How to do it**

1. Interview current customers and recent buyers before writing the first topic list.
2. Ask what problem made them look for a solution, and what words they used when searching.
3. Collect verbatim phrases and objections rather than summarizing them.
4. Build the topic list only from pain points that appeared in more than one interview.
5. For each topic, write down the specific claim you will make and the detail or data that backs it.
6. Name one original element per article, such as your own numbers, a process, or a case, before it goes to a writer.
7. Reject any proposed topic that traces back only to internal opinion about what customers care about.

**Pitfall:** Building the topic list from internal assumptions when positioning is unsettled. Every article then feels wrong to the founder, and the debate about which pain points to target repeats for each piece.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has direct access to practice owners through onboarding and support, so David should pull recurring questions from those conversations into the brief for each new cluster, and require one original element per article, such as Pabau's own aggregated data or a documented workflow.

**Apply anywhere:** Do extensive customer research before producing content. Build the topic list from pain points named in more than one customer interview, and commit to a specific claim, supporting detail and one original element per article before briefing a writer.

### 6. Run keyword arbitration to fix cannibalization as pages accumulate  `23.6`
*useful · best practices · source 23*

As a site starts ranking, Edward introduces 'keyword arbitration': actively checking whether multiple pages are relevant to the same keyword, and if so, ensuring the page that actually best matches the searcher's intent is the one Google is ranking, rather than leaving it to chance. This is the direct fix for keyword cannibalization, treated as an ongoing task at this site stage rather than a one-time cleanup, since new pages keep getting added over time and can inadvertently start competing with existing pages for the same query.

> "making sure that if you have multiple pages relevant to a keyword"

**How to do it**

1. Pull a list of your ranking pages and their target/ranking keywords from Google Search Console or a rank tracker.
2. Group pages that are ranking for the same or very similar keyword queries.
3. For each group, assess which page most closely matches the actual search intent behind the query.
4. If the page that doesn't best match intent is the one currently ranking, strengthen the better-matching page's on-page optimization and internal links, and consider de-optimizing or consolidating the weaker page.
5. Merge, redirect, or canonicalize near-duplicate pages that are directly cannibalizing each other for the same exact query, where appropriate (inferred remediation step).
6. Repeat this arbitration check on a recurring basis as new pages are added to the site over time.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, a rank tracker

### 7. Search each chosen term to plan a differentiator before drafting  `140.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 140*

Lewis ran a Google search for each of her three target terms before writing, but not to harvest the content. Her stated purpose was to strategize how to make her pieces more useful than what was already there, as opposed to regurgitating information that is already out there, which Grow and Convert call mirage content. This inverts the standard top-ten-summary brief. The SERP is read as a list of things not to repeat, and the actual substance then comes from somewhere else, in her case subject matter expert interviews with the client. The practical output of the pass is one sentence per article saying what this piece will contain that none of the ranking pages does. For ADA Compliance Pros the differentiator was that the competitors were software companies, so a human-audit provider could take a contrarian position none of them could.

> "which G&C refers to as "mirage content"

**Evidence:** Lewis positioned ADA Compliance Pros against software-only competitors on the grounds that a partly manual process catches more errors, lowering the chance of a lawsuit from serial litigators.

**How to do it**

1. Search each target keyword and open the top ten results.
2. List, for each result, the sections it covers and any specific data it brings.
3. Mark the claims every page repeats; these are table stakes, and you cover them in one short section.
4. Note who published each page and what their business model stops them from saying.
5. Write one sentence naming what your article will contain that no ranking page has.
6. Source that differentiator from an internal expert, customer data or your own operations, not from the SERP.
7. Kill the topic if you cannot write that sentence honestly.

**Tools:** Google Search

**Pitfall:** Reading the SERP for structure quietly turns into reading it for content, and you ship a competent summary that ranks nowhere. The tell is a draft with no fact in it that is not already on page one.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau comparison and template pages, David should read the SERP only to list what everyone already says, then get the differentiator from Pabau's support and onboarding teams. A real workflow detail from an aesthetic practice beats another feature summary.

**Apply anywhere:** Search each target term before writing and read the top ten only to find what everyone already says. Cover that briefly, then write one sentence naming what your piece will have that none of them do, sourced from an internal expert or your own data. If you cannot write that sentence, drop the topic.

### 8. Sell to searchers who are not product-aware when they show a pain point  `109.15`
*useful · content insights · source 109*

Grow and Convert name and reject a piece of standard marketing advice. Several marketing experts say not to sell hard to people who are not yet product-aware, on the grounds that they are not ready to buy. Grow and Convert disagree from experience: someone who demonstrates a pain point is open to a good product or service that solves it. The condition is that the something else they searched for has to be indicative of the pain point. A person searching for a travel request form has revealed a corporate travel problem. A person searching how to invoice in Word has revealed an invoicing problem. That revealed pain, not product awareness, is what makes the sell land. This is the reasoning that licenses the whole deviant-keyword approach and it contradicts the usual funnel-stage rule.

> "Several marketing experts will say not to sell too hard to people"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated experience across client programs, contradicting the standard advice against selling to non-product-aware searchers.

**Pitfall:** The rule only holds when the query reveals a pain point. Selling hard on a purely informational query with no revealed problem produces the bounce rate the conventional advice warns about.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's articles aimed at clinic owners who have never heard of practice management software can still make the product case, provided the query itself revealed an operational pain such as no-shows or paper records.

**Apply anywhere:** The usual advice is not to sell to searchers who are not yet product-aware. Ignore it when the query itself reveals a pain point your product solves. Revealed pain, not category awareness, is what makes a switch argument work.

### 9. Separate real keyword stuffing from harmless over-repetition  `74.17`
*useful · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid says keyword stuffing is easy for Google to catch but is typically very egregious when it happens. What most people call stuffing, the keyword appearing in every H tag and in every sentence, is not stuffing in his reading. It is just over-repetition. He blames tools like RankMath and its keyword density score for spreading the confusion, calling the score nonsense. The practical consequence is that writers and editors spend effort managing a density number that has no basis, and sometimes make copy worse by forcing the phrase in to hit a target. His point is that repetition is a readability problem to fix on its own merits, not a penalty risk, while genuine stuffing is a different and much more obvious behaviour.

> "That's not keyword stuffing. That's just over repetition"

**Evidence:** David names RankMath's keyword density score as the source of the confusion, and states real keyword stuffing is typically very egregious.

**How to do it**

1. Turn off or ignore the keyword density score in your SEO plugin and remove it from editorial sign-off.
2. Stop instructing writers to hit a keyword count in headings or paragraphs.
3. Keep the exact target phrase in the title, H1, slug and first sentence, then let the rest of the copy read naturally.
4. Edit repeated phrasing because it reads badly, not because you fear a penalty.
5. Reserve real concern for egregious cases: hidden text, stacked keyword lists, or the phrase repeated consecutively.
6. Use varied wording across the body to widen query matching instead of repeating one phrase.

**Tools:** RankMath

**Pitfall:** Editing to a density target. It either forces awkward repetition in or strips out the term where it genuinely belonged, and neither is driven by a real signal.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial sign-off should not include a keyword density check. David should keep the front-loading rule (exact keyword in title, URL, H1 and first sentence) and drop any per-heading keyword quota.

**Apply anywhere:** Remove keyword density from editorial sign-off, front-load the exact phrase once in title, H1, slug and first sentence, and edit repetition for readability only.

### 10. Serve the search intent honestly before making any switch argument  `93.19`
*useful · content insights · source 93*

Goolding's closing rule for JTBD writing is that you properly and honestly serve the search intent: start by giving the reader the information they need to do the job, then make an up-front argument for why they might want a different solution in the future. He stresses both halves. Honest means the walkthrough is complete and correct even where completing it well removes the immediate need for your product, as with the Timetastic Outlook instructions. Up-front means the switch argument is stated openly rather than smuggled in, which is how the Timetastic post was written, without being coy. The tension people expect between the two does not appear in his results, because the reader who successfully did the job is the reader most aware of how painful it was.

> "it's important that you properly and honestly serve the search intent"

**Evidence:** The Timetastic Outlook post explained the full step-by-step for adding staff leave to Outlook and still produced 20 free trial signups in 6 months.

**Pitfall:** Half-answering the job to protect the product argument. The reader completes the task on a competing page instead and never sees your pitch at all.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's how-to posts should complete the manual clinic workflow properly, including the parts Pabau automates, before the Pabau section argues for the easier route.

**Apply anywhere:** Complete the job properly for the reader first, then make the case for your product openly, rather than withholding steps to force the pitch.

### 11. Site quality score is a 0-100 page score averaged sitewide  `10.10`
*useful · content insights · source 10*

Google reportedly assigns every individual page a 0-100-ish "quality score" — Charles gives illustrative examples like a page scoring 79.787, or a homepage scoring around 80 versus a thin category page with duplicate titles and a one-word meta title scoring around 20 — and the site's overall "sitewide quality score" is simply the mean of every page's individual score. Because it is a mean, fixing quality on a subset of pages moves the sitewide number only proportionally: Charles's example is a 100,000-page site with a sitewide score of 58.971 where improving the template for 10,000 of those pages might raise the sitewide average by only two or three points. The practical implication is that page-level score mostly drives that individual page's own ranking ability while the sitewide average mostly drives overall site visibility, and there is no universal 20% fix for raising an individual page's score since it depends entirely on matching that page's specific SERP's consensus, such as missing entities, meta titles, schema, images or video, and load time.

> "sitewide quality score is just the mean of all those page scores"

**Evidence:** Illustrative figures given: individual page scores like 79.787, 20, and 80 out of 100; a 100,000-page site example with sitewide score 58.971 where fixing 10,000 page templates raises the sitewide score by only about 2-3 points.

**Apply at Pabau:** For a site the size of Pabau's blog, don't expect fixing a handful of thin pages to move sitewide visibility; quality improvements need to hit a large proportional share of pages, such as a shared template or component fix across thousands of URLs, to shift the sitewide average meaningfully, while individual high-value pages should still be optimized page-by-page against their specific SERP's consensus signals.

**Apply anywhere:** For a site the size of your blog, don't expect fixing a handful of thin pages to move sitewide visibility; quality improvements need to hit a large proportional share of pages, such as a shared template or component fix across thousands of URLs, to shift the sitewide average meaningfully, while individual high-value pages should still be optimized page-by-page against their specific SERP's consensus signals.

### 12. Sort site content into three update buckets before planning any refreshes  `119.2`
*useful · best practices · source 119*

Grow and Convert split website content into three buckets, each with a different update rule. Brand-accuracy content covers product and service pages that describe what you sell; these update the moment the underlying product changes, with no schedule question at all. General marketing copy such as the home page, About page and resource pages follows the rule that if it is not broken, do not fix it; accurate pages that still hit their goal need nothing. Key SEO pages, mostly long-form blog content, are the only bucket where the timing question is real, and there the trigger is ranking performance. The point of the split is that most debates about update frequency wrongly treat all three as one problem.

> "three main buckets when it comes to updating website content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated framework from client work: only the SEO bucket has a genuine timing question.

**How to do it**

1. Export the full URL list for the site and tag each page as brand-accuracy, general marketing copy, or key SEO page.
2. For brand-accuracy pages, wire updates to the product release process rather than a content calendar.
3. When a feature ships, update the feature page and every other page describing that functionality the same week.
4. For general marketing pages, review only when the stated goal is being missed, not on a cycle.
5. For key SEO pages, add the target keyword to a rank tracker and let performance set the trigger.
6. Keep the three lists separate so a product change never waits behind an SEO refresh queue.

**Pitfall:** Running one refresh calendar over all three buckets. Product pages then go stale between cycles while healthy marketing pages get rewritten for no reason.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's pages describing product features should be updated on release, not in a blog refresh sweep. David should keep a separate list of feature-dependent pages so a Pabau GO or booking change propagates immediately.

**Apply anywhere:** Separate product-accuracy pages, general marketing pages and SEO pages into three lists with three different triggers. Only the SEO list needs a performance-driven refresh process.

### 13. Specific posts get easier to write, promote and rank than broad ones  `98.5`
*useful · content insights · source 98*

Hyam lists four compounding benefits of narrowing a topic, which is the argument for doing the split at all. First, the post becomes easier to write, because a defined topic removes the choice between angles that paralyzes a broad piece. Second, it resonates more, because a narrow topic speaks to a problem the reader actually has and builds a deeper relationship. Third, it becomes easier to promote: with nine specific posts, Sara Binde could join nutrition groups, food science groups and self-confidence groups and share the relevant one in each, which she could not have done with a single broad piece. Fourth, it ranks better on long tail phrases, because the post matches a specific question people type. He notes that when they asked her audience where they search for this advice, most said Google.

> "The benefits of using this strategy are threefold"

**Evidence:** Carob Cherub: one broad post became nine specific ones, each promotable to a different community group.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau argues for narrower articles internally, promotion is the strongest case. A post on injectable consent forms can be shared in injector Facebook groups; a general practice management guide cannot be shared anywhere specific.

**Apply anywhere:** Narrowing a topic pays off four ways: the piece is faster to write, it connects with the reader, it has an obvious community to be shared into, and it matches a real long-tail query.

### 14. Spend content effort on jobs with inadequate or no existing solutions  `75.14`
*useful · best practices · source 75*

The author's second JTBD shift is that effort should go to problems with inadequate solutions, or with no solutions at all. He repeats the rule twice, once as a product principle and once applied to content: a great product nails a poorly performed, very specific job, and so does great content. Read as a prioritization rule, it inverts the usual keyword logic. Instead of ranking candidates by volume and difficulty, you rank them by how badly the job is currently served. The prize is that these are the terms where he says success is close to guaranteed, because you are not competing on the same axis as the incumbents. The cost is that the poorly served jobs are usually specific and low volume, which makes them easy to talk a team out of.

> "problems with inadequate solutions"

**Evidence:** The author's twice-stated rule, credited to Nielsen, that a great product and great content both nail a poorly performed, very specific job, and his claim that underserved search terms guarantee SERP success.

**How to do it**

1. List the jobs your customers do around your product, including the ones you do not sell into.
2. For each job, search the way a customer would and record how well the best available answer performs it.
3. Score each job on how poorly it is served, from adequate to no solution exists.
4. Take the worst-served jobs first, regardless of their search volume.
5. Confirm demand with a real customer question rather than a volume figure, since specific jobs report low volume.
6. Estimate the build cost for the format that would serve the job properly.
7. Ship the cheapest format that fully completes the job, then improve it once it ranks.
8. Revisit the list quarterly, since competitors close gaps and new gaps open.

**Pitfall:** Killing the poorly served jobs at planning because their volume looks small. Those are exactly the terms the rule points at, and the volume tools underreport specific queries.

**Apply at Pabau:** Maintain a Pabau list of jobs aesthetic practices do that nothing online serves well, sourced from support tickets and sales calls. Prioritize by how badly they are served, not by volume, and accept low-volume winners.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep a list of the jobs your customers do that nothing online serves well, sourced from support and sales conversations. Prioritize by how badly the job is served rather than by search volume.

### 15. Spend on a creative asset as a proxy for uniqueness  `123.12`
*useful · best practices · source 123*

Hyam offers a rough test for whether a launch idea is actually hard to replicate. He says investing in some unique creative endeavor is generally a good sign that you are on the right track to making unique, hard-to-replicate content. The mechanism is that the cost is the moat: if the piece required commissioned design work, original research or a production effort, a competitor cannot copy it in an afternoon with a writer. He immediately qualifies it in both directions. You do not have to hire designers, since Grow and Convert's own launch was a public challenge that cost nothing but exposure, and 20,000 visitors in a week is not the bar for a successful launch. The test is a heuristic, not a spend requirement.

> "generally investing in some unique creative"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's own launch required no creative spend and produced 1,878 visitors and 196 subscribers in two weeks, which Hyam presents as a successful launch at a different scale.

**How to do it**

1. Ask what the piece would cost a competitor to reproduce, in money, time or access.
2. If the answer is 'a writer and two hours', the idea is not a launch asset.
3. Look for a cost that is not cash: your own data, your own customers, a public commitment, or personal risk.
4. Where cash is the only available moat, spend it on original design, original research or a build, not on longer copy.
5. Set the success bar for your own size, since Hyam says 20,000 visitors in a week is not required for a launch to count.
6. Sanity check the idea against the 'would a reader say wow' question before committing budget.

**Pitfall:** Assuming spend equals uniqueness. An expensive but derivative asset, such as a glossy version of a guide five competitors already published, is still replicable and still hard to pitch.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's original visuals requirement already forces some of this. David should aim the spend at assets competitors cannot copy, such as anonymized platform data on booking or no-show patterns, rather than better-looking versions of existing guides.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge a launch asset by what it would cost a competitor to reproduce. Prefer moats that are not cash, such as your own data, your own customers or a public commitment, and when you do spend, spend on original design, research or a build rather than on longer copy.

### 16. Split near-identical keywords because you only get one title and H1  `121.6`
*useful · content insights · source 121*

Grow and Convert give a mechanical reason for building a dedicated page per keyword even when two terms mean nearly the same thing. Their example is 'sick leave tracking' and 'sick leave app'. The intent behind both is a tool to track employee sick leave, but Google treats them as distinct queries and the pages that rank for each are often different. Citing their conversation with Bernard Huang of Clearscope, they point out that a page has only one SEO title, one H1, one meta description, and those are key ranking inputs. A page forced to serve two target keywords has to pick which of them those single slots address. The outcome they describe is that the page ranks for one term only, or matches neither intent closely enough and ranks for neither.

> "you only get one SEO title, one H1 heading, one meta description"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite 'sick leave tracking' versus 'sick leave app' as terms with near-identical intent but different ranking pages.

**Tools:** Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Merging two synonyms into one page to save production time. The signal is a page sitting on page two for both terms while a thinner competitor page ranks first for each separately.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should check the near-synonym pairs on pabau.com, such as clinic booking software versus appointment booking system, and split any page currently carrying both into two with distinct titles and H1s.

**Apply anywhere:** Give near-identical keywords separate pages. A page has one title, one H1 and one meta description, so forcing it to serve two queries means it addresses neither precisely.

### 17. State each supporting artifact once and in one place only  `71.20`
*useful · best practices · source 71*

Devesh found the same fault twice in the same section. The draft mentioned that the super brief is built from a brand summary, then a few lines later introduced step one as brand learning, saying the same thing again. His correction is blunt: just say it once, say it in one place. He notes immediately that this is so common, again with humans too. The failure is not a repeated sentence, which is easy to spot, but the same fact arriving twice in two different registers, once as passing context and once as a numbered step. His fix is to decide which slot the fact belongs in, usually the structured one, and strip it from the other. He applied the same rule to the search-intent categorization, which had already appeared above and needed moving rather than restating.

> "Just say it once. Say it in one place"

**Evidence:** Devesh cut a duplicate reference to the brand summary that appeared both as prose and as step one, and moved a search-intent categorization passage that had already been covered above.

**How to do it**

1. List every supporting artifact or mechanism the article references, such as a brand summary, a data source or a setup step.
2. For each one, find every place it is mentioned across prose, numbered steps and captions.
3. Pick the single slot where it does the most work, normally the numbered step or the labeled section.
4. Delete the other mentions rather than shortening them.
5. Where the duplicate mention sits earlier in the piece, move the material instead of deleting, so the sequence stays intact.
6. Run the same check on human-written drafts, since Devesh says the fault is at least as common there.

**Pitfall:** The duplicate is hard to see because the two mentions are phrased differently and sit in different structures, one as prose and one as a step. Search by fact, not by phrasing.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau articles describe a Pabau feature both in a body section and in the Pabau section before the Conclusion, David should keep the mechanism in one place and let the other reference point to it.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit drafts by fact rather than by phrasing. When the same mechanism appears once as prose and once as a numbered step, keep the structured slot and cut the other.

### 18. State first-hand treatment experience as the article's credibility claim  `117.15`
*useful · best practices · source 117*

Grow and Convert's credibility device in healthcare is not an author bio or a schema field. It is a sentence in the introduction saying the information in the piece comes from the organization's first-hand experience treating patients with this condition. The reader's takeaway is that these people treat this problem daily and are speaking from practice rather than compiling literature. It does double duty: it is the E-E-A-T claim and it is the first disclosure that a relevant service exists. This is a cheap, concrete version of experience signaling that most sites skip in favor of a credentials line. In healthcare it is also the honest differentiator against the institutional pages that outrank you, which summarize rather than treat.

> "it helps establish credibility in the eyes of readers"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX articles open this way and the program produces over 50% of all leads on the site.

**How to do it**

1. Write one sentence in the introduction stating the article draws on your organization's direct experience with this problem.
2. Be specific about the basis: how many patients or customers, over what period, in what setting.
3. Name the internal expert interviewed and their credential in the body, near their contribution.
4. Attribute the distinctive claims in the piece to that expert explicitly, so the experience is visible.
5. Keep the claim honest and verifiable; do not assert experience you cannot evidence.
6. Repeat the same device on every article in the cluster so the site reads consistently.

**Pitfall:** A generic authority claim with no numbers, no named expert and no attributed insight reads as boilerplate and does nothing for either trust or differentiation.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau blog intros on clinical-operations topics should say the guidance comes from working with named practices, and attribute distinctive points to the specific clinician or specialist interviewed.

**Apply anywhere:** Open the article by stating it is based on your organization's direct experience with the problem, with specifics, and attribute the distinctive claims to a named internal expert.

### 19. Stop excusing thin intros with the claim that readers skip them  `124.9`
*useful · best practices · source 124*

Grow and Convert name a specific belief that produces bad intros: writers have been taught, or simply believe, that most people skip the intro and go straight to the body, so they submit very light intros. Their counter is a causal inversion. People skip intros because they are weak, not the other way round. Make the intro strong and people will read it. And if some readers still skip it, there is no loss, because the same valuable information is in the body paragraphs. The asymmetry is the point: a strong intro has upside and no downside, while a weak one guarantees the skip it was written to accommodate. This contradicts the common advice to keep intros to two sentences and get out of the way.

> "If you make the intro strong, people will read it"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report writers submitting deliberately light intros on this reasoning, and their own client work relies on strong intros for bottom-of-funnel pieces.

**How to do it**

1. Ask any writer who submits a two-line intro whether they believe readers skip intros, and address the belief directly.
2. Require the intro to carry real information, not a runway into the body.
3. Build the intro from the body's most specific findings rather than from category context.
4. Cut any opening sentence that states something the searcher already knows by virtue of searching.
5. Keep the same information available later in the body so a skimmer loses nothing.
6. Test the intro by asking whether it names a claim a competitor page does not make.
7. Review the intro last, after the body is settled, so you know what you have to sell.
8. Track whether strong intros change scroll depth or conversion before standardizing the length.

**Pitfall:** Writing intros as throat-clearing on the theory nobody reads them, which then guarantees nobody does. The signal is an intro that could sit on any article about the category.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau intros should carry the specific practice pain point and the article's actual finding, not a paragraph about why the topic matters. David should reject the short-intro rationale when a writer offers it.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not accept a thin intro on the theory that readers skip intros. Readers skip weak intros, so build the intro from the body's best specifics; anyone who skips it still gets the same information later.

### 20. Stop treating length as the definition of great content  `100.10`
*useful · content insights · source 100*

Grow and Convert open by arguing that marketers have become too focused on length as the key factor in great content, which has produced too many long posts with nothing original to say. They ask how many epic 3,000-plus-word posts you have read that taught you nothing, and answer that length is not a sufficient definition. Their definition is business results: the piece drives traffic, a decent fraction of that traffic is qualified, and it drives leads or sales. They tie the length obsession back to their earlier concept of Mirage Content, content that looks and feels like it should work because it is long, but does not help the business when you measure it. They also blame a misreading of the Skyscraper Technique, where taller has come to mean longer exclusively, even when the writer has nothing original to add.

> "length is not a sufficient definition for great content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's Mirage Content concept: content that looks and feels like it should work because it is long, but shows no business impact when measured.

**Pitfall:** Word-count targets in a brief guarantee padding. The signal is a piece that hits 3,000 words while its unique contribution fits in a sentence.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should drop word-count minimums from Pabau briefs and replace them with a required originality line plus a qualified-traffic target. Judge existing long articles by leads, not by length.

**Apply anywhere:** Remove word-count targets from briefs and judge content by traffic quality and leads instead. Long posts with nothing original are the most common failure, not short ones.

### 21. Support every claim with a detail and skip beginner-level introductions  `96.17`
*useful · best practices · source 96*

Grow and Convert close on two writing rules they have documented separately. Their detail principle argues that providing details to support the claims made throughout a blog post is what makes it good. Their introduction advice is to write openings that do not make the piece seem beginner level to the target audience, which for them means opening on the specific pain point rather than on background. They tie both to the specificity strategy and to originality nuggets, their term for the unique element that stops a post being generic. Taken together, the standard is that every assertion carries a supporting specific, and the first paragraph signals to an expert reader that the piece is not written for beginners.

> "the detail principle, which argues that providing details"

**How to do it**

1. Read the draft and mark every claim that stands without a supporting specific.
2. Replace each unsupported claim with a number, an example, a screenshot or a named scenario.
3. Rewrite the introduction so the first sentence names the reader's specific situation.
4. Delete background and definition sentences from the opening if the audience already knows them.
5. Confirm the piece contains at least one thing not found in any page-one result.
6. Check the piece would still be worth reading by someone who works in the field daily.

**Pitfall:** An introduction that starts with industry background. An expert reader concludes within two sentences that the piece is beginner level and leaves before the useful part.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's originality-nugget requirement already covers the unique element; add a pass that flags claims with no supporting detail, since aesthetics content is especially prone to unsupported assertions about compliance and revenue.

**Apply anywhere:** Support every claim with a concrete detail, and open on the reader's specific situation rather than on background they already have.

### 22. The product-usage-to-SEO feedback loop  `62.4`
*useful · content insights · source 62*

The mechanism the episode identifies as the compounding engine: the more people actively use Canva, the more likely they are to share their designs, link back to Canva, and contribute to its overall online presence - all of which boosts SEO performance, which in turn drives more product usage. The product drives the SEO and the SEO drives the product usage. The framing worth taking is that the loop only exists because sharing is a native part of the product experience - the shared design carries a link back, so usage produces links without a link building programme. Cody's separate observation in the same corpus is the same point from the other direction: tools with a share feature keep earning links, which can offset content decay.

> "the product drives the SEO"

**How to do it**

1. Look for the point in your product where a user naturally wants to share something.
2. Make the shared artefact public and linkable, carrying an attribution link back.
3. Ensure the shared artefact is indexable, so the share creates a page as well as a link.
4. Measure links and referring domains generated by product usage separately from your link building.
5. Feed the loop by promoting the sharing behaviour, not just the product.
6. Treat this as the offset against content decay rather than as a growth channel on its own.

**Pitfall:** The claim that user engagement is a huge ranking factor is stated as fact by the AI narration and should be treated as the episode's assertion rather than a documented mechanism - the link generation from sharing is the concrete part.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's shareable artefacts - forms a clinic sends to patients, booking pages, reports - are the analogue: anything a customer sends outward is a chance for an indexable, linked page, which is link acquisition that scales with usage rather than with budget.

**Apply anywhere:** Anything your customers send outward - documents, booking pages, reports - is a chance for an indexable, linked page, which is link acquisition that scales with usage rather than with budget.

### 23. Title an integration article by attacking the category, not by matching it  `103.13`
*useful · content insights · source 103*

For '[industry] software for [integration]' keywords, Grow and Convert's live example takes a contrarian line rather than a neutral roundup. Targeting 'HVAC software compatible with QuickBooks' for ServiceTitan, the article is titled 'Why Most HVAC Software Compatible with QuickBooks Are Insufficient (And How Ours Works Differently)'. The title still carries the exact keyword, so the match holds, but the angle is a claim about the category rather than a list of options. It works because integration searches carry a hidden worry: the searcher wants to know whether the integration actually does what they need, not just which vendors advertise it. The same construction appears in Timetastic's 'How to keep track of staff holidays (without clumsy spreadsheets)', where the parenthetical names the inadequate alternative.

> "Targets keyword "HVAC software compatible with QuickBooks"

**Evidence:** ServiceTitan's page for 'HVAC software compatible with QuickBooks' and Timetastic's 'How to keep track of staff holidays (without clumsy spreadsheets)' both ship this construction.

**How to do it**

1. Keep the exact keyword phrase intact at the front of the title.
2. Add a parenthetical or clause that names the inadequacy of the obvious solution, such as spreadsheets or a shallow integration.
3. In the article, document specifically what most integrations in the category fail to sync or automate.
4. Back the claim with named limits from competitor documentation, not with adjectives.
5. Show your own integration handling the exact case the others miss, with a screenshot.
6. Cover the honest workaround for readers who will not switch, so the piece stays useful.
7. Check the SERP first; if it is a straight vendor list, test the contrarian angle on one page before scaling it.

**Pitfall:** A contrarian title with no evidence underneath reads as vendor bias and loses the reader in the first section. The claim needs specific competitor limits documented, or the page converts worse than a neutral list.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's integration and 'without spreadsheets' angles can use this title shape, for example on booking or accounting integrations, provided the article documents exactly what the shallow integrations fail to sync.

**Apply anywhere:** For integration keywords, keep the exact phrase in the title and add a clause naming why the usual options fall short, then prove that claim with documented competitor limits.
