### 1. Narrow topical focus can beat far bigger sites in YMYL SERPs  `139.11`
*core · content insights · source 139*

Brandon's Keeper Tax pages outranked Entrepreneur, The Motley Fool and the IRS itself for some tax keywords. Benji and Devesh's explanation was that Google understands Keeper Tax is solely focused on independent contractor and self-employed taxes, while those competitors are broad finance and government sites covering everything. The narrow entity beat the bigger but broader ones on queries inside its focus. Brandon pairs this with the E-A-T requirement in a money topic: every article was written by qualified accountants, which he calls absolutely critical for ranking in health, wealth and safety niches. The two work together. Focus decides which SERPs you can win at all, and demonstrated expertise decides whether you are trusted enough to appear in a YMYL one.

> "Google understands that Keeper Tax is solely focused on independent contractor taxes"

**Evidence:** Keeper Tax outranked Entrepreneur, The Motley Fool and the IRS on some tax keywords, with all articles written by qualified accountants.

**How to do it**

1. Write one sentence naming the single narrow subject your site is authoritative on.
2. Audit published content and count how many pieces sit outside that subject.
3. Stop commissioning anything outside it, and prune or consolidate the strays.
4. Confine your keyword list to queries inside the narrow subject, including the qualified long tail.
5. Have every piece in a money, health or legal topic written or reviewed by a credentialed practitioner.
6. Publish the reviewer's name, credentials and a real bio page, and cite it on the article.
7. Compare your rankings against broad incumbents on your narrow queries to confirm the focus is paying.
8. Only widen the subject after the narrow set is exhausted and ranking.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Sites dilute the focus signal by publishing adjacent general content for traffic, then lose the advantage that let them beat larger competitors in the first place.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's advantage is being narrowly about running an aesthetic or healthcare practice, so David should keep articles inside that and have clinical or compliance claims reviewed and bylined by a named practitioner.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep your content inside one narrow subject so search engines read you as the specialist, and have credentialed practitioners write anything in a money, health or legal topic.

### 2. PageRank is now a per-topic score, not one global number  `53.13`
*core · content insights · source 53*

David Quaid reframes the old PageRank model, historically a single number between roughly one and a billion or a trillion for a whole page or domain, as now functioning more like a separate authority number per topic and subtopic. Under this model, when a page links to your page, what transfers isn't one universal authority value but the linking page's authority specifically within that shared topic, which effectively adds to your own page's topical authority number for that same topic. He connects this to a separate, ongoing role for backlinks: even though backlinks now count for less of the overall authority equation than they used to, they remain important because losing click-through rate or suffering pogo-sticking can counteract topical authority to a large degree, meaning backlink authority can act as a buffer that keeps a page from being demoted even when its behavioral signals wobble.

> "each topic and subtopic has an authority number as a whole"

**Evidence:** David Quaid's direct reframing: "each topic and subtopic has an authority number as a whole," with a linking page's topical authority and the receiving page's topical authority being essentially added together within that shared topic, paired with his claim that backlink authority can counteract topical-authority losses caused by falling CTR or pogo-sticking.

**Apply at Pabau:** When link-building for Pabau, David should prioritize backlinks from pages that are themselves topically relevant and authoritative within the same subtopic, e.g., med-spa software or clinic scheduling, rather than treating all backlinks as contributing to one generic authority score, and should treat backlink acquisition as insurance against ranking loss on pages where behavioral signals like CTR and pogo-sticking are already under strain.

**Apply anywhere:** When link-building, prioritize backlinks from pages that are themselves topically relevant and authoritative within the same subtopic as the target page, rather than treating all backlinks as contributing to one generic authority score, and treat backlink acquisition as insurance against ranking loss on pages where behavioral signals like CTR and pogo-sticking are already under strain.

### 3. Pair every on-site claim with external proof  `40.3`
*core · best practices · source 40*

Kasra's framework for building believable authority is 'claim, frame, and prove': your own website stating something about itself, such as 'we're the best SEO software,' is only the claim, and it 'doesn't mean much' on its own, per both Kasra and Jason Barnard, whom he cites making the same point. The 'prove' step requires genuinely external sources - third-party reviews, guest posts, comparison mentions - independently saying the same thing. As a concrete way to generate proof at scale, Kasra's Masterminders conference emailed its 150 attendees asking for an honest review of the event, and got about 25 people to write public reviews covering what was good and bad.

> "What you want is claim, frame, and prove"

**How to do it**

1. For every notable claim you make about your brand or product on your own site, identify at least one external, independent source that could also make that same claim.
2. Build or acquire that external proof asset deliberately rather than assuming the on-site claim is self-sufficient - options include soliciting customer reviews, pitching guest posts on relevant industry sites, or getting cited in a comparison or roundup article.
3. When soliciting proof at scale, such as after an event, send a direct follow-up email asking attendees or customers to write a short public review of their experience, framed as helping you improve.
4. Register or build a dedicated reviews asset, such as an exact-match domain or a /reviews page on your existing domain, to consolidate and re-publish this third-party proof in one place.
5. Cross-link the external proof from your owned claim pages, and make sure the external proof source links back to you, so both search engines and AI systems can connect claim to proof.
6. Periodically audit your top brand or product claims and flag any claim with zero external corroboration as a content gap to fill.

**Pitfall:** A claim that only exists on your own website 'doesn't mean much,' in Kasra's words - no matter how well-written, on-site-only marketing claims lack the external corroboration that both users and AI systems weigh when building consensus about a brand.

### 4. Penalties are a rank backstop; PageRank keeps calculating underneath  `09.8`
*core · content insights · source 09*

YMYL is framed here as scoped to specific phrases, not entire sites: if Google doesn't trust a page for a given phrase, it simply won't surface that page for that query, rather than applying some special site-wide "harder" standard. A penalty (YMYL-related or otherwise) is modeled as a "backstop" — a rank ceiling, e.g. capped around position 55 or 100 — while the page continues to be crawled, indexed, and refreshed, and its PageRank/authority keeps being calculated in the background the entire time; when the penalty lifts, the page returns to its true position because the underlying authority calculation never stopped. The speaker adds that YMYL doesn't require serving more E-A-T and that author bylines aren't necessarily what resolves it, calling the push toward visible E-A-T signals "largely... a vanity project and a brand protection project" rather than a proven fix.

> "all of your PageRank authority is still calculated"

**Evidence:** Presented as the speaker's own operating model of Google's trust/penalty mechanism, illustrated with the position-55/100 "backstop" framing rather than a cited external study.

**Apply:** If a Pabau page underperforms on a sensitive or YMYL-adjacent query, don't default to adding more author-bio or credential content as the fix — treat it as a trust/backstop issue tied to that specific phrase, keep building normal authority signals, and expect any resolution to arrive as a step-change once Google's trust threshold for that phrase clears, not as gradual creep.

### 5. Refuse every revenue source that would compromise the trust you are selling  `171.7`
*core · best practices · source 171*

Examine.com started without knowing what it would sell, and made an early decision to refuse anything that would breach the integrity of the brand. Sol Orwell lists what they ruled out: no supplements, no coaching, no consulting, no ads, no donors, no sponsors. That refusal was the differentiator, because the problem they had identified was precisely that every existing supplement information source was selling ads or supplements and therefore could not be trusted. Ruling out the obvious monetization routes forced them to find one that did not conflict, which turned out to be paid reference products bought by the same readers. The trust was the product, so any revenue that eroded it would have destroyed the asset.

> "no supplements, coaching, consulting, ads, donors or sponsors"

**Evidence:** Examine.com ruled out supplements, coaching, consulting, ads, donors and sponsors, and became a go-to reference in health and nutrition with roughly 2M monthly visitors.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the one thing readers must believe about you for the site to be worth visiting.
2. List every revenue source available to you and mark each one that would give a reader a reason to doubt that belief.
3. Rule those out in public, on an about or transparency page, so the refusal is verifiable.
4. Accept that this narrows monetization to things your readers pay you for directly.
5. Ask the audience what they would pay for, rather than taking the money already being offered by advertisers.
6. Re-test the list whenever a new revenue offer arrives, and apply the same question to it.

**Pitfall:** Taking sponsor or affiliate money on a site whose entire value is independence. The moment a reader can name your incentive, your answers become as discountable as the ones you set out to replace.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog and template pages are vendor content, so the equivalent discipline is not refusing revenue but being explicit about the vendor relationship and not overstating competitors' weaknesses. Independence you cannot claim, honesty you can.

**Apply anywhere:** Name the belief your content depends on, then refuse the revenue sources that would give readers a reason to doubt it. If you cannot claim independence because you are a vendor, be explicit about the relationship instead of pretending otherwise.

### 6. Register on Barnard's six-platform starter set for a new entity  `73.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 73*

Asked which platforms a new business must register on, Barnard names a short list rather than a sprawling citation campaign: TheOrg.com, Crunchbase, LinkedIn, then the major social platforms, where he rates Facebook very good, Twitter very good and Instagram less good. He adds one review platform, suggesting Trustpilot or whatever the dominant local review platform is in your country. With just those plus a properly built entity home, he says you would expect a knowledge panel in three months to a year, though he is explicit that this is his experience and not a guarantee. His four-to-five-month case worked because the company was brand new, so the profiles could be created correct from the very beginning: Crunchbase built fresh, and a LinkedIn profile that was new and barely filled in, so there was no existing mess to clean up. For people specifically he singles out Muck Rack as powerful if you write articles.

> "The org.com, Crunchbase, LinkedIn,"

**Evidence:** Barnard's new-company client got a knowledge panel in four to five months building exactly this set from scratch; an established company with a bigger, messier footprint took nine months.

**How to do it**

1. Create profiles in this order: TheOrg.com, Crunchbase, LinkedIn company page, Facebook, Twitter/X, then one review platform such as Trustpilot.
2. Fill every profile from the same fundamental-facts sheet so the name, founding date, category and description match the entity home exactly.
3. Set each profile's website field to the entity home URL.
4. Add Muck Rack for individual people who publish articles.
5. Add the dominant local review platform for your country alongside or instead of Trustpilot.
6. Skip Instagram as an entity signal if effort is limited, since Barnard rates it less useful than Facebook and Twitter.
7. Check Kalicube's trusted-sources list for further platforms Google's knowledge graph actually draws on, rather than picking directories by domain authority.
8. Link from the entity home to each finished profile once its facts are verified.

**Tools:** TheOrg.com, Crunchbase, LinkedIn, Facebook, Twitter/X, Trustpilot, Muck Rack, Kalicube Pro

**Pitfall:** Building profiles fast with slightly different descriptions on each. Inconsistency across the footprint is the second of the three mistakes Barnard names, and it costs more time to unpick later than it saved.

**Apply at Pabau:** Audit Pabau's existing Crunchbase, LinkedIn and Trustpilot entries against the About page facts, add TheOrg.com if it is missing, and make sure each carries the identical one-line description.

**Apply anywhere:** Register the entity on TheOrg.com, Crunchbase, LinkedIn, Facebook, Twitter/X and one review platform, filling all of them from a single facts sheet. Add Muck Rack for individuals who publish. Expect three months to a year for a panel.

### 7. Remove every contradicting fact from the entity home first  `73.2`
*core · best practices · source 73*

Barnard names clarity as the single most important factor for getting a knowledge panel, and the most common failure he sees is self-contradiction on the entity home itself. He describes a client whose page carried three different founding dates for the same company. To a machine that is immediately ambiguous, and ambiguity is what stops the confidence score rising. The facts Google looks for are narrow and specific: the name, where and when the entity was born or founded, the subtitle (which you get to choose - Barnard uses 'entrepreneur'), the description, and which photos to use. Name consistency on the page itself is the thing almost everyone gets wrong. He frames Google as a child trying to understand the world, not a judge: it is not fussy about fame, it just wants a page that clearly represents one specific nameable thing.

> "he had three dates for the founding of his company on the same page"

**Evidence:** Barnard's client had three separate founding dates on a single page, which he cites as the immediate reason the entity was unclear to Google.

**How to do it**

1. List the fundamental facts you want attached to the entity: exact legal name, founding date, subtitle or category, one-line description, and canonical photos.
2. Read the entity home line by line and find every place each fact appears, including image captions, schema, footers and boilerplate.
3. Delete or correct any variant so each fact appears in exactly one form. Founding dates and legal name are the usual offenders.
4. Use the identical name string every time on the page, with no abbreviations, no trading names and no 'we' substitutions in the factual sections.
5. Choose the subtitle deliberately, since Google will take a category label from your own wording.
6. Repeat the same audit across every profile you control before adding links to them.
7. Re-check after every site redesign, since template changes reintroduce old boilerplate facts.

**Pitfall:** Assuming only the visible body copy counts. Contradicting founding dates and name variants usually survive in footers, schema, press boilerplate and old author bios, and the machine reads all of them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Run a one-off fact audit across pabau.com: founding year, legal entity name, product family names and the one-line description. Fix any page where an older founding year or a legacy name variant still appears.

**Apply anywhere:** Before chasing a knowledge panel, audit your entity home for contradicting facts. One founding date, one legal name, one description, one subtitle, one photo set, repeated identically everywhere on the page and in schema.

### 8. Repeat who-what-why-brilliant everywhere to build entity confidence  `26.9`
*core · best practices · source 26*

To raise an entity's Knowledge Graph confidence score, Dooley uses a repeating three-part framework across every bio, press mention, and profile: state WHO the entity is (tied to verifiable facts — founding date, role, affiliated companies), WHAT it does (one clear line), and WHY it's brilliant (backed by third-party corroboration — awards, reviews, testimonials, case studies). Crucially the statements use the entity's actual name as the subject each time ('James Dooley is a UK entrepreneur') rather than pronouns, so Google's parser can directly bind each fact to the named entity, and the same wording should be repeated consistently across every new mention rather than varied.

> "repeat who you are, what you do, and why you're brilliant"

**How to do it**

1. Draft one canonical 'who' statement plus 1-2 verifiable facts (founding year, HQ, leadership) for the brand.
2. Draft one canonical 'what' statement describing the core offering in a single line (e.g., 'Pabau is practice management software for clinics and med spas').
3. Compile a running list of 'why' proof points: review-platform scores (Trustpilot, G2, Capterra), named customer case studies, awards, testimonials.
4. Every time you publish a guest post, press release, directory listing, partner mention, or bio, insert versions of all three elements using the brand name as the subject of each sentence rather than 'it' or 'we.'
5. Keep the category wording identical across mentions (always 'practice management software,' not alternating with 'clinic software' or 'medical spa platform') so the repetition pattern-matches.
6. Add Organization/sameAs schema on the brand's own site linking official profiles (LinkedIn, Crunchbase, Wikidata, review platforms) to reinforce the same claims machine-readably.
7. Check the Knowledge Graph confidence score periodically to see whether it rises as mentions accumulate. (inferred)

**Tools:** Schema.org markup, Google Knowledge Graph Search API

**Pitfall:** Avoid vague, pronoun-led writing in bios and mentions ('it's a great tool,' 'we are the best') — Google's semantic parsing needs the named entity repeated as the subject of each sentence to bind facts to it, so ambiguous references don't feed the Knowledge Graph at all.

### 9. Set a dated public traffic goal and report progress monthly  `123.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 123*

When Hyam and his partner launched Grow and Convert they made the launch itself the story. They publicly challenged themselves to grow a brand new domain to 40,000 monthly unique visitors in six months, and their first post was an opinion piece titled 'Content Marketing Has Become Too Trendy: Here's what we plan to do about it'. They promised monthly updates reporting exactly how much traffic and email list growth they had achieved. Hyam is careful about what made it work: transparent growth reporting was not new, but attaching a concrete time goal to the traffic goal was the unique part, and most sites will not make themselves that vulnerable. Readers stayed because they wanted to see whether the pair would fail.

> "we set a concrete time goal in addition to the traffic goal"

**Evidence:** 1,878 unique visitors and 196 email subscribers in the first two weeks; 39,105 unique visitors over five months, though they pivoted away from the challenge before the six months were up.

**How to do it**

1. Pick one metric you can verify publicly and a number that is genuinely uncertain, such as monthly unique visitors.
2. Attach a hard deadline to it, since Hyam says the time goal, not the transparency, was the differentiator.
3. Write the launch post as an opinion piece stating what is wrong with your industry, then present the challenge as your answer to it.
4. Commit in writing to a monthly update cadence and name the numbers you will publish each time.
5. Publish a screenshot of the actual analytics with every update so the reporting is checkable.
6. Promote each update post into the communities where your target readers already gather.
7. Keep publishing the updates even in bad months, because the possibility of failure is what holds the audience.
8. Convert followers to email at every update, since the audience is attached to the story and not to search.

**Pitfall:** Publishing a vague growth diary with no deadline or number. Without the concrete time goal there is no suspense, nobody follows along, and the update posts read as self-promotion.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau could run a dated public build in its own lane, for example committing to publish and report on a six-month test of how a named partner practice cut no-shows, with monthly numbers on the blog. It gives the blog a following that does not depend on search.

**Apply anywhere:** Make your launch the story: commit publicly to a specific metric by a specific date, publish monthly updates with real screenshots, and keep publishing them even when the numbers are bad, because the risk of failure is what keeps readers following.

### 10. Stack nine verifiable credibility signals on the About page  `69.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 69*

Smarty gives an explicit list of what should be prioritized on an About page, all of it verifiable: years in business, partnerships with known entities or brands, certifications, security verifications, notable clients, recognitions from known organizations, industry awards, mentions in authoritative publications, and testimonials from verifiable people. Her instruction is to be as factual and specific as possible, adding links to known entities, the years an award was given, and links to customer profiles on social media. The pattern behind the list is entity association: each item ties your business to another entity a model already knows, which is what makes the claim resolvable rather than self-asserted.

> "Any verifiable claims should be prioritized here"

**How to do it**

1. Build the nine-row list: years in business, partnerships, certifications, security verifications, notable clients, recognitions, awards, publication mentions, testimonials.
2. Fill each row only if you can name a specific entity, and delete the row if you cannot.
3. Add the year to every award and recognition rather than listing it undated.
4. Link every named partner, certifier and publication to that entity's own site.
5. Link each testimonial to the person's real social profile so the quote is attributable.
6. Get written permission before naming a notable client, then link the client's site.
7. Put the list in visible body copy, not in an image or a logo carousel.
8. Re-verify certifications and security attestations on their renewal dates.

**Pitfall:** Publishing an unattributed testimonial wall with first names and initials. Nothing on it resolves to a real entity, so it adds no verification value for either a rater or a model.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's About page should name its security and data-protection certifications, its integration partners, the year of each award, and link testimonials to the real practice owners who gave them.

**Apply anywhere:** Fill an About page with nine categories of verifiable proof, each naming and linking a specific external entity, and delete any category you cannot support with a name.

### 11. Stop optimizing for E-E-A-T as if it were a ranking factor  `74.18`
*core · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid reads the starter guide's treatment of E-A-T as a pure parody of the idea that it is a ranking factor, noting the guide links to the document showing it is not one. His view of the engine is that it is largely content-agnostic: it takes your content, indexes it if it judges the source authoritative enough, then tests it with users and measures the results. He backs this with the DOJ evidence against Google, which includes a slide from Google's 2018 onboarding deck, seized and still in use in 2024, saying they do not understand content and just fake it. His stress test is self-referential: he argues on YouTube, in blog posts, on Reddit and on X that Google cannot grade content, and those pages rank. He also asks how contradictory blog posts can all rank, and why chiropractors rank everywhere when Google knows there is no scientific proof behind it.

> "This is a pure parody of thinking that EAT is a ranking factor"

**Evidence:** Google's own linked documentation states E-A-T is not a ranking factor; David cites the DOJ exhibit of a 2018 Google onboarding slide, still in use in 2024, saying they do not understand content and fake it.

**How to do it**

1. Remove E-E-A-T scoring from your audit template and from content sign-off criteria.
2. Keep the tangible things people conflate with it, such as named authors, transparency pages and citations, because they help readers and conversion.
3. Judge those items on whether they earn trust and clicks, not on a hypothetical ranking score.
4. Redirect the effort into the two things Google's guide does name: links and matching the reader's search terms.
5. When an agency or tool sells an E-E-A-T score, ask which Google document supports it.
6. Test the claim yourself: check whether contradictory pages on the same topic both rank in your vertical.

**Pitfall:** Running an E-E-A-T improvement programme as if it were a technical fix with a measurable output. There is no signal behind the score, so the work has no way to be validated.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should keep author bios, clinical citations and transparency pages because they earn reader trust and support conversion, but David should stop treating an E-E-A-T checklist as an SEO deliverable with an expected ranking outcome.

**Apply anywhere:** Keep author bios, citations and transparency pages for readers and conversion, but remove E-E-A-T scoring from your SEO audit and stop expecting a ranking return from it.

### 12. Teach the whole method publicly and let execution gap close the sale  `168.8`
*core · best practices · source 168*

CPC Strategy made its webinars public to everyone, competitors included, and gave away the full method. Nii Ahene's reasoning is a specific business claim, not a slogan. Even if the agency taught the audience everything it knew, there would always be individuals and organizations without the time to execute at that level. The gap between knowing the tactic and running it week after week is where the agency sold. His stated goal was for people to conclude that these people have it covered and reach out. He adds that the number of competitors attending was ridiculous and that he did not care, because staying sharp meant always holding the best tactics, and the industry recognized who was ahead. Withholding the method would have cost the authority that generated the leads.

> "Even if we teach you everything there is"

**Evidence:** Nii Ahene says a large number of competitors attended CPC Strategy's public webinars and that he was comfortable with it because the firm stayed sharp and held the best tactics.

**How to do it**

1. Write the full method you would charge for, including the steps most competitors keep behind a sales call.
2. Publish it as a webinar or long guide with the actual thresholds, numbers and tool settings included.
3. Add nothing to hide the mechanism, and do not gate the useful half behind a form.
4. Close by naming the ongoing effort the method requires, such as the weekly cadence and the person-hours, so the reader can price doing it themselves.
5. Let competitors attend and take the material, and keep publishing on a fixed cadence so your public version stays ahead of theirs.
6. Track leads that cite a specific published tactic, since those arrive pre-sold and shorten the sales cycle.
7. Re-publish an updated version each year so the public method reflects your current practice rather than last year's.

**Pitfall:** This only works if you genuinely stay ahead. If your published method is your only method and you stop advancing it, giving it away hands competitors your entire differentiation.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish its full operational playbooks for aesthetic practices, including the steps a practice could run without buying software. The buyers who convert are the ones who read the playbook and conclude they do not have time to run it manually.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish the full method with real numbers and settings, name the ongoing effort it takes to run, and let competitors read it. The buyers who convert are the ones who realize they lack the time to execute it.

### 13. The real-company footprint is the prerequisite everyone skips  `56.18`

Asked what he does differently to avoid getting penalised, Cody's answer is mostly about footprint rather than content. **Apply anywhere:** If your site already clears this bar, that's the point worth internalising - the real-company signal underneath is why your content can rank on topics thinner competitors struggle with, so protect it rather than chasing volume.

### 14. Three-step entity disambiguation process plus a Wikidata entity  `29.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 29*

To stop AI models from confusing him with the more famous actor Hank Azaria, Hank built a three-step, closed-loop process. Step one: publish explicit, dedicated content, including a page literally titled 'Hank Azarian is not Hank Azaria' with photos and a clear breakdown of both identities - he confirms LLMs now quote that page directly. Step two: add structured Person schema markup on-site to give search engines and AI systems a machine-readable distinction, not just prose. Step three: get third parties to state the distinction in indexable, public content - Hank used podcast guest appearances specifically for this, building a 'media' page to aggregate the mentions. He layers on testing (checking how ChatGPT and Google resolve his name logged in vs. out, and with vs. without prior context) and finishes by claiming a Wikidata entity so the content, the on-site entity claim, and third-party/Wikidata signals all reinforce the same facts.

> "create distinction inbound — creating that distinction between yourself"

**How to do it**

1. Publish a dedicated on-site page that explicitly names both confusable entities (Hank's example: a page titled 'Hank Azarian is not Hank Azaria') with photos and a clear breakdown of who is who.
2. Add Person (or Organization) schema markup identifying the correct entity, using sameAs and other identity properties where available.
3. Test how AI systems currently answer identity queries about you/the brand across logged-in vs. logged-out sessions, and with vs. without prior conversation context.
4. Isolate which specific variation triggers confusion - the bare name, name plus location, name plus industry term, or common misspellings - by testing each separately.
5. Pursue third-party validation once on-site content and markup are live: get other people or publications to state the distinction in public, indexable content such as podcast interviews.
6. Build or update a 'media' page on your site that aggregates these third-party mentions.
7. Claim or create a Wikidata entity that reflects the same facts as your on-site content, closing the loop between your content, your claimed entity, and third-party signals.
8. Re-run the same identity test queries periodically after each step to confirm the disambiguation is taking hold. (inferred)

**Tools:** Wikidata, Schema.org Person markup, ChatGPT, Google

**Pitfall:** Publishing a single disambiguation page and stopping there doesn't fix AI confusion on its own - it needs to be paired with structured markup, third-party corroboration, and a claimed Wikidata entity to form a closed loop, or the AI misidentification will persist.

### 15. Trigger a knowledge graph update with persona schema plus search volume  `09.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 09*

Adding schema to your pages can alter or update your knowledge graph entry, but the source stresses the entry fundamentally "needs a lot of search" behind it first — built by running Google Ads on your own name/brand, getting on podcasts regularly to drive name searches, or checking Search Console for existing branded search volume.
