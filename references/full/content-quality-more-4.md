### 1. Google research papers are why top-funnel content has no thought leadership  `110.15`
*useful · content insights · source 110*

Grow and Convert give a mechanism for why top-funnel articles fail as thought leadership rather than just asserting it. Most such articles are what they call Google research papers. They are farmed out to freelance writers with little or no direct experience of the topic. Those writers search the topic, note down what others say, and largely regurgitate the same information in different words. The result technically covers the topic but has no originality, is not provocative and takes no interesting or unique stance. The diagnostic follows from the production method: if the writer had no first-hand experience and the research was a search session, the output cannot contain anything a knowledgeable buyer has not already read.

> "are what we like to call"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's description of the standard production process for top-funnel SaaS articles: freelance writers without topic experience searching, noting and rewording what already ranks.

**How to do it**

1. For each top-funnel piece in your archive, record who wrote it and what direct experience they had with the topic.
2. Read the piece and try to name one claim that is not in the top three ranking results.
3. If you cannot name one, classify the piece as a Google research paper.
4. Count what share of your archive falls into that bucket before defending it as thought leadership.
5. For future briefs, require either a practitioner writer or a recorded interview with one.
6. Make the brief name the stance the piece takes, so the writer cannot default to neutral summary.

**Pitfall:** Judging these pieces by completeness rather than originality. They cover the topic properly, which is why editors pass them, and the absence only shows as zero conversions.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should require either a writer with aesthetic-practice experience or a recorded interview with someone at Pabau who works with practices daily. Without that input a pabau.com article will restate what already ranks.

**Apply anywhere:** Classify any article where you cannot name one claim absent from the top three results as a Google research paper. Fix the production method rather than the draft: use a practitioner writer or a recorded expert interview, and make the brief name the stance.

### 2. Google's stated position is quality, not who wrote it  `72.23`
*useful · content insights · source 72*

Asked directly whether Google penalizes AI copy, Khanal separates what Google says from his own read. His understanding of Google's public statements is that they do not say they dislike AI-generated copy; they say they do not care whether a human or AI wrote it, only whether it is quality content that fulfills search intent. His opinion is that this is a smart long-term stance because the line is already unenforceable. He works through the watermarking case: if a piece is 10 percent watermarked versus 20 or 50, where is the cutoff, and what about a writer who developed all the arguments themselves, wrote them in their own language and used Claude only to translate into English, producing a fully watermarked but genuinely original piece. Earlier in the session he separately notes mass AI publishing does get caught, so his position is that scale and thinness are the risk, not authorship.

> "All we care about is quality and fulfilling search intent"

**Evidence:** Khanal contrasts Google's stated authorship neutrality with documented cases of mass AI publishers whose traffic tanked and did not recover in any short timeframe.

**Pitfall:** Reading Google's authorship neutrality as permission to publish at scale. Khanal notes elsewhere in the same session that sites publishing thousands of AI articles get caught and the traffic does not come back quickly.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's AI-assistance policy should be framed around thinness and originality, not around whether a draft was AI-assisted. The gate is whether the article makes arguments only Pabau could make.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat Google's position as authorship-neutral but quality-strict: the risk in AI-assisted content is thinness and scale, not the fact that a model helped write it.

### 3. HCU penalizes lack of monetization, not lack of helpfulness  `25.8`
*useful · content insights · source 25*

Koray gives three same-content, different-monetization comparisons to argue Helpful Content Update penalties track business function, not writing quality. In the allergy industry, purely informational sites were hit hard since 2023 because regulations prevent them from selling medication online, while allergy sites running on Shopify (an actual commerce function) were not hit and kept ranking. His own pet-industry site 'Horowitz Powers' took identical affiliate review content that was underperforming on a pure affiliate/blog site and republished it on an e-commerce site, and rankings increased post-HCU with the exact same text. In the golf industry, every affiliate site that survived the 2023 HCU now has either a Shopify shop subdomain or sells a magazine; pure affiliate content sites did not survive.

> "Content was the same, it was the same level of"

**Evidence:** Horowitz Powers (pet industry): identical affiliate content moved from a blog/affiliate site to an e-commerce site and ranked higher post-HCU. Allergy industry: Shopify-powered allergy sites survived while purely informational allergy sites were hit hard since 2023. Golf industry: HCU survivors all now sell something (Shopify shop or magazine).

**Apply at Pabau:** Since Pabau already sells software, make sure every content page visibly connects to that function (CTAs, demo bookings, in-app screenshots, pricing links) rather than assuming well-written informational content alone is HCU-safe; purely informational pages with no path to the product are the exact pattern Google has been penalizing across multiple industries.

**Apply anywhere:** Since you already sells software, make sure every content page visibly connects to that function (CTAs, demo bookings, in-app screenshots, pricing links) rather than assuming well-written informational content alone is HCU-safe; purely informational pages with no path to the product are the exact pattern Google has been penalizing across multiple industries.

### 4. Harvest executive opinions that sit outside your core product focus  `125.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert's fourth category is the peripheral opinion. Senior people, the CEO, head of product, head of growth, usually hold strong views about their industry that are outside the main focus of the company. Their example is Bernard Huang at Clearscope, whose product evaluates Google's existing rankings to see which keywords the algorithm wants to rank content for, but who holds all sorts of other opinions about SEO that could be turned into content. They say the same is true of their own agency: the core business is conversion-focused content marketing, but Devesh and the author have plenty to say about hiring marketers, promotion and analytics. The value is that these opinions are already formed and defensible, they just live in conversation and have never been written down.

> "peripherally about SEO that could be turned into thought leadership"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite Bernard Huang of Clearscope, whose peripheral SEO opinions extend well beyond his product's ranking-evaluation focus, and their own agency's views on hiring, promotion and analytics.

**How to do it**

1. List the topics adjacent to your product that leadership argues about internally.
2. Sit with each exec for 30 minutes and ask what the industry gets wrong about those adjacent topics.
3. Record the conversation and pull out any claim stated as a position rather than a description.
4. Check each position against the top 10 results for its obvious keyword to confirm nobody is already arguing it.
5. Write one piece per position, opening with the position rather than with background.
6. Keep the exec's name on the byline so the opinion has a person attached.
7. Reject positions the exec cannot defend with an example from their own work.

**Pitfall:** Adjacent-topic opinions can pull the blog away from anything commercial. If the piece has no path back to the product's audience, it earns attention from the wrong readers.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's leadership holds views on how aesthetic practices should be run, staffed and priced that are adjacent to the software. David can turn those into opinion articles that reach practice owners who are not yet searching for software.

**Apply anywhere:** Interview your leadership about the industry topics adjacent to your product. Those opinions are already formed and unwritten, and each one is a defensible opinion piece with a named author.

### 5. Insert your brand as the third name in competitor-versus-competitor comparisons  `103.5`
*useful · concrete actions · source 103*

When a brand is new or small, almost nobody searches '[your brand] vs [competitor]', so those pages have no demand behind them. Grow and Convert's workaround is to target '[competitor] vs [competitor]' keywords, then publish the article as '[competitor] vs [competitor] vs [brand]'. The searcher's demand is real and already flowing to a comparison the client is absent from; the three-way title piggybacks on it and inserts the client into the set of options being weighed. They run this to four names when the category warrants it. Their live examples are 'CallRail vs CallTrackingMetrics vs WhatConverts', 'ServiceTitan vs Housecall Pro vs Jobber', and a four-way 'Cypress vs. Selenium vs. Katalon Studio vs. Rainforest' targeting the keyword 'Katalon vs Selenium'.

> "insert them into the list of options being weighed by prospects"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert client pages: WhatConverts ranking a three-way for 'CallRail vs CallTrackingMetrics', ServiceTitan for 'Housecall Pro vs Jobber', Rainforest with a four-way for 'Katalon vs Selenium'.

**How to do it**

1. List every pair of competitors in your category that customers actually compare, ignoring your own brand.
2. Check volume for each '[A] vs [B]' pair in Ahrefs and keep any with real demand, even in the tens.
3. Write the article title as '[A] vs [B] vs [you]', keeping the searched pair first and in the searched order.
4. Cover A and B genuinely and in depth first, using their own documentation and pricing pages.
5. Add your product as the third column with the criterion on which it differs, not as a hard sell.
6. Where three or more rivals are compared together, extend to a four-way title as Rainforest did with Cypress, Selenium and Katalon.
7. Add a comparison table so the three-way structure is visible above the fold.
8. Track rankings for the two-name keyword, not the three-name title, since that is the actual query.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Reversing the searched pair or burying it behind your brand name breaks the title match with the query. The page then competes as a brand page with no demand behind it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish comparisons of the competitor pairs that aesthetic practices weigh against each other, with Pabau added as the third name. Those pages capture demand that brand-versus pages do not have yet.

**Apply anywhere:** Target the competitor pairs your buyers already compare, then publish as '[A] vs [B] vs [you]'. You piggyback on existing demand and add yourself to the shortlist.

### 6. Inspiration pages and industry pages segment the same library  `62.2`
*useful · content insights · source 62*

Two extensions of the same structure. Canva builds inspirational pages - picture collage ideas, Christmas list designs - on the understanding that people aren't only looking for tools, they're looking for ideas, and those pages capture a different search intent for the same underlying product. Separately, they build tailored landing pages by industry: agencies, educators, small businesses. The reasoning is that an agency employee needs social media graphics while an educator needs presentation templates or classroom posters, so the same library is segmented and re-presented per audience. The narration makes the double benefit explicit: it's not just SEO surface area, it's a better user experience, because each segment sees the subset that applies to them.

> "inspirational pages"

**How to do it**

1. Separate the two intents: people looking for a tool and people looking for ideas.
2. Build idea and inspiration pages that lead into the same product assets.
3. Identify your distinct customer segments and build a landing page per segment.
4. On each segment page, surface only the subset of your library that segment needs.
5. Use the segment's own vocabulary for the same underlying features.
6. Link segment pages into the main category hierarchy so they inherit and pass authority.

**Pitfall:** Segment pages that only reorder the same links without changing the framing are duplicate content with extra steps - the vocabulary and the selected subset are what make them distinct.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's segments are already obvious - med spa, aesthetics clinic, physio, salon - and each should have its own landing page surfacing only that segment's relevant templates and features, in that segment's language.

**Apply anywhere:** Your customer segments are usually obvious, and each should have its own landing page surfacing only that segment's relevant assets and features, in that segment's language.

### 7. Judge a competitor's post by its examples, not its headline  `97.10`
*useful · content insights · source 97*

The article's method for telling mirage content from real expertise is a side-by-side read of two results for the same query. Grow and Convert point out they found both with one plain Google search. The weak post promises a lot in the headline and delivers items like 'Links are the best way to increase your SEO. Get more of them!'. The strong post shows exactly how a named person, Emil, ranked number one by building a specific piece of content, promoting it and doing outreach, with real examples throughout. The takeaway for competitive analysis is that headline promises and ranking position tell you nothing about whether a competitor is beatable. Count the real examples. A competitor ranking on a fluff post is an opportunity, not a benchmark to copy.

> "and he gives REAL examples"

**Evidence:** A single search for 'how to get more SEO traffic' surfaced both a generic SEO-services post and Brian Dean's example-heavy Backlinko post.

**How to do it**

1. Search your target keyword and open the top ten results as a reader who would buy your product.
2. For each result, count the named people, named companies, real numbers and screenshots, and write the count in a sheet.
3. Mark any result whose advice items restate the goal rather than the method.
4. Treat a top-three result with a low example count as a beatable SERP and prioritize the keyword.
5. Plan your piece around a specific worked example you can supply that none of them have.
6. Ignore word count and heading structure as competitive signals, since fluff posts match those easily.

**Pitfall:** Copying the outline of a ranking competitor reproduces their fluff and gives readers no reason to choose you. A high-ranking post can still be losing every conversion it gets.

**Apply at Pabau:** When David scores a Pabau SERP, he should count concrete examples in the ranking pages. Where competitors publish generic advice, a Pabau article with a real practice workflow and real numbers can outperform without matching their domain strength.

**Apply anywhere:** Score competing pages by how many real examples, names and numbers they contain, and treat a thin top-ranking page as a keyword opportunity rather than a template to copy.

### 8. Judge a content asset by whether researchers ask for the raw data  `168.14`
*useful · best practices · source 168*

Nii Ahene offers an unusual quality test without framing it as one. He notes that people pursuing master's degrees and PhDs contacted CPC Strategy asking for its raw information because they wanted to research the space. That inbound request is a sharper signal than traffic or shares. It means the dataset is being treated as primary source material rather than as marketing, and primary source material is what gets cited by other publishers, quoted by journalists and reused by academics for years. The practical use is as a target: design data assets so that the raw data request is plausible, which forces a stated methodology, a real sample and dimensions someone outside your commercial interest would care about.

> "asking for our raw information because they wanted"

**Evidence:** Nii Ahene reports that a number of master's and PhD candidates contacted CPC Strategy asking for its raw campaign data for their own research.

**How to do it**

1. Set the goal for each data asset as being citable by someone with no commercial interest in you, not as generating traffic.
2. Publish the sample size, collection window, and exact definition of every metric alongside the findings.
3. Include at least one dimension that serves a researcher rather than a buyer, such as change over time or distribution rather than an average.
4. Add a visible line offering the underlying data on request, with a contact route.
5. Log every raw-data request with the requester's institution or publication and treat that count as the asset's real score.
6. Follow up with each requester when their paper or article publishes, and ask for the citation link.
7. Retire data formats that generate traffic but never a single data request, and rebuild them around a stated methodology.

**Pitfall:** A report with no stated methodology cannot be cited by anyone careful, so it collects social shares and no durable references. The signal is zero inbound data requests after a year of publishing.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any Pabau benchmark report should publish its methodology and sample and offer the anonymized dataset on request. Requests from academics or trade analysts are the metric to track, because those become the citations that put Pabau into AI answers about practice operations.

**Apply anywhere:** Design data assets to be citable by someone with no stake in you: publish the sample and method, offer the raw data on request, and track inbound data requests as the asset's real score rather than shares.

### 9. Judge a post idea on specificity after pain point and audience fit  `98.10`
*useful · best practices · source 98*

Hyam identifies the exact reason bad ideas survive review. The generic ideas his program participants submitted passed two real tests: they were generally solving a pain point, and they related to the audience that would be interested in the product. On the surface they looked like good content ideas. Where they fell short was specificity, and specificity is the check most review processes never apply. He is blunt about the consequence of skipping it: by trying to create a post about a subject that broad, your post will be average at best. He also names the outcome for a post that fails the test, calling it 'mirage content' - something that looks like content from a distance but delivers nothing when the reader arrives.

> "But where these ideas fall short is specificity."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's program participants submitted ideas that passed pain point and audience checks but failed specificity.

**How to do it**

1. Score every idea on three checks in order: does it solve a pain point, does it fit the audience, is it specific enough.
2. Treat the first two as necessary but not sufficient, and require all three to pass.
3. For the specificity check, require the writer to name the single question the post answers in one sentence.
4. Reject the idea if that sentence contains an 'and' joining two separate questions.
5. Reject if the answer sentence could serve as the title of an existing site section.
6. Record the rejection reason so the pattern is visible across a quarter's calendar.

**Pitfall:** An idea that solves a pain point and fits the audience feels approved already, so reviewers stop there. That is exactly how average posts get commissioned, and Hyam calls the result mirage content.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add specificity as an explicit third gate on Pabau's article approval, with the one-sentence question written down in the brief. An article whose question needs an 'and' is two articles.

**Apply anywhere:** Make specificity a formal third approval gate after pain point and audience fit. Require the writer to state the single question the post answers in one sentence, and reject anything that needs an 'and'.

### 10. Judge briefs on the reader's circumstances, not on personas  `75.11`
*useful · best practices · source 75*

The author's first JTBD shift for marketers is that customer demographics and product features stop being the organizing input, and the customer's circumstances take their place. The evidence behind it is Moesta's finding that among condo buyers there was no demographic or psychographic pattern, and no feature set buyers valued enough to tip a decision. What predicted the purchase was the situation they were in and what they were afraid to lose. He is careful not to overclaim: he says he is not telling teams to throw out persona targeting, because moving a product team off personas is a long-term change. His narrower claim is that content teams can work at the point where the customer's circumstance meets the product's value, and that is enough to change what gets published.

> "customers' circumstances are more important"

**Evidence:** Moesta found no demographic, psychographic or feature pattern among actual condo buyers; the deciding factor was the situation and the fear of giving up memories.

**How to do it**

1. Replace the persona line at the top of each brief with a one-sentence description of the circumstance triggering the search.
2. Write that circumstance as a moment, for example the front desk just lost a third booking this week.
3. Delete feature lists from the brief unless a feature resolves that specific circumstance.
4. Keep personas for paid targeting and product decisions rather than fighting to remove them.
5. Cross-check that the circumstance came from a customer conversation, not from an assumption.
6. Map each circumstance to the point where it overlaps with what your product does well.
7. Reject briefs where the circumstance is generic enough to fit any reader in the industry.
8. Reuse strong circumstances across formats, since one circumstance can justify an article, a template and a tool.

**Pitfall:** Rewriting personas as circumstances in name only, for example clinic owner who wants growth. That is still a profile, and it will not change which format or angle you choose.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau briefs should open with the moment that triggers the search, such as a practice manager reconciling takings at 9pm, rather than aesthetic clinic owner, 35 to 50. The circumstance decides whether the answer is an article, a template or a calculator.

**Apply anywhere:** Open every brief with the circumstance triggering the search, written as a specific moment, and keep personas for paid targeting. Reject any circumstance generic enough to describe every reader in your industry.

### 11. Judge healthcare writers on nuance and terminology, not word count  `117.18`
*useful · content insights · source 117*

Grow and Convert name the hiring problem plainly: it is hard to find writers who can write clearly and accurately about technical healthcare topics, describing concepts correctly and using the right terminology in the right contexts. They pair it with the stakes, which are higher than in other industries because inaccurate information damages credibility, creates compliance issues and can carry legal consequences depending on how the content touches HIPAA, FDA regulation or patient information. On top of that, the writer must be able to craft narratives that express the company's specific positioning, not just the topic. They say plainly that hiring writers who can do all this is one of their biggest challenges after ten years. So the hiring test should be a technical accuracy and positioning test, not a portfolio review.

> "extremely precise yet nuanced writing is essential in healthcare, but hard to come by"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert call this one of their biggest ongoing challenges across ten years of running a content agency.

**Pitfall:** Hiring on portfolio and speed produces writers who use the right words in the wrong contexts, which reads fluent to an editor and wrong to a clinician.

**Apply at Pabau:** Test Pabau writer candidates with a paid trial that includes an interview transcript with a clinician and requires them to write the mechanism accurately, then have a practitioner mark the terminology.

**Apply anywhere:** Hire healthcare writers with a trial that gives them an expert interview transcript and asks them to explain the mechanism. Have a clinician mark the terminology before you hire.

### 12. Keep only the writing rules that help you connect with the reader  `124.16`
*useful · best practices · source 124*

Grow and Convert close by warning readers to be wary of content writing tips, including their own. Their test for keeping a tip is whether it helps you connect with the target audience, and if a tip is not helping, set it aside. The prerequisite they name is being able to articulate two things: who your audience is, and why they are reading your content. They call that the biggest challenge in writing great content, and it is the thing the interview process exists to answer. This matters as a check on the rest of the article, because the six editing tips are mechanical and easy to cargo-cult. Applied without the audience answer, bulleting a section or hunting verbs just reformats content that was never aimed at anyone.

> "make sure the tips you follow help you connect with your audience"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state this as their closing position after presenting seven tips drawn from eight years of client content work.

**How to do it**

1. Before applying any writing rule, write one sentence naming who the audience is and one on why they are reading.
2. If you cannot write both sentences, stop and go back to the client or expert interview.
3. Take each rule in your style guide and state which of those two sentences it serves.
4. Drop any rule you cannot connect to either, including ones you inherited from a previous client.
5. Keep the mechanical rules such as readability scores as end-of-draft checks, not as writing guidance.
6. Review the kept rules quarterly against what actually converted.
7. When a writer pushes back on a rule, ask them to make the same argument from the audience sentence.
8. Record the two audience sentences at the top of every brief so every later decision can be tested against them.

**Pitfall:** Collecting tips as a checklist and applying them to content with no defined reader. Every rule passes and the piece still fails, because formatting cannot substitute for knowing who it is for.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's guides carry a lot of mechanical rules, so David should keep the two audience sentences at the top of every brief and test any rule conflict against them. The aesthetic-practice reader and their reason for searching decide which rule wins.

**Apply anywhere:** Write one sentence on who your reader is and one on why they are reading, then keep only the writing rules that serve those two sentences. Mechanical checks belong at the end of drafting, not in place of knowing your audience.

### 13. Keep publisher-qualified searches in mind as a reason Google keeps product demand  `89.13`
*useful · content insights · source 89*

Grow and Convert list the reasons searchers may stick with Google for product research, and one is operationally useful. On Google you can deliberately search for a recommendation from a specific source, their examples being 'best tax software pcmag' and 'best blender wirecutter'. You can also choose to ignore a result you distrust, such as a G2 list, by simply not clicking it. With a model answer you get no sources, no way to skip a publisher you distrust, and no explanation when an obscure product appears. That means being named inside a trusted publisher's roundup keeps paying on Google in a way it cannot inside an AI answer, and publisher-qualified queries are a real keyword pattern worth checking for in your category.

> "best tax software pcmag"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite 'best tax software pcmag' and 'best blender wirecutter' as searches Google supports and model answers do not, alongside the inability to verify sources or skip a distrusted list in a chat answer.

**Tools:** G2

**Pitfall:** Assuming source-qualified demand does not exist in your category because you have never targeted it. Check the actual volume before dismissing it.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau lands a mention in a respected aesthetics or healthcare publication, check whether 'best clinic software' plus that publication's name has search volume and consider a page targeting it.

**Apply anywhere:** When you land a mention in a respected trade publication, check whether your category term plus that publication's name has search volume and consider a page targeting it.

### 14. Keep the AI's example when it beats the one you wrote  `71.19`
*useful · best practices · source 71*

Mid-edit, Devesh hit a generated passage that gave a worked example: a category keyword like this one leads with a detailed product walkthrough because the searcher is shopping for a tool, contrasted with a jobs-to-be-done keyword. His reaction was that it might be better than his own version, and he kept it and moved it up in the section. Elsewhere he had the same reaction to a line he assumed he had written himself, then realized the model had produced it from his source material. The discipline is that the editing pass is not a competition with the model. Where the generated line carries a concrete example and yours carries an assertion, the example wins, and the only work left is placing it correctly.

> "This might be better than mine"

**Evidence:** Devesh kept a generated example contrasting a category keyword with a jobs-to-be-done keyword after judging it better than his own version, and moved it up in the section.

**How to do it**

1. When editing, compare your rewrite against the generated line on whether it contains a concrete worked example.
2. Keep the generated version whenever it has the example and yours only asserts the point.
3. Move the kept example to the position in the section where the argument first needs proof, usually earlier than the model placed it.
4. Delete your competing assertion rather than keeping both, to avoid the argue-then-argue-again pattern.
5. Verify any facts or figures inside the generated example before keeping it.
6. Note which of your source articles the example came from, since it usually came from your own prior work.

**Pitfall:** Editing as a contest with the model means rewriting good passages purely because you did not write them, which costs time and often loses a concrete example in exchange for an assertion.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's editorial pass should preserve generated worked examples that survive fact-checking, moving them earlier in the section rather than replacing them with an editor's summary sentence.

**Apply anywhere:** When the generated line carries a concrete example and your rewrite only asserts the point, keep the generated line, fact-check it, and move it to where the argument needs proof.

### 15. Keep the argument aimed at the buyer, not at a second audience  `72.14`
*useful · best practices · source 72*

Mid-paragraph Khanal catches himself drifting. He is writing for a marketer choosing a writing tool, and his sentence has slipped into describing what an end reader experiences when they read AI copy. He stops and says he is moving to the reader's experience when he should be speaking to the marketer, and that if he continues the writing will get muddied. He rewrites the sentence to keep the marketer as the subject: what gives you the nagging sense it is not good is that without heavy human input, the tool does not make the arguments you or your brand would make. He then tests the rewrite against the audience directly, saying most marketers frustrated with AI writing would recognize that sentence.

> "I'm moving to the reader's experience"

**Evidence:** Khanal caught the drift live and rewrote the sentence to keep the marketer as subject, then judged the new version as one most frustrated marketers would recognize.

**How to do it**

1. Name the single persona the page is written for before you draft the body.
2. Read each paragraph and identify who the sentence's 'you' refers to.
3. Flag any sentence where the 'you' has silently become a different person, such as the buyer's own customer.
4. Rewrite the sentence so the named persona stays the subject throughout.
5. Test the rewrite by asking whether that persona would say 'yes, I know exactly what you mean'.
6. Do this check per paragraph rather than at the end, since drift compounds across a section.

**Pitfall:** Audience drift inside a single paragraph. The writing still reads fluently, which is why it survives editing, but the argument stops landing for the person the page is meant to convert.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles frequently address the practice owner but drift into describing the patient's experience. Check per paragraph that the 'you' is still the clinic owner or practitioner the page targets.

**Apply anywhere:** Check paragraph by paragraph that the 'you' in your copy is still the persona the page targets, and rewrite any sentence where it has drifted to a second audience.

### 16. Let readers start and finish research inside the post, then trial  `135.9`
*useful · best practices · source 135*

Grow and Convert deliberately wrote their how-to posts so a reader could learn the solution, see how to use it, and click a trial link without needing training or extra resources. On 'how to edit video fast' they introduced the fast method and its brief steps before the first header, then used the rest of the post to walk through the actual mechanics, showing readers how to highlight and strike through transcript text. They provided enough detail that there was no confusion about the process or how the product works. In theory the reader could read the post, start a free trial, upload a recording and get going immediately, which itself appeals to a searcher whose stated want is speed. Grow and Convert describe this as Googlers starting and ending their research on the blog post.

> "Googlers can start and end their research on our blog post"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's 'how to edit video fast' post converted at 1.47%, against a 1.27% average for posts in positions 1 to 10.

**How to do it**

1. Put the summary of the method and its steps above the first H2 so the answer is available immediately.
2. Write the body as the full mechanics of the task, with the actual interface actions named.
3. Include what the reader needs to prepare beforehand, such as a file format or a recording.
4. Do not link out to a knowledge base or a demo request for the core steps; keep them inside the post.
5. Show the output at each stage so the reader can check they are on track.
6. Place the trial link at the point in the post where the reader would naturally start doing the task.
7. Match the promise in the title to the effort in the post, for example five steps meaning five steps.
8. Review whether a reader with zero prior experience could complete the task from the page alone, and add the missing step if not.

### 20. Make comparisons decision support: disclosed criteria, evidence, and who each option wins for  `66.18`
*useful · concrete actions · source 66*

Content that helps users choose between two or more viable options for a defined scenario. Its role is 'Help me decide', by applying transparent criteria, evidence and trade-offs, then explaining who each option is best for. It covers X-vs-Y comparisons, alternatives pages, shortlists and best-of guides, and the quality bar is disclosed criteria, supporting evidence, limitations, and scenarios in which different options win. > "helps users to choose between two or more viable options for a defined scenario"

**How to do it**

1. Define the scenario the comparison serves — the audience, the constraint, the budget, the use case — before listing options.
2. Include only genuinely viable options for that scenario, and say why anything obvious was excluded.
3. Publish the criteria and their weighting up front, before the verdict, so the reasoning can be checked.

5. State the trade-offs explicitly: what each option gives up to win where it wins.

7. Disclose your own position and any commercial relationship in plain language near the top.
8. State the limitations: what was not tested, what the criteria do not cover, when the comparison was last checked.

### 23. Name related entities the AI already knows inside your description  `73.8`
*useful · best practices · source 73*

Barnard adds a rule on top of the semantic triple: the more related entities you name, the better you will perform. In the Miami electrician example, Miami is a place entity, and it relates to the business because that is where it operates and who it serves. Households is a concept entity, the same class of thing as economics - abstract but nameable, and therefore an entity. His point is that you are anchoring your unknown entity to ones the model already understands, so it can say it associates this word with that idea and place you in a map it already holds. This is why vague descriptions underperform: 'serving customers nationwide' names nothing the machine can hook onto, while naming the city, the industry, the profession and the customer type gives it four anchors. He pairs it with the demand for zero ambiguity in the sentence when read plainly.

> "the more you name related entities, the better you will perform"

**Evidence:** Barnard identifies Miami as a place entity and households as a concept entity in the same worked example, both chosen because the model already understands them.

**How to do it**

1. List the entities genuinely connected to the business: cities and regions, the industry, the professions served, the customer type, notable partners and platforms.
2. Work the strongest of them into the executive summary, keeping the sentences under 25 words.
3. Use the recognized public name for each entity, not an internal shorthand.
4. Include concept entities, such as the customer category or the discipline, alongside places and organizations.
5. Drop generic filler such as 'nationwide' or 'businesses of all sizes', which name no entity.
6. Run the finished text through an entity-recognition tool and confirm each intended entity is detected and typed correctly.
7. Rewrite any entity the tool misses or mistypes until it is recognized.

**Tools:** Kalicube Pro, Google Cloud Natural Language API

**Pitfall:** Stuffing entity names in for their own sake. Barnard's condition is that the sentence stays completely unambiguous when read, so a list of place names that breaks the sentence's meaning defeats the purpose.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's descriptions should name concrete entities: aesthetic clinics, med spas, dermatology, the UK and US markets, and the named integrations. Replace phrases like 'businesses of all sizes' with the actual practice types.

**Apply anywhere:** Anchor your description to entities the model already knows: the city, the industry, the profession you serve, the customer type. Drop vague phrases that name nothing, and confirm each entity is detected by a recognition tool.
