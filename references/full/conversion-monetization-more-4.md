### 1. Put the premium price in bold on the sales page  `169.9`
*useful · best practices · source 169*

Grow and Convert displays its $10,000 price point in bold on the work with us page, which Hyam notes is very rare for agencies at that level. He treats the resulting lead volume as the proof that the positioning works: a little over one new lead a week on average, with a 14.3% close rate, which he is happy with given a boutique service that takes few clients at once. The published price is doing qualification work. Prospects who cannot get that budget approved never book, so the leads that arrive are pre-filtered on willingness to pay, and the close rate stays high on low volume. The move only works when the page also carries the value comparison, in his case the $100k-500k cost of building the same operation in-house, so the number reads as a bargain rather than a shock.

> "price point in bold on our work with us page"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert shows $10,000/month in bold on its work-with-us page and reports roughly one lead a week at a 14.3% close rate.

**How to do it**

1. Publish the actual price, or the honest starting price, on the page where buyers evaluate you.
2. Set it in bold near the offer rather than buried at the bottom.
3. Place the alternative-cost comparison directly around it so the number lands in context.
4. State plainly what is excluded, so the price is not later revised upward in the call.
5. Track lead volume and close rate for a quarter before and after publishing the price.
6. Judge the change on close rate and revenue, not on lead count, since volume should fall.
7. Keep the price current when you raise it, the way Grow and Convert moved from $6k to $10k.
8. If close rate does not improve, the problem is the value comparison, not the disclosure.

**Pitfall:** Publishing a premium price with no value framing next to it. Without the build-it-yourself cost comparison the number reads as expensive and lead volume drops with no gain in close rate.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau already publishes pricing, so the job is the framing around it: put the cost of the tools and admin hours a practice replaces next to the plan price on the pricing page and on comparison templates.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish your premium price in bold on the sales page and put the cost of the alternative right beside it. Expect fewer leads and a higher close rate, and judge the change on revenue rather than lead volume.

### 2. Rank lead magnets: interactive tools first, then gated PDFs, then video  `13.4`
*useful · concrete actions · source 13*

Across his audits, Irwin ranks the highest-performing gated content formats in order: interactive tools or calculators the visitor can try immediately with their own numbers, downloadable PDFs (e.g. a top-five list) where the page shows two or three items free and gates the rest behind an email address, and video or infographic content, now cheaper to produce with AI, used to add extra information and then prompt a call or email for a few extra points not covered. He calls the PDF pattern the movie trailer and the movie itself — show enough free value to prove relevance, then require the email address to buy the ticket for the full asset.

> "the movie trailer and the movie itself"

**How to do it**

1. Build one interactive tool relevant to the article topic first (e.g. an ROI or savings calculator, a pricing estimator) that visitors can use without giving any details, since this is the top-ranked format.
2. On the same page, follow the tool with a soft CTA offering a specialist call or deeper resource tied to the tool's output.
3. If a tool is not feasible for the topic, create a short PDF asset instead: show the top two or three points free in the body text, then gate the remaining points (e.g. top five or top ten) behind an email-capture form.
4. Add one short video (one to two minutes, per the separate dwell-time insight) or an AI-generated infographic summarizing the article, ending with a CTA offering extra tips via a contact form.
5. Sequence the formats on the page as trailer-then-ticket: free preview visible immediately, full asset gated directly beneath it rather than at the page bottom (inferred placement).

### 3. Read high bounce with strong CTR as a landing page fault  `156.15`
*useful · content insights · source 156*

Grow and Convert give a diagnostic that separates ad problems from page problems. If the keyword intent is right, the ad text matches the query, and click-through rates are excellent, but bounce rates are high, then the landing page is where you should look. The mechanism is that click-through rate measures the promise and bounce rate measures whether the page kept it. They add the structural constraint that makes this common: an ad can only be associated with a single landing page, and that page should exactly match the query. So an ad group serving several intents cannot have a page that matches them all, and the mismatch shows up as bounce rather than as a weak ad. Their fix is to narrow the ad group until one page can match it.

> "then this is where you should look"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use this exact sequence, intent then ad text then click-through rate then bounce rate, to locate where a mismanaged account is leaking.

**How to do it**

1. For each ad group, record click-through rate and landing page bounce rate side by side.
2. Split the groups into four buckets: low CTR, high bounce, both, or neither.
3. For low CTR with acceptable bounce, rewrite the ad text to name the query.
4. For strong CTR with high bounce, rewrite or replace the landing page, not the ad.
5. Check whether the ad group serves more than one intent, since one page cannot match several.
6. Split any mixed-intent group and give each new group its own page.
7. Recheck both metrics after a full conversion cycle before making further changes.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Treating high bounce as proof of a bad keyword. When CTR is strong the keyword and ad are working, and the waste is happening after the click.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should compare CTR against bounce rate for each paid ad group, and build dedicated landing pages where CTR is strong but bounce is high, rather than rewriting the ads.

**Apply anywhere:** When click-through rate is strong but bounce rate is high, fix the landing page rather than the ad, and split any ad group serving more than one intent so a single page can match it.

### 4. Reinvest in already-ranking FAQ pages with more sections and on-page CTAs  `51.6`
*useful · concrete actions · source 51*

Edward's follow-up move once PAA pages start ranking, which he says happens easily since these are low-competition queries, is to reinvest specifically in the pages that are already ranking: add additional relevant sections to those pages, which both expands the page's ability to rank for more adjacent keywords and increases the organic traffic it pulls in. On the same already-ranking pages, he also adds direct monetization/lead-capture elements — calls to action, lead magnets, and pop-ups that are deliberately not-super-intrusive — to convert some of that organic traffic into the marketing funnel directly, in addition to funneling authority onward via internal links.

> "These relevant sections allow the pages to rank for even more keywords"

**How to do it**

1. After a batch of PAA/FAQ pages has been live for a few weeks, check Google Search Console/analytics to see which specific pages are already ranking and pulling organic traffic.
2. For each already-ranking page, identify what additional relevant sub-topics or sections could be added based on related questions or adjacent queries the page is already getting impressions for (inferred: check AlsoAsked or GSC query data).
3. Add those new sections directly onto the existing ranking page rather than creating a brand-new separate page, to expand its keyword footprint.
4. Add a clear call-to-action, lead magnet offer, or a non-intrusive pop-up to the same ranking page to start converting some of its organic traffic into your marketing funnel.
5. Monitor whether the expanded page picks up additional keyword rankings and whether the new CTA/lead-magnet elements are converting, adjusting the offer or pop-up timing if conversion is low (inferred verification step).

**Tools:** Google Search Console

### 5. Reject the nurture argument for low-intent paid keywords  `158.12`
*useful · content insights · source 158*

Grow and Convert name and dismiss the standard defence of informational keywords in paid search. Some agencies argue you can bid on a term like 'magento ecommerce platforms' to nurture the reader until they are ready to buy. Their position is that conversion rates from nurture to closed deal are extremely low, so if there are people out there ready to buy your service now, those prospects should be targeted first. The reasoning is budget order, not a claim that nurture never works. Paid search charges per click regardless of intent, so every dollar spent on an educational query is a dollar not spent on someone in-market. They apply the same logic they use in organic, where they prioritize bottom-of-funnel keywords, and they treat paid as the channel where the cost of getting this wrong is immediate.

> "conversion rates from nurturing to actual closed deals are extremely low"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client wasted spend on 'magento ecommerce platforms', an educational SERP, on this reasoning.

**How to do it**

1. Sort every candidate keyword into buying intent or informational intent using the SERP test.
2. Fund the buying-intent list to its full available impression share before funding anything else.
3. Only consider informational keywords once the high-intent list is capped out on budget.
4. When a stakeholder proposes a nurture keyword, ask for the historical nurture-to-close rate before approving it.
5. Serve informational demand with organic content and email instead, where the cost is not per click.
6. Review quarterly and reallocate any informational spend back to high-intent terms that are impression-limited.

**Pitfall:** Nurture keywords are usually cheap and high volume, so they make the account look busy and productive while the pipeline does not move.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not buy clicks on treatment or clinic-management explainer queries. Those belong to the blog, and the ad budget belongs on software-buying queries until impression share on them is maxed.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not buy informational keywords on the argument that you will nurture the reader. Fund your buying-intent keywords to full impression share first, and serve educational demand with organic content instead.

### 6. Report leads per post against homepage leads, not traffic  `117.14`
*useful · best practices · source 117*

Grow and Convert measure a healthcare post by attributable leads and then give that number a denominator. The Cognitive FX pain-point post produced 32 leads in its first year, where a lead is a consultation request form fill for a program that costs $13,000 for a week of in-person treatment. They then convert that into the comparison that lands: it equals 4% of the leads the homepage produced in the same period, from one blog post. They add that many companies' entire blogs do not produce that share. The technique is worth copying independent of healthcare. Raw lead counts from a blog post are easy to dismiss; the same number expressed as a percentage of the highest-converting page on the site makes the case for the program.

> "that's equal to 4% of the leads they got from their homepage"

**Evidence:** 32 leads in the first year from one post, equal to 4% of homepage leads, on a service priced at $13,000 per week of treatment.

**How to do it**

1. Define the lead event precisely, at the level of a specific form fill, and note the deal value behind it.
2. Set up conversion tracking per landing page in Google Analytics so leads attribute to the entry article.
3. Pull leads by post for a fixed window, one year works well for a slow-converting service.
4. Pull leads from the homepage for the identical window as the benchmark.
5. Report each post's leads as a percentage of homepage leads, alongside the absolute number.
6. Rank posts by that percentage to decide which topics to produce more of.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Reporting page views instead of leads hides that a low-traffic pain-point post outperforms a high-traffic head-term post, and it steers the next quarter's topics wrong.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should report demo requests per blog article as a share of homepage demo requests. That framing shows which article types justify more production budget.

**Apply anywhere:** Report leads per article as a percentage of the leads your homepage produces over the same window. The absolute number is easy to dismiss; the ratio makes the case.

### 7. Reprice by the delivery hours you add, not by the content  `183.7`
*useful · concrete actions · source 183*

When Grow and Convert moved from the phone course to in-person workshops, they changed three things at once and repriced against all three. Clients got eight hours in person instead of four hours on the phone, the scope grew by two new modules covering how to measure content marketing ROI and how to scale writing teams, and the work became customized to that company. That combination let them move from $750 to a $1500 to $3000 range. The new modules were not invented; they came from what phone-course exit interviews said companies struggled with. The sequence is worth copying: interview, add the scope buyers named, increase contact hours, then price against the total.

> "The companies got more time with us (8 hrs in person vs. 4 on the phone)"

**Evidence:** Moving from 4 phone hours to 8 in-person hours plus two new modules supported a jump from $750 to $1500-$3000.

**How to do it**

1. List the pain points buyers named in exit interviews that your current offer does not cover.
2. Build one module or deliverable for each recurring pain point rather than guessing at new scope.
3. Count the contact hours in the current offer and in the proposed one.
4. Add customization to the deliverable so the buyer receives something specific to them, not a recording.
5. Set the new price as a range rather than a single number when scope varies by client (they used $1500 to $3000).
6. Sell the price on hours plus customization plus the added modules, in that order.
7. Re-run exit interviews after the first three deliveries to confirm the new scope is what buyers value.
8. Check the new format is still deliverable at volume before promoting it widely.

**Pitfall:** Adding scope without adding contact hours. Buyers in this case were paying for access to the founders, so more material at the same access level would not have supported the higher price.

**Apply at Pabau:** For any paid Pabau onboarding or consulting tier, David should price against contact hours with a named specialist and the customization delivered, not against the length of the documentation. Add tiers only for pain points customers have actually named in calls.

**Apply anywhere:** When you upgrade an offer, price it against contact hours and customization, not against how much material you added. Source the new scope from what past buyers said they struggled with, and quote a range when scope varies.

### 8. Require name, phone, email on lead forms, except for SaaS  `13.6`
*useful · best practices · source 13*

Irwin's Fantastic Four form-field rule requires name, phone, email, and message, with the first three treated as mandatory because each is a fallback if another is entered incorrectly — a mistyped email is caught by having a phone number, a wrong-area-code phone number is caught by having an email. The explicit exception: a SaaS or tech company should not ask for a phone number, since SaaS buyers do not expect to give one, which should guide Pabau's own lead forms toward name, email, and message rather than forcing a phone field. He also warns that address fields must be genuinely clickable and tappable on mobile to open maps, since a purely visual, non-functional field is a common, overlooked failure.

> "name, phone, email, message. The top three are mandatory"

**How to do it**

1. Audit every lead-capture form on the site and list which of name, phone, email, message fields are present and which are marked required.
2. For service, trade, or health lead-gen forms, make name, phone, and email all required fields and treat message as optional.
3. For SaaS or tech product forms, such as Pabau's, drop the phone-number field entirely and rely on name, email, and message or use-case.
4. On mobile, tap every phone number and address shown on the page to confirm tel: and maps links actually open the dialer or maps app rather than just displaying as text (inferred: test on at least one iOS and one Android device).
5. Submit each form as a test and confirm the submission is received end-to-end rather than only checking field validation.

**Pitfall:** Collecting only two of the three (e.g. name and email but no phone) leaves no fallback when one field is entered wrong — a mistyped email with no phone number means the lead is unreachable and silently lost.

### 9. Roll the offer page out across top posts one at a time  `148.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 148*

Once the first message-matched page showed results, Shour repeated steps one and two for the other top performing posts on the blog. The sequence per post is fixed: confirm the post is pulling consistent organic traffic, build the content upgrade, add the three CTAs, then build the offer page around that topic. He does not build them in bulk. The second page, on employee recognition, converted at 7.59% against 10.52% for the first, and he is candid that the traffic and conversion data are still a bit low to be conclusive. Devesh Khanal's note is that you cannot expect every offer to convert the same, and the weaker result is a signal to strengthen the bridge rather than to abandon the page.

> "we repeated the process in Step 1 and 2 for the other top performing posts"

**Evidence:** Second rollout page converted at 7.59% versus 10.52% for the first, on data Shour describes as still a bit low to be conclusive.

**How to do it**

1. Rank blog posts by organic sessions and take the top ten as the rollout list.
2. Apply the 50 visits per day gate and drop anything below it.
3. Work one post at a time through upgrade, CTAs, then offer page.
4. Let each page collect enough conversions to be readable before starting the next.
5. Log conversion rate per page in one sheet alongside the topic-to-product bridge used.
6. When a page lands well below the leader, rewrite the bridge sentence and its examples first.
7. Only after the bridge rewrite fails should you test layout or offer changes.
8. Stop the rollout at the point where remaining posts no longer clear the traffic gate.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Reading a fresh page's rate as final. Shour says his second page's data is still too thin to be conclusive, so acting on 7.59% too early risks scrapping a page that just needs volume.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should sequence this across Pabau's highest-traffic template pages one at a time rather than commissioning a batch, and hold each for enough downloads before judging it.

**Apply anywhere:** Roll the offer page out post by post down your traffic ranking, giving each enough volume to read before starting the next. Fix a weak page's bridge before touching its layout.

### 10. Run CTA A/B tests only after the page ranks for a high-intent term  `113.13`
*useful · best practices · source 113*

Grow and Convert sequence conversion testing behind ranking. Their instruction is to test the basics if you want incremental gains, but only once you are ranking for the high-intent keywords from Steps 1 and 2. The tests they name are CTA placement, in-content against sidebar, CTA copy specificity, Start free trial against Try free, and accessibility improvements. They are explicit about the ceiling: these might improve conversion, but they cannot fix poor keyword targeting or weak product selling. The practical consequence is that a CRO program run on a blog full of low-intent posts is measuring noise, because the traffic in the test has no buying intent to convert in the first place.

> "Test the basics if you want incremental gains"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state directly that these tests cannot fix poor keyword targeting or weak product selling.

**How to do it**

1. Gate the CRO backlog behind a ranking check: no test runs on a page that is not ranking for a category, comparison or jobs-to-be-done keyword.
2. Confirm the page's product section is complete before testing, so the test is not measuring a missing argument.
3. Test one variable at a time: placement first, in-content against sidebar.
4. Test CTA copy specificity second, for example Start free trial against Try free.
5. Treat accessibility and readability changes as maintenance, not as conversion experiments.
6. Record the lift as incremental and cap the time budget accordingly, since the ceiling on these tests is small.
7. Send any page that fails the ranking gate back to keyword selection instead of into the test queue.

**Pitfall:** Running a CRO program across a blog of low-intent posts. Test results come back flat or noisy, and the team concludes conversion is unfixable when the input traffic never had buying intent.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should not queue CTA experiments on Pabau posts that have not cleared the intent check. Testing belongs on the comparison, alternatives and template pages that already rank and already sell Pabau.

**Apply anywhere:** Gate CTA testing behind a ranking check. Only pages already ranking for high-intent keywords and already selling the product go into the test queue; everything else goes back to keyword selection.

### 11. Run a $20 daily budget and size audience exhaustion from reach  `155.15`
*useful · concrete actions · source 155*

Erika's campaign ran at $20 a day against an estimated reach of 730,000 people, which Facebook classified as fairly broad. Over roughly two months, from January 17 to March 13, that produced $492.01 of spend, 8,165 charged clicks and 1,892 subscribers. Grow and Convert use the reach figures to make the point that the campaign is not a one-off: the ads had been shown to only 14% of the 700,000 target audience, at an average of 2.3 times each. That combination, low penetration and low frequency, is what makes the funnel a steady acquisition channel rather than a burst. It also gives a way to forecast how long a campaign can run before creative refresh becomes necessary.

> "Her daily budget is $20 per day."

**Evidence:** $20 a day, $492.01 over roughly two months, 14% of a 700,000 audience reached at 2.3 average frequency.

**How to do it**

1. Start a new interest-targeted campaign at $20 a day rather than a large test budget.
2. Record the estimated potential reach Facebook gives at setup.
3. After the first month, divide people reached by total audience to get penetration.
4. Check average frequency alongside it; 2.3 after two months means plenty of runway.
5. Project remaining runway by dividing remaining audience by the current monthly reach.
6. Hold the budget flat while cost per subscriber stays inside target.
7. Raise budget only after revenue from the sequence covers the current spend.
8. Queue a second creative before penetration passes about half the audience.

**Tools:** Facebook Ads

**Pitfall:** Scaling budget early inflates frequency fast on a narrow audience, so the fatigue curve arrives before the sequence has proven it can pay the bill.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau tests paid social to a gated template, start at $20 a day per audience and report penetration and frequency next to cost per lead so the runway is visible before budget rises.

**Apply anywhere:** Start paid social at $20 a day, then track what share of the target audience you have reached and at what frequency, so you can forecast how long the campaign can run.

### 12. Run a paid search audit in five passes on any inherited account  `158.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 158*

The case study is effectively an audit checklist. Grow and Convert took over an account that looked fine on CTR, impressions and cost per lead, and found five separate problems: keywords chosen for volume and CPC rather than buying intent, broad match diluting good keywords into unrelated searches, ad groups holding too many mixed-intent keywords with generic copy and mismatched landing pages, Google Search Partners on and consuming a share of spend Google controlled, and no search-term-level optimization of what was already converting. They fixed all five and reported roughly double the MQLs at a quarter of the cost per MQL over six months. Running the same five passes in that order on any inherited account is the transferable procedure, because each pass depends on the one before it.

> "we dig into the main changes we implemented and why"

**Evidence:** Six months after the rebuild: ~1.4 MQLs/month at $3.2k each, versus 0.67/month at ~$14k before.

**How to do it**

1. Pass one: SERP-test every active keyword and pause the informational ones.
2. Pass two: convert all broad match to phrase or exact and negative out the wrong search terms it pulled.
3. Pass three: split ad groups down to about six same-intent keywords, rewrite ad copy to carry the keyword, and repoint each to a matching landing page.
4. Pass four: turn off Google Search Partners and baseline the MQL rate before and after.
5. Pass five: work the Search Terms report monthly, promoting converters to exact-match keywords and negativing the wasteful terms.
6. Rebuild reporting around MQLs and cost per MQL before starting, so the passes can be judged.
7. Allow six months before declaring the rebuild a success, since qualified-lead data accumulates slowly.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Running the passes out of order wastes work. Restructuring ad groups before fixing match types means you rebuild groups around search terms you are about to negative out.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau brings paid search in-house or changes agency, run these five passes in order on the inherited account before increasing budget, and set the baseline on qualified practice demos.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit any inherited paid search account in five ordered passes: SERP-test keywords, kill broad match, split ad groups and align copy and pages, disable Search Partners, then optimize monthly from the Search Terms report.

### 13. Run emotional customer stories as paid social creative  `137.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 137*

Grow and Convert say the Cognitive FX patient stories perform well on social platforms, both paid and organic, and that the emotional appeal produces good CPCs, comments and sharing. They recommend brands with a heavy social presence produce these specifically for content promotion. The stories also bring first-click conversions even though that is not their main purpose; the figure-skater story brought leads from both paid and organic Facebook. The wider argument is that a story does three jobs a utilitarian SEO post cannot: it builds emotional connection so a buyer who arrived through a functional post remembers you at decision time, it gives paid social something with a low cost per click, and it accumulates into a brand story about changing lives.

> "we see really good CPCs, comments, and people sharing"

**Evidence:** The Cognitive FX figure-skater story brought leads from both paid and organic Facebook, and Grow and Convert report good CPCs, comments and sharing across the story posts.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the customer stories with the sharpest before-and-after, ideally with a timeframe such as years of symptoms.
2. Publish the full story as a blog post with the customer's own words and specific life details.
3. Cut a short social version with the single most striking detail as the hook and link to the full story.
4. Run it as paid social creative and compare CPC against your standard product ads.
5. Read the comments; people sharing their own similar stories is the signal it is working.
6. Track first-click conversions from these campaigns separately from organic performance.
7. Build a rolling library so the brand story accumulates rather than resting on one story.

**Tools:** Facebook

**Pitfall:** Promoting a story that has no real transformation in it. Without a genuine before-and-after the emotional hook is absent, CPCs return to normal, and the comments stay empty.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should film or write clinic-owner transformation stories, publish them on /blog/, and run the strongest detail as paid social creative rather than only promoting feature announcements.

**Apply anywhere:** Turn your strongest customer transformations into full stories, cut the sharpest detail into paid social creative, and compare the CPC against your standard product ads.

### 14. Run paid ads to your articles while they wait to rank  `153.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 153*

Grow and Convert address the gap created by their own data: if a post takes over a year to reach page one, the program produces no leads for months. Their first bridge is paid ads pointed at the articles themselves rather than at a generic landing page. They flag the common failure as buying hollow traffic that never converts, and frame their difference as targeting for return on ad spend rather than volume. The tactic only works because the articles are bottom-of-funnel and written to convert, which is their standing content position. It turns the waiting period into a live test of whether the piece converts at all, before any organic traffic arrives.

> "Paid ads can be a great way to drive traffic to your articles while you're waiting"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use paid ads to their client articles specifically as a bridge during the 12-plus months before organic rankings arrive.

**How to do it**

1. Pick the three or four bottom-of-funnel articles closest to a purchase decision.
2. Confirm each has a clear conversion action on the page before spending anything.
3. Build tightly-matched ad groups around the article's own target keyword rather than broad interest targeting.
4. Set a small daily budget and judge on conversions, not clicks or sessions.
5. Kill any ad set producing traffic with no conversion events within its first spend cycle.
6. Use the conversion rate you observe to decide which articles deserve link building later.
7. Wind the spend down on any article as its organic ranking takes hold.
8. Keep the ad copy and the article promise identical so the click is not wasted.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Buying broad top-of-funnel traffic to a blog post. Grow and Convert call this hollow traffic: the sessions look good in the report and never convert.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau could run small paid campaigns to new bottom-of-funnel articles, such as practice-management comparison and template pages, and use the conversion data to decide which pages later get link investment. Judge those campaigns on demo requests, not sessions.

**Apply anywhere:** Point small, tightly-targeted paid campaigns at your new bottom-of-funnel articles during the year they spend climbing. Judge on conversions only, and use the results to decide which pages later earn link investment.

### 15. Segment ad groups by business stage and by industry vertical  `157.12`
*useful · concrete actions · source 157*

Grow and Convert give a concrete structure. Inside a campaign called 'Accounting Software - US - Search', one ad group for 'Small Business' carries 'accounting software for small business' and 'small business accounting app'. A 'Freelancer' group carries 'freelancer accounting software' and 'freelancer accounting app'. A 'Real Estate' group carries 'real estate accounting software' and 'realtor accounting software'. The segmentation axes are business stage and industry vertical. They add two more axes for separate campaigns: competitor brand terms, with ad groups again split by business stage, and product use cases such as invoicing and billing. The test for any grouping is that all keywords in a group share the same intent, so one piece of targeted ad copy is enticing for every searcher in it.

> "we show potential ad groups based on business stage"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's worked structure: 'Accounting Software - US - Search' with Small Business, Freelancer and Real Estate ad groups.

**How to do it**

1. Name campaigns by product, country and network, following the pattern 'Category - US - Search'.
2. List the business stages your buyers fall into and make one ad group per stage.
3. List the industry verticals you sell into and make one ad group per vertical.
4. Put two to three keyword variants in each group, all naming that stage or vertical.
5. Create a separate campaign for competitor brand terms, with its own stage-based ad groups.
6. Create a further campaign for use-case terms such as invoicing or billing.
7. For each group, confirm one ad text would appeal to every keyword in it; if not, split again.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Mixing a vertical group and a stage group in one campaign budget. The stage groups usually have higher volume and starve the vertical groups of spend.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's equivalent axes are practice type and size: medical spa, dermatology clinic, single-room practice, multi-site chain. Each deserves its own ad group and, ideally, its own landing page.

**Apply anywhere:** Segment ad groups by business stage and by industry vertical, with two or three keyword variants each. Run competitor terms and use-case terms as separate campaigns so they do not share a budget.

### 16. Set price from perceived value, then plan the step-ups in advance  `169.14`
*useful · general insights · source 169*

Hyam repeats the rule twice because he thinks people forget it: price is a function of the perceived value of your service. When he first listed everything the offering would require he refused outright, telling Khanal it was too much work and expectations would be unrealistic. Khanal's reframe was the useful one, that at some price it is worth it, so what is that price. Hyam blurted $10,000 a month. They judged that high but not unreasonable, since agencies in the space charge it, then discounted for two specific facts: not many companies can get that budget approved, and they had zero client history so were essentially unproven. They settled at $6,000, a rate companies already pay agencies, with an explicit ladder to $7,000, then $8,000 and beyond. They are at $10,000 now, having added link building and Facebook ads.

> "Ok, but at some price, it's worth it"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert priced at $6,000/month at launch against an aspirational $10,000, planned $7k and $8k steps, and now charges $10,000 after adding link building and Facebook ads.

**Pitfall:** Anchoring the launch price on what you can currently prove and then never raising it. Grow and Convert only reached $10k because the step-up ladder was decided at launch and tied to added scope.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau prices a new add-on, set the value-based ceiling first, discount only for unproven track record, and write the price ladder and the scope additions that justify each step before launch.

**Apply anywhere:** Name the price the service would be worth if it delivered everything promised, then discount only for the fact you are unproven. Write the step-up ladder and the scope each step adds before you launch, and actually climb it.

### 17. Split B2B survey responses into top 20% by contract value and the rest  `161.9`
*useful · concrete actions · source 161*

For B2B, Khanal modifies the segmentation. Instead of usage frequency, split by contract value: run the survey among your highest contract value or best customers, the top 20%, and separately among the other 80%. Then compare how the answers differ. The value is in the delta. If your best accounts name a different primary benefit, a different alternative or a different ideal user than everyone else, that difference is your ideal customer profile stated in the customers' own words. It also tells you which messaging to lead with if you want more accounts that look like the top 20% rather than more accounts generally.

> "the top 20% and then the other 80% of your customers"

**Evidence:** Khanal recommends the 20/80 contract-value split specifically for B2B companies as a modification of the consumer SaaS usage split.

**How to do it**

1. Rank every customer by annual contract value and cut at the 80th percentile.
2. Send the same survey to both groups with a hidden field marking which one they are in.
3. Build a side-by-side table of the top answers per question for each group.
4. Highlight every question where the two groups disagree; those are the load-bearing differences.
5. Rewrite the primary messaging around what the top 20% say, not the blended average.
6. Check whether the top 20% found you through a different channel in question one, and shift budget accordingly.
7. Use the top 20%'s named alternatives to decide which competitor comparison pages get built first.
8. Re-run annually, since the profile of your best accounts moves as the product matures.

**Tools:** Survey Monkey

**Pitfall:** Averaging across all accounts produces messaging tuned to your smallest, least profitable customers, because there are more of them and they reply more often.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should compare answers from large multi-location groups against single-room practices. If the two name different benefits, the enterprise and small-practice landing pages need different headlines rather than one shared one.

**Apply anywhere:** For B2B, survey your top 20% by contract value separately from the other 80% and build messaging from where the two groups disagree.

### 18. Split SEO keyword and emotional benefit across H1 and 'eyebrow'  `13.16`
*useful · concrete actions · source 13*

For pages that need to satisfy both Google's exact-match keyword matching and the human visitor's emotional, benefit-led hook, Irwin's technique is to not force both into one line: the true H1, kept keyword-focused, is styled as small eyebrow text sitting above a larger, visually dominant heading that carries the feeling, question, or benefit line instead, such as his own site's large visual heading reading 'Your chances of getting more leads' with the keyword-bearing H1 as small text above it. Edward names this pattern the eyebrow, small text sitting above a big heading, and notes it has become common on AI-generated, specifically Claude-generated, websites recently, making it a recognizable, current design convention worth adopting deliberately.

> "little text over the H1, it's called an eyebrow"

**How to do it**

1. Identify the primary target keyword that must appear in the true, crawlable H1 tag for SEO purposes.
2. Write a short, roughly one-line, keyword-bearing H1 and style it visually small, placed directly above the main visual heading; this is the eyebrow.
3. Write a second, larger heading, which can be an H2 or styled text since the H1 tag is already used by the eyebrow, expressing the benefit, feeling, or question the visitor cares about, without needing the exact keyword.
4. Confirm in the page's HTML source that the keyword-bearing line is the actual h1 element, not just visually large text, so search engines parse it correctly (inferred: view page source or use a browser SEO extension to confirm heading tag order).
5. Style the eyebrow smaller and lighter and the benefit line larger and bolder so the visual hierarchy matches how a visitor should read it, benefit first, keyword as supporting context.
6. Visually review the combined eyebrow plus benefit-heading layout against a single-line H1 to confirm it does not look cluttered above the fold (inferred).

### 19. Sponsors pay creators because the CPM undercuts the platform's own ads  `68.18`
*useful · general insights · source 68*

Cody explains why brands wire creators large sums for a single video. It is a media buy and the whole thing is arbitrage. A brand knows TikTok's average CPM is around $6, so it knows what a thousand views costs on-platform. If a creator can deliver a million views for $30,000, that is cheaper than buying the same reach through the platform, so the deal makes sense before any brand-affinity argument. This also explains why podcasts are hard to sponsor: there is no established cost per thousand impressions for a podcast, whereas email newsletter CPM and cost per click are well understood. He gives the going rates he has seen: Replit paying about $2,500 for a single video from a channel doing 10,000 views with 20,000 subscribers, and paying bounties through Passionfroot for videos that involve building something with the product. He says the print-on-demand affiliate category on YouTube supports $400k to $500k a year from tutorials plus an info product.

> "they'll pay you 2500 like for a single video"

**Evidence:** TikTok CPM around $6; a $30k deal for a million views is cheaper than the platform equivalent; Replit paying about $2,500 per video to a channel with 10,000 views and 20,000 subscribers; print-on-demand YouTube affiliates earning $400k-$500k a year.

**How to do it**

1. Work out the platform CPM your sponsor would otherwise pay, such as roughly $6 on TikTok.
2. Estimate your realistic view count per placement from your last ten pieces.
3. Price your slot so the implied CPM sits below the platform's, and show that maths in the pitch.
4. Offer an affiliate or tracked link so the brand can compute CAC and payback period.
5. Report the payback period and lifetime value back to the sponsor after the campaign to earn the renewal.
6. Register with a marketplace like Passionfroot to reach brands running bounty campaigns.
7. Where no CPM convention exists, such as podcasts, bundle the slot with measurable inventory instead of selling it alone.

**Tools:** Passionfroot, YouTube, TikTok

**Pitfall:** Pricing a placement on follower count rather than implied CPM against the platform's own ad rate makes the deal fail the buyer's only real test.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau buys influencer or newsletter placements, price them against the equivalent platform CPM rather than the creator's asking rate, and always insist on a tracked link so payback period is computable.

**Apply anywhere:** Price creator placements as a media buy: work out what the same reach costs on the platform, come in under it, and hand the sponsor a tracked link so they can compute CAC and payback.

### 20. Start any paid engagement with a structural audit, not a bid change  `156.14`
*useful · concrete actions · source 156*

Grow and Convert start every paid search engagement with an audit that analyzes the account against the factors they set out: keyword buying intent, match types, keywords per ad group, ad text and keyword pinning, landing page match, Search Partners, Dynamic Search Ads, automated assets and Smart campaigns. The audit produces an honest assessment of the account's issues and opportunities, and also decides whether they are a good fit to manage it. The sequencing matters. Every item on that list is structural, and each one changes which queries you buy. Changing bids before the structure is fixed just adjusts the price you pay for the wrong clicks. Their stated position is a careful, manual, non-automated, no-shortcuts approach.

> "We start every engagement off with an audit"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert run this audit at the start of every paid search engagement and use it to decide whether they take the client at all.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the search terms report and classify queries by buying intent.
2. List every keyword by match type and flag all broad match.
3. Count keywords per ad group and flag any group above six or holding mixed intents.
4. Read each ad group's ad text and check the keyword is present and pinned to headline one.
5. Click each ad group's landing page and check it matches the group's intent.
6. Check campaign settings for Search Partners and the Display Network.
7. List any Dynamic Search Ads or Smart campaigns and the share of spend they take.
8. Open account settings and list every automatically created asset.
9. Write up the findings in that order, since each one changes which queries you buy.
10. Only after the structural fixes are live should you touch bids or bidding strategy.

**Tools:** Google Ads

**Pitfall:** Auditing bids and budgets first. It produces visible changes fast and leaves the structural waste untouched, so cost per qualified lead does not move.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before increasing Pabau's paid budget, run this nine-point structural audit on the existing account, since spending more on a mis-structured account multiplies the waste.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit a paid account structurally first, in the order intent, match type, ad group size, ad text, landing page, network settings, automated campaign types and automated assets, and only touch bids once those are fixed.

### 21. Stop expecting email nurture to create buying intent that is not there  `107.17`
*useful · content insights · source 107*

Grow and Convert name this as the second flaw in traffic-first thinking, and it is worth separating from the first. The assumption is that capturing an email from a top-of-funnel reader with an ebook or whitepaper, then sending a drip sequence, will eventually convert them. Their position is that you cannot manufacture or force buying intent with email marketing. If a prospect read a top-of-funnel article and is not in the market for the product, no amount of nurture email makes them decide to buy. The consequence for planning is that the gated-asset and nurture layer should not be used to justify top-of-funnel topics on the calendar. It also means list size on its own is not evidence a content program is working, since a list built from non-buyers has no latent pipeline in it.

> "You can't manufacture or force buying-intent with email marketing"

**How to do it**

1. Segment your email list by which content the subscriber first converted on, top-funnel or bottom-funnel.
2. Compare the eventual close rate of the two segments over a full sales cycle.
3. If the top-funnel segment closes at a negligible rate, stop using list growth to justify top-funnel topics.
4. Keep gated assets only where they sit on genuinely high-intent pages.
5. Judge the content program on pipeline from bottom-funnel pages, not on subscribers acquired.
6. Reallocate the nurture effort toward buyers who visited comparison, pricing or alternatives pages.

**Pitfall:** A large list from ebook downloads looks like a content asset in reporting and produces almost no closed revenue, which surfaces only when someone segments close rate by first-touch content.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's template downloads do attract practice staff, so David should check close rate by which page the download came from. Templates on evaluation-intent topics are worth the gate; general industry ebooks probably are not.

**Apply anywhere:** Segment your list by the content that first converted each subscriber and compare close rates. If top-funnel signups rarely close, stop treating email nurture as the justification for top-funnel content.
