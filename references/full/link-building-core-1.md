### 1. Add links only when the post sits at position 3-5 with a stable SERP  `116.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 116*

Grow and Convert build links for clients as part of the retainer, and they explicitly do not distribute them evenly across articles. Early in an engagement links go to newly published pieces; later they shift toward the pieces already proven to convert. After the audit they used three conditions to decide whether extra links, rather than a rewrite, were the right fix for a slipping post. The post must not already have many backlinks, it must be ranking in position 3 to 5 for its target keyword, and the SERP for that keyword must not have changed much since publication. All three matter together. A post at position 3-5 on an unchanged SERP is losing on authority, so links close the gap. If the SERP itself has changed, links will not fix a mismatch of intent.

> "We were more likely to choose this strategy if"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert allocate a fixed monthly number of links per client and shift them from new posts to proven converters as an engagement matures.

**How to do it**

1. For each slipping post, check its referring domains in Ahrefs and note whether it is under-linked relative to the pages above it.
2. Check its current position for the target keyword and keep only posts sitting at 3 to 5.
3. Pull the SERP for that keyword and compare it against the SERP at the time of publication using historical SERP data.
4. Confirm the ranking pages still serve the same intent and format as when you published.
5. If all three hold, assign that post the next month's link allocation instead of a new article.
6. Skip link building for anything at position 6 or lower; send those to the re-optimization track.
7. Re-check positions monthly and stop building once the post re-enters the top three.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Pointing links at a post that slipped because the SERP changed. The authority is not the constraint, so the links buy nothing and the page keeps falling.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's outreach and digital PR budget should go to articles sitting in positions 3-5 on stable SERPs, such as established comparison and category pages, rather than being spread across every new blog post.

**Apply anywhere:** Point your monthly link budget at articles sitting in positions 3-5 on stable SERPs rather than spreading links evenly across new posts.

### 2. Analyze public data you do not own to earn links without outreach  `125.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 125*

Grow and Convert point out that in many industries people are starving for data to cite, so they link to whoever published it. The strongest evidence in the article comes from Devesh's previous company, Growth Rock. Looking at his best pages by links in Ahrefs, three of the top eleven are data-based pieces. The first was a research post with an infographic comparing social media engagement to email engagement, and it earned 272 dofollow links, none of which were built manually. The important detail is that the data was not his. He researched and analyzed public data available online. Grow and Convert call this the most accessible form of thought leadership content, because you do not need your own unique ideas or your own dataset. You need one smart person to do the research and the analysis.

> "It has 272 Dofollow links, none of which he built manually"

**Evidence:** Growth Rock's research post comparing social media to email engagement earned 272 dofollow links with no manual link building, and three of its top eleven pages by links were data-based pieces.

**How to do it**

1. Pick a comparison your industry keeps asserting without numbers, such as one channel versus another.
2. Find public datasets, industry reports and platform benchmarks that bear on it.
3. Pull the figures into one sheet with a source column for every number.
4. Do the analysis that nobody has done, usually a direct comparison across sources that were never compared.
5. Build one chart or infographic that carries the headline finding on its own.
6. Write the post around the finding, not around the methodology.
7. Cite every source inline so other writers can verify before citing you.
8. Check earned links in Ahrefs after 60 and 180 days rather than doing outreach.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Combining sources with different definitions or timeframes produces a headline number that a knowledgeable reader can dismantle. The signal is that you cannot state the denominator for each figure.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build one analysis-of-public-data post a quarter for the aesthetics market, for example comparing published treatment pricing or no-show rates across public industry reports. These earn links that Pabau's feature pages cannot.

**Apply anywhere:** Research and analyze public data you do not own. Compare sources nobody has compared, publish one chart that carries the finding, and cite everything inline. This earns links without outreach and needs no proprietary dataset.

### 3. Ask integration and supplier partners for a natural feature link  `39.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 39*

Ask your own suppliers, subcontractors, partners, and trade associations directly for a feature or mention, since these links are genuinely natural in Google's eyes precisely because the underlying relationship is real, though less than half of partners approached will say yes and most businesses only have a short list of such partners, making this a limited but very high-quality link source. The direct SaaS example given is a payroll software company asking its accounting-firm partners to add the software to their recommended payroll tools page; a parallel example has a commercial HVAC company asking its building-management software vendor to feature it in a customer success story built around a concrete, specific outcome. Notably, this is a one-way link, since the client is not obligated to link back to the vendor in return.

> "These links are genuinely natural. Google treats them that way because they"

**How to do it**

1. List every supplier, integration partner, subcontractor, and trade association your business actually works with — for Pabau, this could include payment processors, insurance or billing integrations, hardware vendors, or industry associations for clinics and med spas.
2. For each, identify whether they publish a recommended-tools page, partner directory, integrations marketplace, or customer-story section on their site.
3. Reach out asking specifically for a feature or mention on that existing page or section, rather than asking them to create something new from scratch.
4. Offer a concrete, specific success story or use case as the basis for the feature, such as a real customer outcome or a specific number or scale achieved, rather than a generic product mention.
5. Do not expect to reciprocate with a link back automatically; treat this as a one-way ask, since these placements do not require a link back to the partner.
6. Expect fewer than half of the partners approached to say yes, and prioritize the partners most likely to already have relevant existing pages or sections. (inferred)

**Pitfall:** Expecting a high yes-rate — fewer than half of partners approached are expected to agree, and most businesses only have a short list of eligible partners, so this method should be treated as limited-but-high-quality rather than scalable.

### 4. Automate drop catching across multiple registrars or do not attempt it  `67.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 67*

Dirk walks through what actually happens when a domain drops. The owner fails to renew, the domain enters a grace phase, then it is removed from the registrar and released to public registration. From that point the contest is decided in milliseconds. His first attempt was refreshing a page on his laptop, which he says teaches you fast that this is not the game. The working setup is automated systems connected to several registrars at once, constantly pinging for the terms you are watching, firing on the order of a thousand registration attempts in a single second the moment a domain releases. Using one supplier is usually not enough. Even a successful back-order only registers your interest, not the domain: contested names then go to a private auction.

> "this is a game in terms of milliseconds"

**Evidence:** Dirk says a previous employer ran eight full-time people bidding on domains 24/7 to sustain this operation.

**How to do it**

1. Build a watch list of the exact domains or keyword patterns you want, rather than reacting to auction lists.
2. Connect API access to several registrars at once, because Dirk says a single supplier is usually not enough to win a contested drop.
3. Run constant pings against the expired-domain feeds so you know the exact moment a name leaves the registrar and enters public registration.
4. Automate the registration burst so the system fires roughly a thousand register attempts within one second of release.
5. Place back orders through more than one drop-catch service in parallel for the same name.
6. Treat a won back order as an entry ticket only, since contested names then move to a private auction.
7. Set a written maximum bid per domain before the auction opens and hand it to whoever is bidding.
8. Accept that manual refreshing loses every time, because normal browser latency is already a second too slow.

**Pitfall:** Assuming the back order is the win. Dirk says you have only registered interest at that point, and the real cost arrives in the private auction that follows.

**Apply at Pabau:** This is intelligence rather than a Pabau tactic. If Pabau ever wants a specific dropping domain, use a paid back-order service through more than one provider rather than trying to catch it in-house, and set the walk-away price first.

**Apply anywhere:** If you want a dropping domain, place back orders through more than one drop-catch service rather than trying to catch it manually, and set a written walk-away price before the auction opens.

### 5. Budget links as 20% of SEO effort and content as the other 80%  `115.5`
*core · best practices · source 115*

Grow and Convert give an explicit split: 80% of what drives results from SEO content is picking the right keywords, structuring articles around the SERP, incorporating subject-matter expertise and selling the product. The remaining 20% is link building. They run links through partner agencies and describe the effect as improving rankings on a keyword-by-keyword basis, after the writing process is done, and as a supporting element rather than the main driver. The claim they are arguing against is the common attempt to shortcut writing quality by spending heavily on links. Internal links to other relevant articles on the client's site sit in the same supporting bucket. The number is useful as a budget check: if links are taking half your SEO spend, the ratio is inverted relative to what they say produces results.

> "The other 20% is made up by link building"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated split from agency work: 80% keyword selection, structure, expertise and product selling; 20% link building, run through partner agencies.

**How to do it**

1. Total your monthly SEO spend across writing, keyword research, SERP analysis and links.
2. Check that link spend is around a fifth of the total, and cut it back if it is more.
3. Move the recovered budget into SERP analysis time and subject-matter interviews for each article.
4. Sequence links after publication, never before: build to an article only once it is live and matching intent.
5. Point links at individual articles keyword by keyword rather than at the homepage.
6. Record each article's position before and after its link push so the 20% contribution stays visible.
7. Add internal links from other relevant articles on the same site as part of the same supporting 20%.
8. If a page with links still stalls, spend the next increment on rewriting rather than on more links.

**Pitfall:** Inverting the ratio to buy speed. The tell is a link invoice larger than the writing invoice, alongside articles that were never SERP-analyzed and still bundle several keywords.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should hold Pabau's link spend at roughly a fifth of the content budget and put the rest into SERP analysis and interviews with clinic-side staff. Links go to published bottom-funnel pages one keyword at a time, not to pabau.com's homepage.

**Apply anywhere:** Hold link building to roughly a fifth of your SEO budget and put the other four fifths into keyword selection, SERP-driven structure, subject-matter expertise and product-relevant writing. Build links to individual published articles after they already match intent, keyword by keyword.

### 6. Build new-site authority via directories and AI-assisted gap analysis  `40.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 40*

Kasra's playbook for building authority for a brand-new website combines manual citation-building with AI-assisted competitor gap analysis. Start with generic business directories, both Yellow Pages-style and industry-specific, since searching '[industry] directory websites' typically surfaces 25-plus options, and check each paid directory's own rankings before paying, since a non-ranking paid directory is 'just a directory trap.' Layer in geo-specific directories (city, then state or country) and service-specific directories, such as a payroll-only directory for an accounting firm, to multiply your citation opportunities to roughly 8-9 distinct searches per business type. Then run a competitor backlink-gap analysis: pull competitor backlinks via Ahrefs and Semrush, diff them against your own existing backlinks to produce a target list (Kasra's example generated about 150 target domains), a process he says is normally very manual but can be partly automated using the Claude Chrome extension to drive the tools directly.

> "some of it you can automate with the Claude Chrome extension"

**How to do it**

1. Search '[your industry] directory websites' and '[your industry] citation sites' to compile an initial list of at least 25 relevant directories.
2. For every paid directory on that list, check whether it currently ranks in Google for anything before paying - skip any paid directory with no visible rankings.
3. Add geo-specific directories for your city, then broaden to state or region and country-level business directories, multiplying your directory list to roughly 8-9 distinct searches.
4. Add service-specific directories that list only your specific sub-services as an additional citation layer.
5. Compile a list of your top 4-5 direct competitors.
6. Using Ahrefs and Semrush, pull each competitor's full backlink list and compare it against your own site's current backlink list to isolate links they have that you don't.
7. Where this manual cross-referencing is too slow to do by hand, use the Claude Chrome extension to drive Ahrefs and Semrush in-browser and compile the gap-analysis list automatically.
8. Build out foundational social profiles on YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn linked back to your main site, since these count as additional foundational links and are harder to fake now due to anti-spam fingerprinting.
9. For each top competitor, search their exact brand name in Google and record which third-party sites rank underneath their brand name in the results.
10. Reach out to those same third-party sites, often free placements, to try to get your own brand listed in the same slots, repeating this brand-SERP-mining process across all competitors and every relevant geo.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Claude (Chrome extension)

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Here's my competitors, go into Ahrefs, go into Semrush, look at their backlinks, look at the ones I don't have versus the ones they have
```

**Pitfall:** Paying for a directory listing without first checking whether that directory itself ranks for anything is a common trap - a non-ranking paid directory provides no SEO value regardless of its price or apparent authority.

### 7. Build one dead-simple interactive tool, not a generic calculator  `46.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 46*

The best-performing linkable assets are absurdly simple, single-action interactive experiences, not generic utility calculators. Before Calm had brand recognition, its founders launched the standalone site donothingfor2minutes.com — stare at a sunset for two minutes, and moving your mouse resets the countdown — which Edward says pulled in roughly 384,000 backlinks purely by going viral, with a single CTA ('Want to get better at meditation? Try Calm') before eventually being 301-redirected into the main Calm product. Canva's color-palette generator (drag in a photo, get four hex codes back) reportedly earned about 7,100 backlinks the same way. Travis argues 'vibe coding' tools have raised the bar so much that a basic mortgage calculator 'is no longer going to cut it' anymore — the opportunity now is adding an unexpected creative or gamified layer to stand out.

> "drag an image in, get the four dominant colors"

**How to do it**

1. Pick one narrow, delightful interactive action tightly connected to the emotional benefit of your product category, not the product itself.
2. Strip the concept to a single input and single output with zero sign-up and zero navigation, e.g. drag a photo in, get 4 hex codes out.
3. Prototype and ship it fast using an AI 'vibe coding' tool on its own short, memorable domain or subdomain so it feels like a standalone discovery.
4. Add exactly one soft CTA back to your core product rather than cluttering the experience with marketing.
5. Seed the tool organically to a handful of relevant communities or journalists rather than mass outreach, and let social sharing do the rest.
6. Monitor referring domains and backlinks weekly using a backlink-tracking tool to see if it is catching on (inferred).
7. Once the standalone asset has accumulated meaningful authority and your core brand is established, 301-redirect it into your main domain to consolidate the link equity.
8. For any new asset, deliberately add a creative or gamified twist beyond the base utility, since plain calculators no longer stand out post-vibe-coding.

**Pitfall:** A basic, generic utility such as a plain mortgage calculator no longer earns links now that vibe-coding tools have made simple calculators commonplace — brands that aren't taking the creative risk of combining utility with genuine novelty are losing PR value.

### 8. Build site authority with PR first under DR 30, page links after  `94.7`
*core · best practices · source 94*

Grow and Convert give an explicit threshold for how link building fits the underdog sequence. If your domain rating is a decent 30 or above, the strategy works with only a few links per article, and in many cases their articles have ranked with no intentional links built at all. If your domain rating is below roughly 30, they start an engagement by building authority to the site through PR link building, and only then follow up with individual links to each article they publish. The wider point is that the common belief that you need tens or hundreds of links to rank an article is not what they see, because the strategy targets specific terms with a page built for that term. Links speed the pages up, they are not the entry ticket.

> "if you have a decent domain rating (30+)"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say only a few links per piece get these pages onto page one at DR 30+, and that many of their articles have ranked with no intentional links at all.

**How to do it**

1. Check your domain rating in Ahrefs before planning any link spend.
2. Under roughly DR 30, run a site-level PR link building push first, aimed at earning links to the domain rather than to individual posts.
3. At DR 30 or above, publish the keyword-tailored pages first and watch them for four to eight weeks before spending on links at all.
4. For pages that stall outside the top three, build a small number of links to that specific URL rather than to the homepage.
5. Point links at the underperforming article, not at the category page, since the article is what the keyword is assigned to.
6. Reject any proposal that assumes tens or hundreds of links per article; that budget belongs to head terms you are not chasing yet.
7. Recheck DR quarterly and switch from site-level PR to page-level links once you cross the threshold.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Low-DR sites that skip the site-level push and buy a handful of links per post get neither, because the pages still lack the domain authority to compete. The signal is many pages stuck on page two at once.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should check its DR before commissioning link work. If it sits comfortably above 30, publish the qualified pages first and only buy links for the ones that stall outside the top three.

**Apply anywhere:** Check your domain rating first. Below about 30, run site-level PR link building before anything else; above it, publish the pages and only build a few links to the specific URLs that stall.

### 9. Canva runs link outreach as a quota-carrying sales function  `62.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 62*

The detail Cody flags in his intro as the thing he found most fascinating: Canva has link building specialists who function almost as salespeople would, carrying quotas of links they have to build on a monthly basis, exactly as a sales individual carries a number. The episode's own account confirms the practice: they have a whole team dedicated to outreach, constantly looking for opportunities to get content featured on relevant websites, and they are not relying on organic mentions - they strategically reach out to publications and websites to secure links. The narrator's own evidence is a personalised outreach email received from Canva while researching this very episode, asking whether they'd include a link to Canva's design resources in an article being written. The instructive part is the organisational design: a company at a $40 billion valuation still treats link acquisition as a staffed, target-carrying function rather than a byproduct of good content.

> "a whole team dedicated to outreach"

**How to do it**

1. Treat link acquisition as a role with a target, not as a task that happens after content is published.
2. Set a monthly quota per specialist, in the way a sales team carries a number.
3. Build a prospecting motion: monitor who is writing about your category and reach out while they're writing.
4. Personalise the ask around the specific article, offering a genuinely relevant resource.
5. Have real resource pages worth linking to, so the ask is defensible.
6. Track links secured per specialist per month so the function can be managed like a pipeline.

**Pitfall:** A quota without genuinely linkable resources turns into low-quality placements - the outreach works because there is something worth linking to at the end of it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should own at least one genuinely linkable resource set (templates, industry data, compliance guides) and treat outreach to people currently writing about those topics as a standing, measured activity rather than an occasional campaign.

**Apply anywhere:** Own at least one genuinely linkable resource set - templates, industry data, guides - and treat outreach to people currently writing about those topics as a standing, measured activity rather than an occasional campaign.

### 10. Chase referral-traffic links and brand mentions, not 'vanity SEO'  `42.4`
*core · best practices · source 42*

The recommended standard for link building past the initial foundational stage, such as social profiles and directories, is to prioritize links that actually pass referral traffic, real visitors clicking through with intent to take action on your site, plus brand mentions that drive people to search your brand name or reviews directly. This is explicitly contrasted with "vanity SEO": acquiring a backlink that doesn't functionally matter, or ranking for a low-volume, purely informational keyword that your actual ideal customer profile never even searches. The stated reasoning is that Google favors sites that behave like a real business generating real interest, referral clicks and branded search volume, over sites that look like "meaningless SEO machines" optimizing for metrics that don't reflect real audience interest.

> "Vanity SEO could be literally getting a backlink that doesn't matter"

**How to do it**

1. Before pursuing any specific link opportunity, ask whether it would plausibly send a real visitor to your site who might click through and take action, not just pass generic authority.
2. Separately track brand-name and brand-plus-review search volume, for example in Google Search Console or a rank tracker, as its own success metric alongside backlink counts.
3. When evaluating a keyword to target, check whether your actual ideal customer profile would realistically search it, and deprioritize low-volume informational keywords they wouldn't.
4. Deprioritize or decline link opportunities that would only exist to manipulate a metric, such as a backlink placement with no topical fit, no referral potential, and no brand-building value.
5. Report on link building using referral traffic and branded-search lift, not just raw link count or a domain authority score, so the internal team is optimizing for the right outcome (inferred reporting practice to operationalize the distinction).

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Vanity SEO is explicitly defined as getting a backlink that doesn't matter, or ranking for a low-search-volume, purely informational keyword your ICP doesn't even see — chasing these metrics looks like SEO activity without building anything Google or a real audience actually rewards.

### 11. Check expired-domain backlinks in at least two tools before buying  `67.11`
*core · concrete actions · source 67*

Dirk's biggest operational failure was a checking failure, not a bidding failure. His team wrote an SOP for drop catching that turned out to be too generic, so buyers followed it and skipped real checks. On close to a thousand domains bought that way, a secondary review found the majority were poor and unusable. Those domains could not go into the PBN system, so the money and time were written off entirely and they were left to die. He names the specific gap: do not run background checks from one supplier like Ahrefs alone, because backlinks visible in one tool are missing in another. He adds the compounding risk, that if you keep bad domains and use them to push links at guest posts or directly at money sites, you end up damaging the money site itself.

> "don't just check from a one supplier like Ahrefs"

**Evidence:** Close to a thousand domains bought under a too-generic SOP; the majority failed a later review and were abandoned with no recoverable use.

**How to do it**

1. Write the SOP as a specific checklist with named tools and thresholds, not a general description of what to look at.
2. Pull the backlink profile from at least two independent providers, since Dirk found links present in one tool and absent in another.
3. Cross-check ownership history and the Wayback timeline for every domain, not a sample of the batch.
4. Have a second reviewer sign off each domain before purchase, rather than running the secondary review after the money is spent.
5. Set an explicit reject rule in the SOP, such as any niche change into a YMYL vertical or any evidence of past bulk 301 abuse.
6. Where a domain fails, drop it entirely rather than repurposing it as a guest-post source, since bad links pointed at a money site hurt the money site.
7. Track the reject rate per buyer as a quality control on whether the SOP is being followed.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** A generic SOP produces the illusion of process. Dirk's team bought close to a thousand domains under one and the majority failed secondary review, becoming a total write-off because bad domains cannot be salvaged or safely reused.

**Apply at Pabau:** Any process Pabau documents for repeatable SEO work should name the tool, the filter and the reject threshold rather than the step. A vague SOP scales the mistake, not the work.

**Apply anywhere:** Write SEO SOPs with the named tool, the exact filter and the reject threshold in each step. A generic checklist scales the mistake rather than the work, and vetting backlinks in only one tool misses links the other one shows.

### 12. Commission a small coded calculator for a searched calculator term  `100.9`
*core · concrete actions · source 100*

Grow and Convert call small coding projects a vastly underutilized content strategy. For their client Pilot they learned through user research that customers were preoccupied with their burn rate. 'Burn rate calculator' was a popular search term on that topic, so they designed and coded a burn rate calculator with graphs and definitions built in. It ranked #2 within several weeks of publishing. Their argument for the format is threefold: these are hard to reproduce, they are valuable provided the user research was done first, and people link to them. They also argue the cost objection is wrong, saying you can find HTML and CSS developers on sites like Upwork around the world who will do simple projects without breaking the bank. The caveat they attach is explicit: do the user research first, or you will code something useless.

> "small coding projects like these are a vastly underutilized content strategy"

**Evidence:** The Pilot burn rate calculator was published several weeks before the article and already ranked #2 for the author at time of publishing.

**How to do it**

1. Run user research or customer interviews to find the number your buyers keep trying to work out.
2. Check that a calculator keyword exists for it and look at what ranks today.
3. Scope the tool to one calculation with clear inputs, not a multi-tab suite.
4. Include graphs of the output and definitions of each input on the same page, since those carry the ranking text.
5. Hire an HTML and CSS developer on Upwork for the build rather than routing it through a full dev cycle.
6. Publish it on your own domain at a URL matching the calculator keyword.
7. Track its ranking from a few weeks after publishing, and add links to it from related articles.
8. Pitch the tool to sites that currently link to worse calculators.

**Tools:** Upwork

**Pitfall:** Building a calculator nobody was trying to calculate produces a tool with no traffic and no links. The signal is that the calculator keyword has no search demand and no customer mentioned the number in research.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's obvious candidates are calculators for clinic revenue per treatment room, no-show cost or treatment pricing, chosen from what practice owners actually ask. Build one, host it under a calculator URL, and link it from related blog and template pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Find the number your customers keep working out, confirm a calculator keyword exists, then have a contract front-end developer build a single-purpose calculator with graphs and definitions on the page.

### 13. Community links to a reference page can rank head terms  `171.11`
*core · content insights · source 171*

The links Examine earned were not built; they arrived because community members answering questions in r/Fitness pointed at the page that answered them. Grow & Convert record the outcome: Examine.com ranks on page one for terms like creatine at position four, fish oil at three and curcumin at two, and takes 40,000 to 50,000 monthly visitors from Google organic. These are one-word head terms in a commercial supplement market, which is normally a link-budget fight. The mechanism is that the site earned the citation slot in the specific place people ask the question, so the link volume grew every time the question recurred. Sol calls it symbiotic: they published on creatine, people asked about creatine, people linked.

> "gets over 40,000 – 50,000 visitors a month"

**Evidence:** Examine.com ranked #4 for 'creatine', #3 for 'fish oil' and #2 for 'curcumin' as of the article, with 40,000-50,000 monthly Google organic visitors and around 2M total monthly visitors.

**How to do it**

1. Build one genuinely definitive page per head term rather than many shallow ones.
2. Make it the page a knowledgeable member would link to end an argument, with sources attached.
3. Find the recurring threads where that exact question is asked, on Reddit, forums and community groups.
4. Answer the question in the thread, in full, with the page as the source rather than as the answer.
5. Let other members do the linking on subsequent occurrences; do not repost your own link on every thread.
6. Track referring domains to the page monthly and check whether the head term moves in Search Console.
7. Update the page whenever the underlying research changes, since the link value depends on it staying the best answer.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** This only works when the page settles the question better than anything else available. A page that merely restates what is already ranking gets read and not linked.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's best link-earning bet is one definitive, source-backed page per recurring practice-management question, then answering that question in clinic-owner communities with the page as the citation. That is a different play from the template pages, which serve search directly.

**Apply anywhere:** Build one definitive, source-backed page per recurring question in your category, then answer that question in communities with the page as your source. Links accumulate every time the question recurs, and that is what puts a page on head terms.

### 14. Design and code an interactive calculator to take a tool keyword  `128.18`
*core · concrete actions · source 128*

Among the three number-one rankings Grow and Convert cite, the mechanism behind one is explicit: they rank first for "burn rate calculator" for their client Pilot, and for that client they designed and coded an interactive calculator. The competition on that SERP was Gusto, Geckoboard, Fundera and Baremetrics, all substantially larger and better-resourced sites. The lesson is narrow and worth acting on. When the keyword itself contains the word calculator, template, checker or generator, the searcher wants the instrument, and an article describing how to compute the thing loses to a page that computes it. Building the instrument is also what makes the page linkable, since other sites cite a working tool and rarely cite an explainer, which compounds the ranking advantage over time.

> "For whom we designed and coded an interactive calculator."

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert rank number one for "burn rate calculator" for Pilot with a calculator they designed and coded, against Gusto, Geckoboard, Fundera and Baremetrics.

**How to do it**

1. Pull your keyword list and isolate every term containing calculator, template, generator, checker or estimator.
2. Check the SERP for each: if the top results are working tools, an article will not rank there.
3. Pick the one term with the clearest commercial link to your product and build the tool, not a post about it.
4. Keep the tool simple enough to use without instructions, with inputs above the fold and the result updating live.
5. Put the target keyword in the title, H1 and URL, and put the tool itself at the top of the page.
6. Add a short explainer beneath the tool covering the formula and the inputs, for coverage rather than as the main content.
7. Place one product CTA directly beneath the result, where intent is highest.
8. Track referring domains to the tool page, since working tools earn citations that explainers do not.

**Pitfall:** Publishing an article about how to calculate the thing on a keyword where the SERP is full of working tools. It can rank for a while on content quality and then loses every position to any competitor who ships the actual instrument.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should build working instruments on the calculator-style terms in aesthetics practice management, such as a no-show cost or treatment-room utilization calculator, rather than blog posts explaining the formula.

**Apply anywhere:** Isolate every keyword containing calculator, template, generator or checker, check whether the SERP is full of working tools, and build the instrument rather than an article about it. Put the tool above the fold with the result live, add the explainer beneath, and track referring domains, since tools earn citations explainers do not.

### 16. Don't trust Domain Rating or traffic at face value  `40.11`
*core · best practices · source 40*

Kasra warns against evaluating any site - a guest-post target, a competitor, a link opportunity - using Domain Rating or traffic estimates at face value, because both are directly, commercially manipulable. He cites paid gigs on Legiit and Fiverr specifically sold to inflate a site's DR up to around 65, aimed at buyers who 'don't know how to correctly score and evaluate websites' and just look at the DR number. He says traffic estimates in both Ahrefs and Semrush can likewise be manipulated to look far higher than genuine visitor numbers, so a reported 'DR65 site getting 10,000 hits a month' should trigger scrutiny of what's really going on, not automatic trust. His alternative approach is to judge sites by what they actually and relevantly rank for, and by whether their authority is on a genuine upward trajectory over time.

> "Traffic is another metric that can be manipulated"

**How to do it**

1. Before paying for or accepting a guest-post or link placement, do not evaluate the site by DR or traffic estimate alone.
2. Check what the site's top pages actually rank for in Ahrefs or Semrush and confirm those rankings are for terms genuinely relevant to your industry, not just any high-volume term.
3. Check whether the site's authority or DR trajectory is genuinely climbing over time by comparing historical DR and traffic snapshots month over month, rather than trusting a single current reading.
4. Treat unusually high DR relative to a site's apparent age, content quality, or niche relevance as a red flag consistent with paid DR-manipulation services.
5. Cross-check reported traffic against independent signals where possible, such as visible social engagement or actual SERP presence for its claimed traffic-driving keywords.
6. When evaluating your own or a competitor's backlink profile, also check toxicity scores via a tool such as Link Research Tools rather than assuming a 'clean' profile is automatically better, since expected toxicity varies heavily by industry.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Link Research Tools (LRT)

**Pitfall:** Both DR and traffic - the two most commonly cited third-party metrics - are directly, commercially manipulable, since paid DR-boosting gigs exist on Legiit and Fiverr and traffic estimates in Ahrefs and Semrush can be inflated, so a high number on either metric alone is not evidence of a genuinely valuable site.

### 17. Evaluate an expired domain by owner count and Wayback history, never DR  `67.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 67*

Asked what he checks first on a domain, Dirk says it is never DR. He calls third-party metrics the last thing he would look at. His first check is ownership history: the more times a domain has changed hands, the bigger the red flag, and hands changing every six to twelve months is disqualifying on its own. Second is age and depth of indexation, because age is the one thing you cannot buy in SEO. Third is topical continuity: a 2010 car-sales site relaunched for car services is fine, a car site with a 2021 gambling or adult front page is not. Fourth is the backlink profile, judged by kind rather than count, and whether the domain has been savagely 301-redirected into unrelated money sites. Wayback Machine is his main tool for the timeline, with the caveat that an owner can hide history from it.

> "It's never DR. DR would be the last thing"

**Evidence:** Dirk names the amount of owners as his single biggest deal breaker and says a niche jump into any YMYL vertical is a bigger no-go than the link profile.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the domain's ownership history and count how many times it has changed hands; treat changes every six to twelve months as an immediate reject.
2. Open Wayback Machine and step through the front page year by year to build a timeline of what the site actually was.
3. Reject any domain whose history shows a jump into gambling, adult, crypto or health if that is not the niche you intend to run.
4. Confirm the intended new use is topically continuous with the old use, in the way car sales to car services is continuous.
5. Check how long and how deeply the domain was indexed, since age and indexation are the parts you cannot manufacture.
6. Pull the backlink profile and judge it by type: a handful of related editorial links beats thousands of bought ones.
7. Check whether the domain has been bulk 301-redirected into an unrelated money site in the past, which signals prior abuse.
8. Skip anything with a heavily abused profile outright, because Dirk says cleaning it costs more time than the domain is worth.
9. Remember Wayback can be blocked or pruned by a previous owner, so treat a suspiciously clean archive as missing data, not as a pass.

**Tools:** Wayback Machine, Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Buying on DR alone. Dirk warns a strong-looking DR usually means a purchased link blast, and that a heavily abused profile can never be economically cleaned, so the domain is dead money.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau ever considers acquiring an expired aesthetics or clinic-software domain, the check order should be ownership count, Wayback timeline, niche continuity, then links, with DR excluded from the decision entirely.

**Apply anywhere:** When you evaluate an expired domain, check ownership count, then the Wayback timeline, then niche continuity, then link quality. Leave DR out of the decision entirely.

### 18. Expect a few positioning stories to become slow link assets  `91.15`
*core · content insights · source 91*

Grow and Convert say some disruption stories, not all, strike enough of a chord to become long-term link building assets. Their example is Pain Point SEO, which argues for going after bottom-of-funnel keywords first instead of chasing traffic and search volume and nurturing prospects down the funnel. It did not go viral. Over several years it naturally acquired 167 backlinks from 113 domains. They are careful to say this is not indicative of typical results for most disruption stories and only shows the potential. The mechanism they point to is not promotion, it is that the position resonated with lots of marketers and companies who then cited it when making the same argument. That makes a named, contrarian position on a real industry problem the closest thing to a passive link asset in their program.

> "it has naturally acquired 167 backlinks from 113 domains"

**Evidence:** Pain Point SEO accumulated 167 backlinks from 113 domains over several years without going viral, per Grow and Convert's Ahrefs data.

**How to do it**

1. Take a clear, contrarian position on an industry practice rather than summarizing the consensus.
2. Give the position a short name others can cite in one phrase.
3. Explain the reasoning in enough depth that someone arguing your side can use your piece as the citation.
4. Publish it on a URL you will never move, since links accrue over years.
5. Reference the named position consistently in every talk, podcast and post so the name spreads.
6. Do not budget outreach against it; treat any links as a byproduct of the position being useful to cite.
7. Check referring domains annually rather than monthly, since accumulation is slow.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Most disruption stories earn no links at all, so a link forecast is not a reason to commission one. Grow and Convert say the Pain Point SEO result is not typical.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should not expect links from a positioning piece as the business case. But if Pabau names a genuine contrarian position about how aesthetic practices should run, that named position is a better long-term link asset than another comparison article.

**Apply anywhere:** Take a named, contrarian position on a real industry practice and explain it well enough to be cited. A minority of such pieces accumulate links steadily for years, but do not build the business case on it.

### 19. Fix dedicated pages and intent match before spending anything on links  `108.14`
*core · best practices · source 108*

Grow and Convert are explicit that link building is a supporting element, not the mechanism that gets content ranking, and they say agencies and companies often believe the opposite. Their claim is conditional and strong: if you have not created a dedicated page per keyword and deeply matched search intent, then no amount of outreach, link building or internal linking will get the content onto the first page. Links do help. They report that building links to individual articles often gives a boost and helps push a page onto page one, and their agency builds links to a different set of published articles every month as an ongoing effort. But the sequencing is fixed. Diagnose a stalled page as a content and structure problem first, and only treat it as a link problem once the page is genuinely the best match for the query.

> "no amount of outreach, link building, or internal links will get your content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's monthly practice of building links to a rotating set of already-published articles, framed as a boost rather than the ranking cause.

**How to do it**

1. Before commissioning any link campaign, confirm the target page has one keyword, not several.
2. Confirm the page type matches the dominant page type in the SERP.
3. Confirm every subtopic shared by the top results is covered on the page.
4. If any of those three fail, rewrite or split the page and wait a month before spending on links.
5. Only once the page passes, add it to the monthly link-building queue.
6. Build links to individual articles in rotation rather than pointing everything at the homepage.
7. Track each page's position before and after its link push so the contribution is visible.
8. If a page with strong links still stalls, return to intent match rather than buying more links.

**Pitfall:** Buying links for a page that bundles three keywords. The links raise the domain a little and the page still never ranks, and the budget is spent before anyone questions the content.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should gate outreach behind a check that the target article has a single keyword and matches the SERP's dominant format. Link spend on a bundled article is wasted until the page is split.

**Apply anywhere:** Links support ranking, they do not create it. Confirm the page targets one keyword, matches the SERP's page type and covers the expected subtopics, then queue it for links. Otherwise fix the page first.

### 20. Image alt text beat exact-match anchor text in a controlled test  `14.1`
*core · content insights · source 14*

In Dan Petrovic's 2013 anchor-text-proximity experiment, four nearly identical domains were each linked from a separate page on the same well-established website, with only the link type varying: exact-match anchor text, an exact keyword phrase in a sentence followed by a naked URL, an image link using the exact keyword as alt text, and a 'click here' link with the keyword nearby. The exact-match anchor text link performed well as expected, but the image link using the target keyword as alt text outperformed it and beat every other link type, while the naked URL and 'click here' links produced no ranking effect at all. This was a controlled, real-world test isolating link type/context as the only variable, making the alt-text result unusually clean evidence rather than a correlational guess.

> "the alt text-based image link worked best"

**Evidence:** Four domains, each linked from a separate page on the same well-established site with only link type varied; result: 'the alt text-based image link worked best,' beating the exact-match anchor text link, while 'the click here link and the naked URL... did not show up at all.'

**Apply:** You should treat a linked image's alt text as functionally equivalent to — and per this test, potentially more powerful than — traditional anchor text, meaning alt-text keyword quality on any image likely to attract external links deserves the same attention as anchor text in guest posts or digital PR placements.
