### 1. Place your company on the competition-to-new-category spectrum first  `110.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 110*

Grow and Convert answer the objection 'there aren't many buying-intent keywords for my product' with a spectrum. At one end you are in a known category with a known solution, like Pipedrive selling a CRM, and there are plenty of bottom-funnel terms you should spend a long time knocking out before touching top of funnel. At the other end you are genuinely creating a new category, like Coinbase when almost nobody knew what Bitcoin was, and top-funnel education makes sense. They stress that end of the spectrum comes around once in many years. Most companies sit in the middle: a category with a few players, some differentiation, some direct alternative-style terms and a large pool of mid-funnel pain-point terms. That middle is what they call the pain point SEO sweet spot.

> "companies fall on a spectrum on this issue"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite Coinbase at launch as a genuine new-category example and note such companies come around once in many years.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the category noun a stranger would use for your product, not your internal positioning.
2. Search that noun in Ahrefs and count how many established competitors rank on page one.
3. If three or more established players exist, place yourself at the competition end and rule out top-of-funnel work entirely for now.
4. If you believe you are creating a new category, apply the replacement test in the next insight before accepting it.
5. For the middle case, list direct alternative terms first, then the mid-funnel pain points from customer research.
6. Assign a rough count to each bucket so the shortage argument becomes a number rather than a feeling.
7. Sequence the calendar: alternatives and product-intent terms, then mid-funnel pain points, then top of funnel only when both lists are exhausted.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Founders routinely place themselves at the new-category end because of a technical differentiator. Grow and Convert say lots of founders and marketers think they are there and are not, and the cost is a calendar full of education content nobody needed.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau sits at the competition end: practice management software is a mature category with several known players. That rules out category-education content as a priority and puts alternatives, competitor comparisons and feature-intent pages first on pabau.com.

**Apply anywhere:** Place your product on the spectrum from crowded known category to genuine new category before setting funnel priority. Crowded categories get alternatives and feature-intent terms first; only a genuine new category justifies education content, and those are rare.

### 2. Press an agency on how its process produces leads, not whether  `178.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 178*

Grow and Convert say every agency answers yes when asked if its content drives leads and sales, so the yes/no question is worthless. They tell buyers to name the goal first (traffic, email signups, or leads and purchases), then ask two follow-ups that are hard to bluff: how exactly does the process turn a published article into a service signup or a product purchase, and can you prove it with data. They warn that email signups are a different metric and should not be accepted as a substitute for leads. Their final filter is a gut check: if after the explanation you still cannot trace the path from article to sale, the path does not exist. They name goal misalignment as the single biggest complaint they hear from companies who had bad agency experiences, and from their own past agency experiences.

> "Use your gut instinct"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert name agency-to-client goal misalignment as the biggest complaint they hear from companies with bad agency experiences, and the biggest complaint they had themselves.

**How to do it**

1. Write down your one goal metric before any sales call: traffic, email signups, leads, demos, or purchases.
2. Ask the agency directly which of those their strategy is optimized for, and do not accept 'all of them'.
3. Ask them to walk the causal chain out loud: which keyword, which page, which on-page element turns a reader into a signup.
4. Refuse an answer that substitutes email signups for leads; say the two are different and re-ask.
5. Ask what data they hold that proves the chain, and ask to see it for a named client.
6. Score the answer on whether you could redraw the chain yourself afterwards; if not, disqualify.
7. Repeat the same three questions to every agency on the shortlist so answers are comparable.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
Is your process designed to produce content that gets leads and sales? How does it work to actually achieve that goal? Are you data-driven and can you prove that your process works?
```

**Pitfall:** Asking only the yes/no version. Every agency says yes, so the call ends with no information and the mismatch surfaces six months in, when spend is sunk.

**Apply at Pabau:** If David briefs an outside agency or freelancer for Pabau content, the brief should state the goal metric as booked demos, not sessions, and the trial should be judged on whether a page can be traced to a demo request.

**Apply anywhere:** Name your one goal metric before any agency call, then ask how the process turns a published page into that metric and what data proves it. If you cannot redraw the chain afterwards, walk away.

### 3. Put sales and marketing on one shared revenue target, not two  `177.2`
*core · best practices · source 177*

The author contrasts two employers. At the first, sales carried a revenue target and marketing carried a qualified-lead target. When sales missed, they blamed marketing for lead volume; when the lead goal was hit, the blame moved to lead quality. The blame moved back and forth and the company suffered. At the second, there was one revenue target covering both teams, and nobody hit their goal unless the company hit its overall goal. He describes that environment as extremely collaborative: marketing started caring about lead quality rather than lead count, and sales gave constant feedback on what was converting. He attributes 20%-plus year-on-year growth to that collaboration. The mechanism is simple. Separate goals make the two teams compete for credit, because a rep can hit their number while the company misses.

> "one revenue target for sales and marketing"

**Evidence:** The author reports 20%-plus year-on-year growth at the company that ran a single shared revenue target for both teams.

**How to do it**

1. Replace the marketing team's lead-volume target with the same company revenue target sales carries.
2. Make bonus payout conditional on the company number, not the team number, so nobody wins alone.
3. Add a lead-quality measure such as closed-won rate per source so marketing owns quality, not just count.
4. Require sales to submit written feedback on lead quality per source each month as their side of the deal.
5. Publish one dashboard both teams read, showing pipeline by source through to closed revenue.
6. Ban the two teams from reporting separate scoreboards in the same leadership meeting.
7. Squash blame-shifting the first time leadership sees it, rather than treating it as normal friction.

**Pitfall:** Team-level goals produce company politics, which the author defines as employees caring more about their team or their power than about the organization. The tell is a quarter where marketing hit its lead goal and sales missed revenue, and both teams claim they succeeded.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau's content function is measured on traffic or MQLs while sales is measured on closed deals, the two will argue about lead quality. David should get content measured on demos that reach a sales conversation, using the same number sales reports.

**Apply anywhere:** Give marketing and sales one shared revenue target instead of a lead target and a revenue target. Separate goals make the teams compete for credit and produce quarters where one claims success while the company misses.

### 4. Qualify a market with the books-or-academic-papers readability test  `137.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 137*

Grow and Convert built a 'readability index' to decide whether long-form written content can work for a business at all. Readability means how much people read and learn about the topic before buying. Most B2B is high because there is a lot to explain. Commoditized consumer goods like orange soda are low, which is why those brands buy TV and influencer reach instead. Their shortcut test is one question: are either books or academic papers consistently written on this topic? Fitness and nutrition, yes. Investing and finance, yes. Concussions and health, yes. Best soft drink, no. That test is what convinced them to take Cognitive FX, a B2C concussion clinic, despite a firm-wide assumption that B2C meant shallow high-volume publishing. They now say B2C and B2B content marketing are not different at all when readability matches.

> "you can use to decide on readability"

**Evidence:** Cognitive FX passed the test and grew to over 200,000 organic sessions and 200 conversions a month over four years, with agency content responsible for more than 50% of consultations.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the buying decision your customer makes and ask how much they read before making it.
2. Search for published books on the topic; a shelf of them is a pass signal.
3. Search Google Scholar for academic papers on the topic; consistent publication is the second pass signal.
4. Check whether an online community exists that discusses the topic in depth, such as a subreddit or forum with long threads.
5. If neither books, papers nor a deep community exist, mark the topic low readability and put budget into brand and paid channels instead of long-form.
6. If it passes, confirm the complexity is real by checking whether page-one results go beyond list-and-photo format.
7. Commit to depth on the passing topics rather than volume, since complexity is what correlates with high readability.

**Tools:** Google Scholar

**Pitfall:** Teams assume B2C automatically means low readability and default to shallow volume publishing. Grow and Convert made exactly that mistake and nearly turned down their fastest-growing client. The signal you have hit it is a content plan full of listicles for a topic that has textbooks written about it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Aesthetics and clinic operations pass this test: there are textbooks and peer-reviewed papers on injectables, laser treatment and practice management. David should treat that as licence to keep publishing in-depth /blog/ and /templates/ pages rather than thin listicles, and apply the same test before opening any new topic cluster.

**Apply anywhere:** Before committing to long-form content, test whether books or academic papers are consistently written about your topic. If both are absent and no in-depth community exists, buyers do not read before purchase and your budget belongs in brand and paid channels instead.

### 5. Qualify clients on four criteria that map to your pain points  `169.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 169*

Grow and Convert is wide in vertical, taking B2B agencies and a concussion treatment center alike, but narrow on characteristics. Hyam says the narrowness is entirely about criteria that show a prospect resonates with the pain points the agency solves. His working hypothesis of a best customer has four tests: a substantial existing customer base, which shows the model is proven and is the basic ticket of entry many agencies use; an existing sales or marketing channel that already works, so leads generated can actually be closed; prior experience trying content marketing, so they understand the difficulty and are asking a third party for help; and medium to high readability, meaning people in the market read about the topic or research before buying. This is a fit filter, not a demographic one, which is why the vertical can stay wide.

> "we are narrow in specific characteristics"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert runs roughly one new lead a week with a 14.3% close rate while displaying a $10,000 price in bold on its work-with-us page.

**How to do it**

1. Write your pain point list first, since the qualification criteria are derived from it.
2. Add a proof-of-business test: does the prospect already have a substantial customer base.
3. Add a downstream test: do they have a sales or marketing motion that already converts leads.
4. Add an awareness test: have they already tried this in-house and felt the difficulty.
5. Add a channel-fit test: does their audience actually read or research before buying.
6. Score every inbound lead against the four before booking a full sales call.
7. Decline prospects that fail the downstream test even when they can pay, since your results will not close.
8. Revisit the criteria quarterly and treat them as a hypothesis, which is how Hyam frames his.

**Pitfall:** Taking a paying client who has no working sales channel. The content will generate leads nobody closes, and the engagement gets judged a failure on a metric you do not control.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's sales qualification and the copy on its pricing and demo pages should filter on practice characteristics rather than vertical, for example existing patient volume and an existing booking process, and the blog should target the reading, researching clinic owner.

**Apply anywhere:** Qualify buyers on fit with the pain points you solve, not on industry. Test for a proven customer base, a sales channel that already closes, prior experience with the problem, and an audience that reads before buying.

### 6. Rank your four lead channels, then fund only the one you control  `168.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 168*

Nii Ahene of CPC Strategy describes how the agency picked its growth channel around 2010. They audited where business actually came from and found four sources: referrals, cold calls, partnerships, and content. Rather than run all four, they scored each on whether they could influence the outcome. Referrals failed because they could not design incentives clients responded to. Partnerships failed on lack of experience. Cold calling failed on will, not economics, since nobody on the team wanted to do it. Content won because it was the one channel whose output they could directly control, and early lead numbers already showed signal. The lesson is not that content always wins. It is that the selection test was controllability plus early evidence, not theoretical channel size.

> "referrals, cold calls, partnerships, and content brought them the most business"

**Evidence:** CPC Strategy grew 50% year over year through the Great Recession and reached 135 staff by 2018, with content named as the channel that spearheaded growth.

**How to do it**

1. List every source that produced a closed deal in the last 12 months, not every channel you could theoretically run.
2. Count deals per source so you rank by real contribution rather than by effort spent.
3. Score each source on whether your team can change the output through its own work, with no gatekeeper in the loop.
4. Score each source on whether anyone on the team will sustain it for a year without being forced.
5. Drop any source that fails either score, even if it currently produces deals.
6. Pick the single highest-scoring source and give it the majority of budget and headcount for at least four quarters.
7. Set an early read metric, such as leads per published asset, and review it at 90 days before increasing spend.

**Pitfall:** Teams keep all four channels alive at 25% each, which starves the one channel they could actually compound. The signal you have hit this is a channel that has run for a year with no owner and no target.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run the same audit on Pabau's inbound sources before adding another channel. If blog and template pages are the sources Pabau can influence most directly, concentrate there rather than splitting effort into partner co-marketing that depends on someone else's calendar.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit which sources actually produced closed deals, then score each on whether your own team can change the result and whether anyone will sustain it. Fund the winner heavily instead of running every channel at a quarter strength.

### 7. Read Google's starter guide, spam policies and YMYL PDF before any other SEO source  `74.1`
*core · best practices · source 74*

David Quaid argues the Google SEO Starter Guide is the most accurate and up-to-date document Google publishes about SEO, and that roughly 90% of SEO myths are already answered in it, especially in the closing 'things we believe you shouldn't focus on' section. He names two primary documents anyone confused about SEO should read: the guide listing what is actually penalizable, and Google's reference on what YMYL covers. He notes many SEOs and most web developers skip it because they assume they are past it. His challenge at the end of the walkthrough is direct: name a real-life scenario the starter guide does not cover. Treat the guide as the baseline that any paid advice has to beat, not as beginner material.

> "one of the most accurate and"

**Evidence:** David estimates 90% of SEO myths are dealt with in the starter guide, and says it is the document he posts most frequently on the web.

**How to do it**

1. Read the full Google SEO Starter Guide on Search Central end to end, including the 'Things we believe you shouldn't focus on' section at the bottom.
2. Read Google's spam policies page next and list every behaviour Google actually names as penalizable.
3. Read Google's guidance on YMYL and note how narrowly it is scoped.
4. Read the interstitial/intrusive-ads page, since that is one of the few explicit deranking behaviours named.
5. Write down every SEO belief your team holds that the guide contradicts, and drop those beliefs from briefs and tickets.
6. When someone sells you a tactic, ask which line of the starter guide supports it and treat 'the guide is out of date' as a red flag.
7. Re-read the guide once a year and diff it against your notes, since Google edits it quietly.

**Tools:** Google Search Central

**Pitfall:** Assuming the guide is entry-level and skipping it. David says web devs and SEOs both do this, then spend budget on tactics the guide explicitly says do not matter.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should keep the starter guide as the reference the SEO checklist for pabau.com is measured against. Any recommendation applied to blog or template pages that the guide names as a non-factor should be dropped from the process rather than argued about.

**Apply anywhere:** Make Google's own starter guide, spam policies and YMYL reference the baseline for your SEO process, and drop any internal rule the guide contradicts.

### 8. Refuse sales-collateral requests that displace demand generation work  `177.6`
*core · best practices · source 177*

The author names this as the single biggest frustration for marketers. Because sales carries the revenue target, marketing gets treated as a support function and asked to create sales material, make things look pretty, and write emails for reps. His argument is that if marketing at your company means producing support material for sales, there is no marketing strategy, because a team executing a real strategy would be too busy to do it. He allows one exception: it is legitimate when the marketer was hired specifically for that product-marketing or design role. He also notes the correlation runs the other way. When strategy and execution are actually happening, sales usually ends up with the leads, content and emails it needs as a byproduct.

> "create sales material for the sales team"

**Evidence:** The author's example: a marketer with deep AdWords expertise can carry an early-stage B2B company's twelve-month revenue goal on one channel, and putting that person on sales collateral wastes the resource.

**How to do it**

1. List every recurring request that lands on marketing from sales, with the hours each consumes per month.
2. Split the list into demand generation, and support material such as decks, one-pagers and rep emails.
3. Check each support item against the marketer's actual hired role; keep only what matches it.
4. Take the total support hours to leadership as a share of the team's month, not as a complaint.
5. Propose a named owner for the rest: a product marketer, a designer or a contractor.
6. Set a standing cap, for example one support day a month, and hold it.
7. Show that the strategy work already produces most of the collateral sales asks for, so the requests shrink.

**Pitfall:** Absorbing the requests quietly reads as capacity, and the asks grow. The signal you have hit the limit is a marketer with adwords or content depth spending their week in slide decks.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau's sales team routes deck and one-pager requests to David, he should quantify the hours against publishing capacity and get a separate owner assigned. Every day spent on collateral is a day not spent on pages that generate demo requests.

**Apply anywhere:** Track how many hours a month sales support requests take from marketing, split them from demand generation work, and get a separate owner for anything outside the marketer's hired role.

### 9. Refuse the unmeasurable-brand-value argument until measurable leads exist  `132.7`
*core · best practices · source 132*

Grow and Convert draw a sharp line about when the you-cannot-measure-everything argument is legitimate. They accept it themselves, because they can point to 1,300-plus attributable leads from the bottom-funnel tier and only then claim additional unmeasured value on top. The problem, they say, is that most companies only do top-funnel content, so the unmeasurability claim becomes an excuse for why the company can see hardly any leads from the entire blog. The test is order. If the blog has a proven measurable-conversion layer, unmeasured brand and long-term value is a real bonus on top of it. If it does not, the argument is being used to avoid the question. Grow and Convert are blunt that traffic, brand building and untrackable leads have value, just not as much as leads you know you generated.

> "becomes an excuse for why the company can't see any or hardly any leads"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: 'for Geekbot, we know we generated 1300+ leads with our BOTF posts' before claiming any additional unmeasured value from the top-funnel tier.

**How to do it**

1. Report attributable blog conversions by funnel stage before any strategy discussion begins.
2. Set a floor: the bottom-funnel tier must show a countable number of conversions before top-funnel spend is defended on brand grounds.
3. If that floor is not met, treat the gap as a bottom-funnel content problem and fix it before commissioning more top-funnel work.
4. Once the floor is met, allow unmeasured value as an argument for top-funnel, and say explicitly that it is an addition rather than a substitute.
5. Keep the two numbers separate in reporting so the measurable tier never gets folded into a blended blog average.
6. Revisit quarterly; if measurable conversions fall back to near zero, suspend the brand-value argument again.

**Pitfall:** Marketing teams reach for brand value and unmeasurable impact precisely when the attributable numbers are weakest. The signal is a blog reporting traffic growth and share of voice while nobody can name a single deal that started on a blog post.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should require pabau.com's blog reporting to show attributable demo requests by funnel stage. Until the bottom-funnel pages such as competitor comparisons and specialty software pages produce a countable demo number, brand-awareness reasoning should not be accepted for new top-funnel briefs.

**Apply anywhere:** Only allow the unmeasurable-brand-value argument for informational content once your buying-intent pages already show a countable number of attributable conversions. Without that floor, the argument is covering for a blog that generates almost no leads at all.

### 10. Reject SEO when the modeled value only slightly beats the cost  `129.7`
*core · best practices · source 129*

Grow and Convert set a decision rule that goes against normal ROI thinking. If the estimated value of SEO comes out only a little above the estimated cost, that is a no, not a marginal yes. Their reasoning is that the whole point of the channel is compounding: once you cross breakeven, the value should far outweigh what it took to get the rankings, because the rankings are already paid for and keep producing. So a model that lands close is almost certainly a model that will land negative once the real timeline, real rankings and real conversion rates land. This is also why they tell you not to sweat cost precision. Being off by thousands of dollars a month should not change the verdict, and if it does, the verdict was already no.

> "the value and potential customers it can generate shouldn't be slightly higher"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: if it is a close call, it is likely SEO is not worth integrating into your marketing efforts.

**How to do it**

1. Finish the value estimate and the cost estimate as independent numbers.
2. Compare them as a ratio, not a difference.
3. Treat anything under roughly a two-to-one value-to-cost ratio as a fail, since a close call will not survive real-world variance.
4. Test the verdict's sensitivity: change the monthly cost by a few thousand dollars and see if the answer flips.
5. If the answer flips on that change, record the decision as no.
6. If the answer holds comfortably, stop modelling and start with a small batch of pages.
7. Revisit the model only after real ranking and conversion data exists, not with better assumptions.

**Tools:** Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Talking yourself into a marginal case by refining assumptions until the model clears breakeven. The signal is a spreadsheet that only works at the optimistic end of every input.

**Apply at Pabau:** Use the ratio test when deciding whether a new Pabau page family is worth commissioning. A family whose projected lead value barely covers the production and visual cost should be dropped in favor of refreshing pages that already rank.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge SEO on a ratio, not a margin. If the modeled value only slightly exceeds the cost, call it a no. Test the verdict by moving monthly cost a few thousand dollars; if the answer flips, the answer is no. A genuinely worthwhile SEO case clears its cost by a wide multiple.

### 11. Relevance engineering replaces SEO, AEO, and GEO  `20.18`
*core · general insights · source 20*

The speaker rejects 'SEO,' 'AEO,' and 'GEO' as accurate framings altogether, arguing you can't literally optimize someone else's engine — only the engineers who built it can — so what practitioners actually do is feature engineering: preparing content and websites as the input data these systems consume. His proposed replacement framework, 'relevance engineering,' is defined as the confluence of five disciplines — AI, information retrieval, content strategy, UX, and digital PR — and is explicitly designed to be surface-agnostic, applying equally to Google, app stores, AI platforms, or any future search surface that doesn't exist yet.

> "the confluence of AI, information retrieval, content strategy, UX, and digital PR"

**Evidence:** 'I think the term search engine optimization doesn't make any sense — neither does answer engine optimization or generative engine optimization... What I'm calling relevance engineering is really the confluence of AI, information retrieval, content strategy, UX, and digital PR.'

**Apply at Pabau:** David can use this as an internal organizing framework for Pabau's SEO/content function — deliberately building capability across all five named disciplines, not just content output — so the team's skill set stays valid as attention shifts between Google, AI answer engines, and whatever surface comes next.

**Apply anywhere:** You can use this as an internal organizing framework for your SEO/content function — deliberately building capability across all five named disciplines, not just content output — so the team's skill set stays valid as attention shifts between Google, AI answer engines, and whatever surface comes next.

### 12. Replace the thought-leadership topic list with pain-point terms  `111.13`
*core · concrete actions · source 111*

Grow and Convert name the default that companies like Leadfeeder would otherwise have followed: loosely related topics such as 'sales training tips', 'CRM best practices', 'sales funnel automations' and other thought-leadership pieces of the kind on HubSpot's sales blog. They went instead after terms where searchers indicated intent to buy or a problem Leadfeeder helped solve. The result was significantly higher conversion rates and product signups scaled from content much faster, reaching over 200 signups a month. The argument is not that the thought-leadership topics get no traffic. It is that the traffic is from people who cannot be sold to on that page. The swap is testable: for every thought-leadership topic on the calendar, there is a narrower term describing a problem the product fixes, and it is usually lower volume.

> "sales training tips," "CRM best practices"

**Evidence:** Leadfeeder scaled to over 200 signups a month using pain-point terms instead of the sales thought-leadership topics; Circuit saw organic traffic up 1,484% and conversions up 313%.

**How to do it**

1. Print the next quarter's content calendar and mark every topic that is category commentary rather than a problem you fix.
2. For each marked topic, write the narrower query a buyer with that problem would actually type.
3. Check both in Ahrefs and expect the narrower term to have far lower volume.
4. Confirm the narrower term maps to a feature, using the feature-mapping test.
5. Swap the calendar entry for the narrower term and note the volume you gave up.
6. Set the success metric for the swapped piece as conversions, not sessions.
7. Review after 90 days and compare conversions per article for swapped versus unswapped pieces.
8. Keep a small quota of commentary pieces only if they earn links, and judge them on links.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Traffic drops after the swap and the team reverts. The correct comparison is conversions per article, and it takes about a quarter of published pieces before the difference is visible.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should audit Pabau's /blog/ calendar for aesthetic-industry commentary pieces and swap each for a term naming a problem Pabau solves, then report conversions per article rather than sessions.

**Apply anywhere:** Audit your calendar for category commentary, swap each entry for the narrower query a buyer with that problem would type, and judge the swap on conversions per article over a full quarter.

### 13. Replicate each winning keyword across three verticals, not twenty  `130.2`
*core · best practices · source 130*

Grow and Convert's client served more than 20 industries, but the agency focused almost entirely on the three highest-priority verticals. Their rule was that once a keyword shape worked, they built the equivalent page for each of the three core verticals rather than for all twenty. So an industry-one invoicing app page was followed by industry-two and industry-three versions. This keeps the compounding effect of the industry axis while capping the production cost and avoiding a site full of thin near-duplicates for verticals the client does not really sell into. The choice of which three came from the client's own priorities, not from search volume.

> "we've focused almost entirely on their three highest priority verticals"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert: client serves 20+ industries; the engagement covered three, producing 120+ pieces and 150+ demo requests a month.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the business which verticals it actually prioritizes commercially, ranked, and take the top three.
2. Prove the keyword shape once in the strongest vertical before replicating it.
3. Once a page ranks and converts, build the same page for verticals two and three.
4. Write each vertical version with its own examples, terminology and pain points, not a find-and-replace of the first.
5. Hold the remaining verticals in a backlog and only open a fourth once the first three are fully covered.
6. Track conversions by vertical so a weak vertical can be dropped rather than expanded.
7. Review the vertical shortlist annually against sales pipeline data.

**Pitfall:** Replicating across all verticals at once produces near-duplicate pages that cannibalize each other and dilute crawl budget, and the verticals with no sales motion never convert regardless of ranking.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should pick three priority verticals and build the full keyword matrix for those before adding a fourth. Publishing one page per treatment type across every specialty at once would create near-duplicate template and software pages that compete with each other.

**Apply anywhere:** Pick your three commercially strongest verticals and replicate each proven keyword shape across just those. Prove the shape in one vertical first, then clone it with genuinely vertical-specific examples, and hold the rest in a backlog.

### 14. Report conversions per post, not traffic rank, to defend pain-point topics  `114.9`
*core · content insights · source 114*

Grow and Convert close the argument with their own numbers. Their post on pain-point SEO ranks tenth on their site for traffic, first for conversions, and converts four percent higher than any other post they have published. They present that gap as the whole case against traffic-chasing formats: top of funnel white papers, infographics and ultimate guides. The practical point is a reporting one. If you only rank posts by sessions, the piece doing the most commercial work sits in tenth place and looks like a candidate for cutting, while the high-traffic no-intent pieces look like your best work. Sorting the same set by conversions inverts the list. They say the result is not an accident but the direct output of choosing topics by customer pain point rather than by search volume.

> "the post is #10 for traffic, #1 for conversions"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's pain-point SEO post: #10 for traffic, #1 for conversions, converting four percent higher than any other post on the site.

**How to do it**

1. Set up conversion goals so every blog post can be attributed to signups, demos or leads.
2. Build one report that lists every post with sessions and conversions side by side.
3. Sort the report by conversions, not by sessions, as the default view.
4. Add a conversion rate column so low-traffic high-intent posts are visible.
5. Flag any post in the top ten by traffic that sits outside the top thirty by conversions as a no-intent ranking.
6. Flag any post in the top ten by conversions that sits low on traffic as a template for the next commissions.
7. Use the sorted-by-conversions list to decide what to commission next quarter.
8. Present both sortings to stakeholders so the traffic number never stands alone.

**Tools:** Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** A content program judged on sessions cuts its best converting pages during a pruning exercise because they sit low on the traffic list. The signal is a top-performing post by leads that nobody on the team can name.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's content reporting should sort /blog/ and template pages by demo requests, not by sessions. Pages ranking well on general aesthetics topics with no demo requests should be re-targeted rather than celebrated.

**Apply anywhere:** Make conversions the default sort in your content report, with sessions as a secondary column. Commission against the top of the conversion list, and treat high-traffic pages with no conversions as targets for retargeting rather than proof of success.

### 15. Report only conversions from the pages your content program created  `149.18`
*core · concrete actions · source 149*

The framing behind Grow and Convert's whole reporting setup: they build every report so it shows just the conversions their agency contributed, excluding conversions from all other pages. In the model comparison tool that is a Landing page dimension filter listing their own post URLs. In the exploration it is a regex in the sequence segment covering the same URL set. They note this is written for agencies, freelancers and consultants who report to clients, but say it works equally for in-house marketers who want to know how one part of the site, such as the blog, contributes. The point is that a site-wide conversion number never answers whether the content program is working, because the homepage and product pages swamp it.

> "just the conversions our agency is contributing"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say the URL filter step is what allows them to see and report on just the conversions their agency is contributing.

**How to do it**

1. Define the URL set your content program owns and keep it in a maintained list.
2. Write a regex that matches that set and nothing else, for example a blog path plus specific slugs.
3. Use the regex in the Explore sequence segment and the URL list in the model comparison filter.
4. Exclude homepage, pricing and product pages from the set so they cannot inflate the result.
5. Report the resulting figure as content-attributed conversions, labeled with the URL set.
6. Re-run with the same segment each month so the trend is comparable.
7. Add newly published URLs to the regex on publication, not at reporting time.

**Tools:** Google Analytics 4

**Pitfall:** A site-wide conversion count makes content look either irrelevant or heroic depending on what else changed that month, and neither reading is about the content.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should segment /blog/ and /templates/ conversions away from the marketing site entirely, so the content program's demo contribution is a standalone number rather than a share of a total the homepage dominates.

**Apply anywhere:** Segment the content program's URLs away from the marketing site so its conversion contribution is a standalone number, not a share of a site-wide total.

### 16. Require CEO/CMO sign-off on your target keyword universe  `21.8`
*core · best practices · source 21*

David argues every SEO effort - agency, freelance, or in-house - needs a documented 'keyword universe' explicitly signed off by the CEO or CMO, not just handed to a marketing manager. > "needs a keyword universe that's signed off all the way"

2. Present that list and get explicit sign-off from the CEO or CMO directly, not just a marketing manager.
3. Give the CEO/CMO direct access to a live keyword-performance view rather than only sending periodic summary reports.
4. Identify the core 'money' keywords that reflect the company's actual positioning and keep them distinct from secondary PR/partner/integration content.

### 17. Rule out SEO only when nobody searches for what you sell  `129.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 129*

Grow and Convert's screen for whether SEO is worth funding has exactly one disqualifier: no search demand for the product or service. Everything else — budget, competition, patience — is a scheduling question, not a go/no-go. They say it is rare to meet a profitable business in a niche so new that nobody searches for it, and if it has a market it probably has search volume. Their counter-example is a client whose earlier company sold a tool for validating the authenticity of B2B testimonials and case studies. 'B2B testimonial authentication tool' returned no registered volume, and neither did 'validate case studies' or 'validate testimonials'. The client confirmed they sold almost entirely by outbound, with salespeople explaining both the concept and the value. Category creation is possible with content, but they warn most companies lack the budget or the wherewithal for that long game.

> "reason you should rule out SEO as a channel"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's client sold a B2B testimonial authentication product with zero registered volume for the category term and its two nearest variants, and sold almost exclusively by outbound.

**How to do it**

1. Type your plain business category into Ahrefs, Semrush or Clearscope before you cost anything out.
2. Check the head term plus its variation list for registered monthly volume, not just difficulty.
3. Test two or three alternative phrasings of the same job, the way your customers would say it, before accepting a zero.
4. If every phrasing returns no registered volume, check how your sales team currently sells — heavy outbound with concept explanation confirms the zero.
5. Rule SEO out as a primary channel only on that combined evidence, not on cost or competition.
6. If demand exists at any level, keep SEO in and move on to sizing its value.
7. If you still want the category, budget for demand creation over years and fund it from somewhere other than the SEO line.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Semrush, Clearscope

**Pitfall:** Killing SEO for the wrong reason. Teams cite competition, budget or slow results, then rebuild the same argument as 'no demand'. The opposite error is inventing category language nobody types and reading that zero as proof the market does not search.

**Apply at Pabau:** Practice management for aesthetic and healthcare clinics clearly has demand, so Pabau should never treat 'is SEO worth it' as an open question. Apply this test instead to individual new page families — before commissioning a diagnostic-code or procedure-code set, confirm the codes are typed as searches rather than only used internally.

**Apply anywhere:** Use search demand as the single disqualifier for SEO. Put your plain category language into a keyword tool, test two or three customer phrasings, and only rule the channel out if every one returns no registered volume and your sales team already sells by explaining the concept from scratch.

### 18. Run SaaS SEO as five ordered steps with keyword selection first  `121.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 121*

Grow and Convert define SaaS SEO as picking keywords that indicate someone needs your product, building content to rank for them, then supporting that content with links and technical work. Their agency runs it as five steps in a fixed order: buying-intent keyword research, one dedicated post per keyword that deeply satisfies intent, selling the product inside each post, fixing and monitoring technical errors, and external link building. The order is the argument. They say keyword selection is the most important thing to get right, because it does not matter how technically clean the site is, how many links you build, or how much you publish if none of it points at queries that bring customers. Technical SEO and links sit at positions four and five deliberately, as support rather than as the engine.

> "selecting keywords that indicate people are looking for (or need) your product"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated process from 10+ years of agency work with dozens of SaaS companies, credited with page-one rankings for hundreds of buying-intent keywords.

**How to do it**

1. Write the buying-intent keyword list before commissioning any page, sorted by intent rather than volume.
2. Assign exactly one dedicated post per keyword on the list.
3. Analyze the SERP for each keyword to find the required topics and one differentiation angle.
4. Interview an internal product expert per post so the draft carries expertise the SERP lacks.
5. Include a product walkthrough with screenshots in every bottom-funnel post.
6. Run one thorough technical audit up front, then automated periodic audits.
7. Start monthly link building only after the pages exist, pointing links at the ones already converting.
8. Report conversions per article, not sessions, and reorder the keyword list each quarter on that data.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Teams start at step four or five because technical fixes and link buys are easy to scope and bill. The signal is a clean audit, a growing link profile, and no change in demos.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's SEO calendar should be driven by a buying-intent keyword list for practice management, with technical cleanup on pabau.com scoped as one-time work rather than a recurring line item that crowds out page production.

**Apply anywhere:** Sequence your search program so keyword selection comes first and technical work and links come last. Build the buying-intent list, give each term its own page, then use audits and links to support pages that already exist.

### 19. Run a four-question SERP check to choose blog post or landing page  `146.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 146*

Grow and Convert's decision framework is a SERP analysis, reviewing the titles, page types and sources of the page-one results for the bottom-of-funnel keyword. They ask which page types show up, whether a blog post fits the intent, whether a blog post could plausibly reach the top three, and what type of post would best satisfy the intent. If all or most results are blog posts, the decision is made. If the SERP is a mix of product landing pages and home pages with few or no posts, the client's brand authority breaks the tie. A well-known brand that gets product pages ranking for competitive terms should build the landing page, since the SERP suggests users want a product, not a wordy article. A brand search engines do not yet treat as an authority gets the blog post, because it is the more realistic route to a ranking.

> "we look to SERP analysis, which (among other steps) involves reviewing the titles"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert apply this framework across dozens of agency clients and cite it as the reason they almost always choose posts for bottom-of-funnel terms.

**How to do it**

1. Search the target bottom-of-funnel keyword and list every page-one result by type: review list, competitor home page, competitor product landing page, or blog post.
2. If all or a majority are blog posts, write a blog post and stop here.
3. If the SERP mixes product landing pages and home pages, ask whether a blog post can genuinely satisfy the intent behind the query.
4. Assess your own brand's authority in that space: do your product pages already rank for competitive bottom-of-funnel terms?
5. If yes, build the product landing page, since the SERP composition suggests searchers want a product page rather than a long article.
6. If no, write the blog post, because it gives more room for relevant keywords and time on page and a more realistic shot at ranking.
7. Then choose the post type that matches intent, defaulting to a list post when the intent is to view and weigh options.
8. Note the decision and the SERP composition so the call can be re-checked when the SERP changes.

**Pitfall:** Copying the dominant SERP format without checking your own authority. A weak domain that mimics the ranking product landing pages usually ends up with a thin page that never ranks.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run these four questions before every Pabau bottom-of-funnel commission and record the answer in the brief, since pabau.com is not yet the authority whose product pages outrank incumbents on category terms.

**Apply anywhere:** Run these four questions before every bottom-of-funnel commission and record the answer in the brief, weighting the authority question honestly against the incumbents on that SERP.

### 20. Run a paid-social disruption story to cover the ranking lag  `135.10`

Alongside the pain-point posts, Grow and Convert published a few articles with no SEO angle at all, telling the brand's story and the reasoning behind the innovation, then promoted them with social ads. Publishing one early was deliberate: it produced traffic and conversions in the months before any SEO post ranked. **How to do it**

### 23. SEO's core formula hasn't changed: keywords, relevance, authority, signals  `52.1`
*core · best practices · source 52*

Edward Sturm's recurring framework, what he calls the core of SEO, is that the most effective tactics genuinely have not changed over time, provided you're not trying to skirt Google's rules: choose the right keywords (ones that will actually bring conversions, whether customers, users, or warm leads), make the page relevant to those keywords, build real authority, reduce pogo-sticking, and avoid anything that seems clearly spammy. He argues newer SEOs mistakenly believe tactics are in constant flux, when in reality the appearance of change mostly comes from people getting more efficient with better tools, or from bad and outdated advice circulating, not from the underlying principles shifting. He uses this five-part formula as a standing checklist to evaluate any new tactic or claim, including ones made by guests on his own show.

> "choosing the right keywords, plus relevance, plus authority, plus reduce pogo"

**How to do it**

1. Write down Edward's five-part formula as a standing checklist: right keywords, relevance, authority, reduced pogo-sticking, avoid clearly spammy tactics.
2. Before adopting any new SEO tactic or piece of advice, test it against each of the five criteria in that checklist.
3. For "right keywords," confirm the target keyword is one that plausibly brings conversions, customers, signups, or warm leads, not just traffic.
4. For "relevance," confirm the page's content and structure genuinely match what that keyword's searcher wants.
5. For "authority," confirm the tactic builds real referral-traffic-generating links or ranking-driven authority, not just link volume.
6. For "reduce pogo-sticking," check whether the tactic could hurt user satisfaction or engagement on the page.
7. For "avoid spam," ask whether the tactic would look clearly manipulative to a reasonable observer, and reject it if so, regardless of short-term results.
8. Re-apply this same checklist periodically to tactics already in use, not only to new ones, since the goal is a consistent standard rather than a one-time filter.

**Pitfall:** Assuming SEO "keeps changing" and therefore any confident-sounding new tactic might be the new right answer is the trap — the formula argues most apparent change is really either increased efficiency with tools, or bad advice, not the underlying principles moving.
