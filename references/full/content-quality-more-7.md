### 1. Treat a two-thirds top-funnel archive as normal after two years  `132.10`
*useful · content insights · source 132*

Grow and Convert flag something people find counterintuitive in their own case study: despite preaching bottom-funnel first, the Geekbot archive is 65.6 percent top-funnel. They explain it as a function of two things, how long they worked with the client and how many bottom-funnel keywords existed in that space. Two years of steady publishing simply outlasts a finite list of 22 buying-intent terms. This is worth holding onto when auditing a mature blog, because the raw ratio of top to bottom funnel content tells you very little on its own. The diagnostic question is not what proportion of the archive is top-funnel, it is whether the bottom-funnel list was completed before the top-funnel work started. A blog can be two thirds informational and correctly sequenced, or one third informational and badly sequenced.

> "That's simply a function of how long we've worked with them"

**Evidence:** Geekbot after roughly two years: 42 of 64 articles (65.6%) top-funnel, because only 22 bottom-funnel keywords existed in the category.

**Pitfall:** Auditing a blog by content-mix ratio alone gives the wrong verdict in both directions. Ask which pages were published first and whether any buying-intent keyword is still unwritten.

**Apply at Pabau:** If pabau.com's blog looks heavily weighted toward guides, that is not automatically a fault. David should check publication order and the unwritten bottom-funnel list instead, and only call the mix wrong if buying-intent pages are still missing.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not judge a mature blog by its ratio of informational to buying-intent content. Check publication order and whether any buying-intent keyword remains unwritten; a correctly sequenced blog can still end up two thirds informational simply because the buying-intent list was finite.

### 2. Treat nailed intent as both a ranking signal and a conversion lever  `96.4`
*useful · content insights · source 96*

Grow and Convert give two reasons intent matching matters, and the second is the one most SEO advice omits. If the piece is exactly what the searcher wanted, readers spend more time on it and click into things, producing on-page behavior signals that tell Google the piece satisfies intent, which they argue makes higher rankings likelier. Separately, the searcher feels the brand gets them, and is therefore more likely to give the product a chance and convert. So intent work pays twice: once in rankings and once in conversion rate. This is why they define SEO content writing as choosing topics, structuring articles and writing content intended both to rank for valuable keywords and to convert readers into customers, rather than the usual definition of inserting keywords into pages.

> "the searcher is more likely to feel your brand "gets" them"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's stated experience across dozens of brands, and their claim of hundreds of top-three rankings using this definition.

**Pitfall:** Optimizing for keyword coverage alone. The page can score well on a content grader and still miss what the searcher wanted, which costs both the behavior signals and the sale.

**Apply at Pabau:** Judge a Pabau article by whether a clinic owner reading it feels understood, not only by keyword coverage. That test belongs in the /fact editorial pass alongside the factual checks.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge a draft by whether the searcher would feel understood, not only by keyword coverage. Intent matching lifts rankings and conversion at the same time.

### 3. Treat the Skyscraper Technique's four levers as optional, not a checklist  `100.13`
*useful · content insights · source 100*

Grow and Convert critique Brian Dean's Skyscraper Technique on the same axis as 10X content. Step two of the technique is 'make something better', and Dean defines better as longer, more up to date, better designed and more thorough, recommending all four at once. Their objection is that this turns any keyword into a grueling journey: longer than every page-one result, then more current, then more thorough, then professionally designed. They concede Dean executes it masterfully, noting Backlinko ranks top five for terms like 'link building', 'backlinks' and 'keyword research'. But they argue most businesses do not need head terms to generate qualified traffic and leads, so moving heaven and earth for a huge guide makes no sense on lower volume, high-intent keywords. Their second objection is that originality is missing from the definition entirely, so 'taller' has collapsed into 'longer'.

> "I think originality is missing from the Skyscraper Technique definition"

**Evidence:** Backlinko ranks in the top five for 'link building', 'backlinks' and 'keyword research', which Grow and Convert accept as evidence the technique works on head terms specifically.

**Pitfall:** Running all four Skyscraper levers on every keyword stalls the calendar and still ships unoriginal work, because none of the four levers requires an original idea.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau refreshes an article, David should not default to making it longer and more thorough. Add the missing original element first, then only extend length where the SERP genuinely demands it.

**Apply anywhere:** Do not run longer, fresher, better-designed and more thorough as a mandatory checklist. None of them adds an original idea, and on low-volume terms the effort is wasted.

### 4. Treat under-level B2B writing as reputational risk, not just weak copy  `178.13`
*useful · best practices · source 178*

Grow and Convert raise the stake on domain expertise beyond conversion rate. For B2B businesses whose audiences are advanced industry experts, content that does not speak at the reader's level risks reputational damage and turns off potential customers. That reframes generic content as actively harmful rather than merely ineffective, and it changes who should sign off. They extend the same logic to B2C when the strategy targets leads and sales, because those topics are product or service centric and demand knowledge of the product, the nuanced pain points it solves, and how it differs from competitors. The practical consequence is that expert review is not a nice-to-have quality gate on technical content. It is the control that stops a published article from costing you credibility with the exact buyers you were trying to reach.

> "reputational damage and turning off potential customers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say B2B audiences of advanced industry experts must be communicated with at an expert level or you risk reputational damage.

**How to do it**

1. Classify each topic by how expert its audience is, and mark the expert-audience ones.
2. Require sign-off from an internal practitioner on every article in that group, before publication.
3. Give the reviewer one instruction: flag anything a working professional would find naive or wrong.
4. Treat a naive framing as a blocking issue, at the same severity as a factual error.
5. Track flagged articles by writer, and route repeat offenders back to an expert interview.
6. Do not publish an expert-audience article that no internal expert has read.

**Pitfall:** Treating expert review as an optional accuracy check. An article that is technically correct but pitched at a beginner's level still signals to a specialist reader that you do not work in their world.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau content aimed at clinic owners, practitioners and compliance topics should carry a named internal reviewer, since a naive framing of a clinical or regulatory workflow costs credibility with exactly the buyer Pabau wants.

**Apply anywhere:** Mark which topics have expert audiences and require an internal practitioner to sign off on those before publishing. Pitch level is a credibility risk, not just a quality preference.

### 5. Use earned external links as the test of whether a piece led anything  `125.15`
*useful · best practices · source 125*

Grow and Convert use external link counts as their operational definition of thought leadership. Reviewing Growth Rock's best pages by links in Ahrefs, they note that the data pieces and A-B test case studies each have a nontrivial number of external links, and call that a key indicator of having thought leadership. The logic runs the other way too: their diagnosis of me-too content is that prospects are not impressed, nobody cares and nobody shares it. So the metric is not traffic or time on page, it is whether other people in the industry felt the need to cite you. This gives a program a check that cannot be gamed by production quality, since a beautifully designed ebook with no ideas earns no links at all.

> "a key indicator of having thought leadership"

**Evidence:** Growth Rock's top pages by links in Ahrefs: three of the top eleven are data pieces or single-element A-B case studies, each with nontrivial external links, which Grow and Convert treat as the indicator of thought leadership.

**How to do it**

1. Set up an Ahrefs best-by-links report for your own domain and review it quarterly.
2. Tag every page you published as thought leadership and check its referring domain count at 90 and 180 days.
3. Treat zero organically earned referring domains at 180 days as a failed piece, regardless of traffic.
4. Separate manually built links from earned ones, since only earned links test the idea.
5. Look at which of your categories earn links: data pieces, case studies, opinion or disruption stories.
6. Reallocate next quarter's budget toward whichever category actually earned links.
7. Do not judge a piece on design quality or word count, since neither predicts citation.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Reading a link count too early. Data pieces accumulate citations slowly as other writers research their topics, so a 30-day check reads as failure on pieces that go on to earn hundreds of links.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should review Pabau's best-by-links report each quarter and check which article types earn referring domains. If the aesthetic practice guides earn none after 180 days, shift budget to data analyses and practice case studies.

**Apply anywhere:** Judge thought leadership pieces by earned referring domains at 90 and 180 days, not by traffic or design. Separate built links from earned ones, and shift budget to whichever content type actually gets cited.

### 6. Use headings for both reader navigation and heading-level keyword weight  `96.14`
*useful · best practices · source 96*

Grow and Convert give two explicit reasons for their heading structure. Headings organize the post so it is easy to follow, which improves readability and user experience, decreases bounce rates and positively influences other quality signals Google uses. Separately, they say Google's algorithm places weight on relevant keywords in page headings and subheadings, so a well-structured outline also improves on-page optimization and ability to rank. They use H2 for the main sections, H3 for the steps inside them, and H4 for sub-procedures a reader would get stuck on, as in their TapClicks outline where 'How to Create a New Widget' sits as an H4 under the template-customization H3. The nesting depth reflects the actual task hierarchy rather than being decorative.

> "Headings serve 2 key functions"

**How to do it**

1. Give every distinct section an H2 and never let a section run past a screen without a subhead.
2. Nest H3s for the steps within a section and H4s only for genuine sub-procedures.
3. Phrase each heading so a scanner can tell what the section delivers.
4. Work the relevant keyword variants into headings where they read naturally.
5. Check the heading tree alone reads as a coherent summary of the article.
6. Do not skip heading levels to control visual size; use styling for that.

**Pitfall:** Using heading levels for font size rather than hierarchy, which breaks the outline and removes the navigation benefit that reduces bounce.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's block contract already fixes the document order; the addition here is checking the H2/H3/H4 tree reads as a standalone summary before publishing, especially on long template articles.

**Apply anywhere:** Structure headings to match the real task hierarchy, phrase them so a scanner understands the payoff, and let keywords appear only where natural.

### 7. Use the blog post's extra room to argue positioning against competitors  `146.12`
*useful · best practices · source 146*

Grow and Convert give a content reason for the conversion gap they measured, not just a traffic reason. A product landing page format is typically thin on content. It gives you less room to discuss your positioning at length or to educate visitors on how the product differs from and beats competitors. A blog post gives you that room, and also more space to include relevant keywords and to increase time on site, which they list as ranking factors Google takes into account. So the same length that makes a post easier to rank is what lets it out-sell a landing page, provided the writing actually sells. They are explicit that most companies do not use the room this way, which is why landing pages usually win by default.

> "a product landing page format is typically thin on content"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert attribute their measured 1.13% blog rate against 0.4% for landing pages partly to the room a post gives for positioning and competitor differentiation.

**How to do it**

1. On any bottom-of-funnel post, dedicate a named section to how your product differs from the alternatives on that SERP.
2. Write the differentiation as specifics a buyer can check, not adjectives, for example which integrations and which workflow steps.
3. Educate before you pitch: explain what the category actually does and what varies between vendors.
4. Use the extra length to cover the relevant secondary keywords a landing page has no room for.
5. Place a conversion CTA where the product argument starts, not only at the end of the post.
6. Check the finished post reads as a decision aid rather than a brochure, and cut anything that is claim without evidence.

**Pitfall:** Writing a long post that never argues for the product. It ranks and takes traffic, then converts at the top-of-funnel rate, which reinforces the belief that posts do not convert.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's bottom-of-funnel articles should each carry a section that says plainly how Pabau differs from the named alternatives for aesthetic and healthcare practices, with the book-demo CTA placed at that section rather than only in the conclusion.

**Apply anywhere:** Bottom-of-funnel articles should each carry a section that says plainly how your product differs from the named alternatives, with the CTA placed at that section rather than only in the conclusion.

### 8. Vanity metrics are a symptom of a strategy that cannot convert  `150.14`
*useful · general insights · source 150*

Grow and Convert answer their own question about why revenue reporting is not standard practice. It is not mainly a skills gap. Most consultants, agencies and in-house teams are running a content strategy that would not generate ROI in the first place, so even if they knew how to track leads they would have nothing to report. That makes vanity metrics rational from their side: social shares, overall traffic, or output metrics like articles published and word count are easier to move and safer to present. Their prescription is that the reporting problem cannot be fixed at the reporting layer. You need a strategy that attracts people ready to buy, sign up or request a demo, and that means bottom-of-funnel topics and keywords first, because that is where the leads and sales come from.

> "it's not in their interest to report on a meaty"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert attribute the industry's reliance on vanity metrics to most content strategies being unable to produce ROI in the first place.

**How to do it**

1. If your report leads with traffic, shares or articles published, treat that as a strategy diagnosis, not a reporting choice.
2. Instrument the revenue KPI first, then look at what the last six months of content actually produced.
3. Expect the number to be small; do not soften it with a proxy metric.
4. Classify the published archive by intent: purchase-intent, comparison, how-to, definitional.
5. Shift the next quarter's calendar to bottom-of-funnel topics before touching the reporting deck again.
6. Ban output metrics such as word count and articles published from the report entirely.
7. Re-report the revenue KPI after two quarters of the new mix and compare.

**Pitfall:** Fixing the dashboard without fixing the topic selection just makes a failing program legible. The signal is accurate lead tracking on a blog that produces almost no leads.

**Apply at Pabau:** If Pabau's blog reporting still leads with sessions, the underlying issue is the topic mix rather than the dashboard. David should classify the existing /blog/ archive by intent before rebuilding reporting.

**Apply anywhere:** If your content report leads with traffic, shares or articles published, the problem is usually the strategy rather than the measurement. Instrument the revenue KPI, accept the small number, then move the calendar to bottom-of-funnel topics before redesigning the report.

### 9. Verify a nugget is unique by checking all ten page-one results  `100.14`
*useful · best practices · source 100*

Grow and Convert repeat one verification step across every example, light and heavy. After choosing an angle they check the other results in the SERP to confirm nobody has it. For the Leadfeeder list they say you can check the other 10 results in the SERP to confirm the claim, and that nobody else has it arranged that way, so it is unique. For the hiring post they establish that no one had taken the 'hiring marketers isn't easy' angle. For the marketing-job post they check that none of the other page-one results had a section on why you are not getting jobs. For the mobile checkout study they state they know of no other resource with that data. Originality is defined relative to the competing set, not in the abstract, so the check is what makes the claim real.

> "You can check the other 10 results in the SERP to confirm this claim"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert apply this check across their light, medium and heavy examples and use it as the basis for calling each piece unique.

**How to do it**

1. Open all ten page-one results for the target keyword in tabs before writing.
2. Record each result's format, organizing structure and any distinctive section.
3. Compare your proposed nugget against that list item by item.
4. Drop any nugget that appears in even one result and pick another.
5. Re-run the check at publication time if the draft took weeks, since the SERP changes.
6. Note in the brief which competitor came closest, so the writer knows what to beat.
7. Repeat the check on the refresh cycle to see whether the nugget has been copied.

**Pitfall:** Assuming an idea is original because it feels fresh to you. The signal is discovering after publishing that the result ranking third has the same section, which explains why the piece never moved.

**Apply at Pabau:** Add a SERP-check line to the Pabau brief template listing all ten results and their formats, with the chosen nugget marked as absent from each. This should be a required field before a writer is assigned.

**Apply anywhere:** Before writing, open all ten page-one results and record their formats and distinctive sections. Only claim a nugget as original after confirming none of them has it, and re-check at publication.

### 10. Vulnerability is a differentiator most competitors will not copy  `123.14`
*useful · content insights · source 123*

Explaining why the Grow and Convert launch worked, Hyam points at exposure rather than novelty. The uniqueness was that most sites simply do not make themselves vulnerable by committing publicly to a number and a deadline they might miss. He says the strategy worked because they attempted something that was not easy in their industry and did it so people could follow along. Readers were, in his words, excited to see whether they succeeded or failed. He does not overclaim: it was not world changing and they were not the first to share growth transparently. The differentiation came from being willing to be seen failing, which is a moat because competitors with more to lose will not take it, not because the idea is hard to think of.

> "most sites simply don't make themselves vulnerable like this"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert publicly targeted 40,000 monthly uniques in six months, pivoted away from the challenge near the end, and reported 39,105 uniques in five months; reader comments showed people following to see whether they would fail.

**Pitfall:** Announcing a commitment you have no intention of reporting honestly. Once you stop publishing the bad months, or quietly move the goalposts, the audience that came for the story leaves and the credibility loss outlasts the traffic.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau can be vulnerable in a narrow, safe lane: publish a named practice's real numbers before and after a workflow change, including what did not improve. Competitors publishing only success stories will not match it.

**Apply anywhere:** Being publicly accountable is a differentiator because competitors with more to lose will not copy it. Commit to a number and a date, report the bad months as honestly as the good ones, and let readers follow whether you make it.

### 11. When an edit stalls, stop and rewrite the passage from scratch  `72.13`
*useful · best practices · source 72*

Khanal gets stuck trying to repair an AI paragraph and names the move: stop editing and write your own version fresh from your brain to work out what is making you uncomfortable. His diagnosis of the stall is that you sense something is wrong but cannot articulate why, and trying to produce a polished sentence at the same time trips you up. So the fresh rewrite should not aim for polish, it should aim to get the idea right, with polish coming afterward. He adds a working condition from the live stream: he cannot write and talk at the same time, so he goes quiet to write and explains afterward. Writing and narrating are different tasks and doing both degrades the writing.

> "just write your own thing fresh from your brain"

**Evidence:** Khanal applied this live after repeatedly failing to fix an AI paragraph, and the fresh rewrite led him to discard the three-category framework entirely.

**How to do it**

1. Notice the stall: you have reworded the same sentence more than twice and still dislike it.
2. Stop editing the existing text and open a blank space below it.
3. Write your own version of the idea in plain unpolished language, ignoring style entirely.
4. Compare the two and identify what the original was doing wrong, which is usually now obvious.
5. Keep whichever version carries the idea, then polish it as a separate pass.
6. Do the writing in silence rather than while explaining or in a meeting, since narration degrades it.

**Pitfall:** Polishing while you are still working out the argument. Khanal says trying to write polished prose is exactly what stops you from getting the idea right.

**Apply at Pabau:** When a Pabau editorial pass gets stuck on an AI-drafted paragraph, have the editor rewrite it from scratch in plain language rather than iterating on the draft. That rewrite often exposes a structural problem, not a wording one.

**Apply anywhere:** When you have reworded a sentence three times and still dislike it, stop editing and write your own plain version from scratch, then polish as a separate pass.

### 12. Write JTBD posts for a reader who does not know your category exists  `105.13`
*useful · content insights · source 105*

Grow and Convert split their three buying-intent keyword buckets by what the reader already knows, and that split changes how the article is written. Category keywords and competitor comparison or alternatives keywords come from readers who already know a product like yours is the solution; they are choosing between vendors. Jobs-to-be-done keywords, such as 'how to track employee time off' or 'how to stop losing leads in my pipeline', come from readers who have not yet worked out that a product category solves this. They are still high buying intent, because the searcher is actively trying to fix something rather than browsing. The writing consequence is that a JTBD post has to teach the manual method credibly first and introduce the product category as the better route, while a category or comparison post can open on vendor selection.

> "Readers in the last bucket don't yet know what the solution"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert place JTBD keywords higher in the funnel than category and comparison keywords in their own diagram, while still classifying them as high buying intent.

**Pitfall:** Writing a JTBD post like a comparison post loses the reader in the first paragraph, because they never asked which vendor. The signal is a page ranking for a 'how to' query with high bounce and no conversions.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's 'how to' articles, such as tracking consent forms or reducing no-shows, should walk through the manual process honestly and then introduce practice management software as the better route. The comparison and 'software for [vertical]' pages can open straight on vendor selection.

**Apply anywhere:** Write jobs-to-be-done posts for a reader who has not realized a product category solves their problem: teach the manual method credibly, then introduce the category as the better route. Category and comparison posts can open directly on vendor selection because that reader is already choosing.

### 13. Write disruption stories that target no keyword at all  `153.13`
*useful · content insights · source 153*

Grow and Convert describe a content type they run alongside SEO articles. A disruption story highlights the core problem the business was created to solve. Unlike SEO content it does not target a keyword. It is built to be shareable on social media and to reach buyers who are not actively searching for a solution. They position it as the one exception to their view that social media is weak for B2B lead generation, because B2B buying is driven by specific needs like outgrowing a CRM and usually requires a company-wide process change. Social media works better for B2C, where buyers decide on personal preference and immediate need. The disruption story is how a B2B company gets social distribution without pretending to be B2C.

> "Disruption Stories focus on highlighting the core problem your business was created to solve"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert use disruption stories as the exception to their position that social media is ineffective for B2B lead generation, which they attribute to B2B buying requiring process change.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the specific problem that caused your company to be founded, in the founder's own words.
2. Name who suffers from that problem today and what it costs them, with a concrete example.
3. Draft the piece as a narrative argument about the problem, not as a product page and not around any keyword.
4. Skip keyword research, headings-for-search and on-page optimization for this piece entirely.
5. Publish it on your own site so the traffic and any links land on your domain.
6. Distribute it through founder and executive social accounts rather than the brand account.
7. Measure it on shares, replies and inbound conversations, not on rankings or organic sessions.
8. Run one or two a quarter alongside the keyword-targeted articles, not instead of them.

**Pitfall:** Judging a disruption story by organic traffic. It targets no keyword, so it will look like a failure on the SEO dashboard while doing the job it was written for.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has a natural disruption story in what badly-run practice admin costs a clinic owner in hours and no-shows. David should commission one or two a quarter, publish them on pabau.com outside the keyword-targeted set, and measure them on shares and inbound conversations.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish one or two disruption stories a quarter about the problem your company exists to solve. Target no keyword, distribute through personal social accounts, and measure on shares and conversations rather than rankings.

### 14. Write specificity about who the product suits so personalization can match it  `84.12`
*useful · content insights · source 84*

Grow & Convert's first argument for owned content is a content-design argument, not a distribution one. Content on your own site gives you room and flexibility to explain the details and nuances of who your products help, in what scenarios and how. They note ChatGPT in particular knows a lot of details about the user and personalizes its recommendations, so the more specific and complete your on-site content is, the more likely it is to be cited and recommended in the right moments. That reframes the job of a product or comparison page. It is not to rank for a head term with generic feature copy, it is to carry enough qualifying detail about fit, scenario and constraint that a model matching a personalized query can find the sentence that matches this particular user.

> "the room and flexibility to explain the details and nuances of who your products help"

**Evidence:** Grow & Convert's stated reason for placing owned content above off-site mentions in their Prioritized GEO pyramid.

**Pitfall:** Feature-list pages with no statement of who the product suits give the model nothing to match against a qualified query, so it recommends a competitor whose page names the scenario.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's product and comparison pages should say plainly which practice types, sizes and specialties each feature suits, so a model answering 'software for a two-room injectables clinic' has a matching passage to cite.

**Apply anywhere:** State plainly on your product pages which customer types, sizes and situations each capability suits, so a personalized query has a matching passage to retrieve.

### 15. Write the comparison your buyer would otherwise make on Amazon or in town  `179.17`
*useful · best practices · source 179*

Grow and Convert extend their product-copywriting argument beyond SaaS to two other business types with the same underlying requirement. For an ecommerce store, the content has to explain why your products are worth buying over the dozens of alternatives the customer can find on Amazon. For a local service business chasing local SEO terms, the content has to communicate why someone should choose you over the other providers in town. In both cases they say generic content that could apply to any competitor will not cut it. The mechanism is the same one that drives their intent-first strategy: high buying-intent searchers are comparing options at the moment they search, so the page that makes the comparison for them wins both the ranking and the sale. Low-intent, higher-volume topics let you skate by without this; buying-intent keywords do not.

> "why someone should choose you over the other providers in town"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert say the requirement holds identically for SaaS, ecommerce and local service businesses, because the searcher on a buying-intent query is comparing options.

**How to do it**

1. List the alternatives a buyer realistically considers alongside you: for ecommerce, the Amazon listings; for local, the named providers in your area.
2. On every buying-intent page, add a section that answers why you rather than each of those alternatives.
3. Replace generic benefit claims with ones a competitor could not copy verbatim onto their own page.
4. Pull the reasons from what customers actually say when they choose you, taken from sales calls and support conversations.
5. Name the alternative categories directly where you can do so without disparaging any specific competitor.
6. Test each page by asking whether a competitor could publish it unchanged; if they could, rewrite it.
7. Keep the generic-safe version only for low-intent topics where the comparison is not what the searcher wants.

**Pitfall:** Writing differentiation as adjectives. Fast, reliable and easy to use appear on every competitor's page too, so they fail the copy-it-unchanged test and leave the searcher with nothing to decide on.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's comparison and alternatives pages should name the specific reasons aesthetic practices choose Pabau over each competitor, drawn from sales conversations. A page a competitor could republish with their own name on it is not doing the job.

**Apply anywhere:** On any buying-intent page, answer why you rather than the alternatives the buyer is genuinely weighing, whether that is Amazon listings or the other providers in town. Pull the reasons from what customers say when they choose you, not from adjectives. Then run the test: could a competitor publish this page unchanged? If yes, rewrite it.

### 16. Write the intro for someone weighing 400 other priorities  `127.12`
*useful · best practices · source 127*

Grow and Convert frame the reader's state before they analyze any copy. The sales manager sees the article on the commute home, does not have much time, and the headline piques their interest because they are tired of the CRM their team is using and are thinking about switching. Then the line that sets the bar: it is one of 400 other things they are thinking of doing. The reader is not evaluating your article, they are half-deciding whether this problem is worth attention at all this week. That rules out any opener that requires patience. It also explains why the specific-problem opener works: naming the exact operational failure is what moves the topic from item 400 to item one. The distraction is the constraint the intro has to survive, not the reader's intelligence.

> "It's one of 400 other things you're thinking of doing"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert set the scene with the reader on a commute, short on time, with the article as one of 400 things they are considering.

**How to do it**

1. Assume the reader gives the intro one sentence of attention before deciding to leave.
2. Put the most specific claim or problem statement in that first sentence, not the second or third.
3. Cut any clause that has to be read before the specific claim makes sense.
4. Keep the first sentence short enough to read on a phone in one glance.
5. Name the consequence of not fixing the problem, so the topic competes with the reader's other priorities.
6. Read the intro on a phone screen and check the specific claim is visible without scrolling.
7. Do not rely on the headline having already done this work; the intro has to re-earn the read.

**Pitfall:** Writers assume the click means the reader is committed. They are not; the click was cheap and the topic is competing with everything else on the reader's list, so a slow opener loses to a notification.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau intros should put the specific problem in sentence one and name what it costs, because a practice owner reads between patients. Check the first sentence renders above the fold on mobile for template and blog pages.

**Apply anywhere:** Assume the reader is skimming on a phone with the topic ranked somewhere near the bottom of a long list. Put the specific problem and its cost in the first sentence, keep it short, and check it is visible on a mobile screen without scrolling.

### 17. Write the limitations-of-the-free-workaround post for your category  `111.10`
*useful · concrete actions · source 111*

Grow and Convert used a repeatable pattern for Circuit's mid-funnel work: 'shortest route for multiple destinations in Google Maps'. The searcher is trying to achieve in a free tool what Circuit makes much easier. The post discussed the limitations of Google Maps for route optimization and showed how Circuit provides a better solution. This is the same structure as their Excel template play, applied to a competing free tool rather than a spreadsheet. Every category has one: the free or generic tool buyers try first. The keyword is high volume because the workaround is popular, the intent is a job your product does, and the article earns the right to compare because the reader arrived already trying to do the thing. Grow and Convert pair it with 'how to be a faster delivery driver', a direct pain-point term where route optimization is presented as the fix.

> "we discussed the limitations of Google Maps for route optimization"

**Evidence:** Circuit's 'shortest route for multiple destinations in Google Maps' post, plus 'how to be a faster delivery driver' as the direct pain-point companion.

**How to do it**

1. Name the free or generic tool your buyers try before they buy software: a spreadsheet, Google Maps, a calendar, a paper form.
2. Find the keyword describing the hardest job people attempt in that tool.
3. Write the honest step-by-step of doing it in the free tool, including screenshots.
4. List the exact points where it breaks: item limits, no re-optimization, no sharing, manual re-entry.
5. Quantify at least one limit with a number the reader can check.
6. Show the same job in your product against the same limits.
7. State the threshold at which the free tool is still the right answer, to keep the piece credible.
8. Add the matching direct pain-point keyword as a second article and link the two.

**Pitfall:** Overstating the free tool's flaws. Readers use it daily and know the article is wrong, which costs the credibility the comparison depends on.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, the workarounds are Google Calendar, paper consent forms and WhatsApp reminders. Each deserves a /blog/ article on doing the job that way, its exact limits, and the same job in Pabau.

**Apply anywhere:** Write the article on doing the job in the free tool your buyers already use, name its precise limits with numbers, then show the same job in your product and say where the free tool is still enough.

### 18. Write to expert level for B2B or accept reputational damage  `101.12`
*useful · best practices · source 101*

Inside their step on expressing domain expertise, Grow and Convert make a sharper point about B2B specifically. B2B audiences are often advanced industry experts, and content pitched below their level does more than fail to convert. They frame it as reputational risk: you turn off potential customers who now know you do not understand their work. This is why the expertise extraction process matters more in B2B than in consumer content, and why the 'Google research paper' pattern of summarizing what already ranks is more dangerous there. When the strategy is optimized for leads, the topics are product and service centric, so the writer needs the nuanced pain points and the differentiation from competitors, not a general grasp of the category.

> "you risk reputational damage and turning off potential customers"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite this as the reason per-piece expert interviews matter most for B2B clients.

**How to do it**

1. Identify the seniority and expertise level of the actual buyer for each topic.
2. Have a practitioner, not a marketer, read the draft and flag anything they would not say.
3. Cut definitions of terms your audience uses daily.
4. Require at least one detail per article that could only come from operating experience.
5. Name specific competitor approaches and how yours differs, rather than describing the category.
6. Kill any draft that reads as a summary of the current top-ranking pages.

**Pitfall:** Explaining basics to experts reads as ignorance; the cost is credibility with exactly the buyers you most want, and it does not show up in traffic data.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau articles aimed at practice owners and clinic managers must assume they know their own workflow; the differentiating detail has to come from Pabau specialists who have watched real clinics run.

**Apply anywhere:** For expert B2B audiences, content written at a beginner level costs credibility rather than just conversions. Have a practitioner review every draft and require at least one detail that only operating experience produces.

### 19. Write what a knowledgeable friend would know before recommending you  `83.12`
*useful · best practices · source 83*

Grow and Convert close on an analogy that is a usable writing test. Asking an AI for a recommendation is like asking a friend. Devesh's literal question to Benji might be 'what board should I buy', but Benji answers the effective question, factoring in that Devesh is a beginner, 6 foot 6, and currently rides a 10 foot foam board. The same is true of asking a friend about software, a law firm, clothes or a coffee shop. The one difference matters: Benji knows surfboards from years of surfing, while ChatGPT only knows what it has read. So if you want an LLM to recommend you the way a knowledgeable friend would, you have to have published the content that gives it the context to do it. The test for any page is whether a stranger reading only your site could match you to a specific buyer's circumstances.

> "ChatGPT only knows what it has read"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's surfboard example: the literal question is four words, the effective question includes skill level, height and current board.

**How to do it**

1. List the qualifying questions your sales team asks on a first call.
2. Write down the answers that decide whether a prospect is a good fit or a bad one.
3. Publish a page per fit criterion, naming customer size, industry and constraint explicitly.
4. Include the situations where you are the wrong choice, since that is what a friend would say.
5. Add the concrete details a friend would factor in, such as team size, budget range and existing tools.
6. Test each page by asking whether a stranger reading only it could match you to a specific buyer.
7. Update the pages whenever sales starts hearing a new qualifying question.

**Tools:** ChatGPT

**Pitfall:** Product knowledge that lives only in sales calls and onboarding is invisible to models. If your best fit and worst fit criteria have never been written down publicly, no LLM can apply them.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should publish the qualifying knowledge sales uses on calls, such as which practice sizes and specialties fit best and which do not. That is exactly the context an LLM needs to route the right practice to Pabau.

**Apply anywhere:** Publish the qualifying knowledge your sales team applies on calls, including which customers fit best and which do not. That is the context an AI needs before it can recommend you the way an informed friend would.

### 20. Run a free decay tool over old posts before commissioning new ones  `180.12`
*context · concrete actions · source 180*

In profiling Animalz, Grow and Convert note that the agency built Revive, a free tool that identifies content decay and finds opportunities to refresh outdated articles. That is worth separating from the agency profile, because content decay auditing is work any site owner can do without an engagement. It also matches the wider position in this base that refreshing declining pages beats publishing new ones. The pattern to look for is a page whose traffic peaked and has fallen steadily since, rather than one that never worked. Those are the pages where the ranking history proves demand and the only missing piece is currency.

> "a free tool that helps marketers identify content decay"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite Animalz's Revive as a free tool for identifying content decay and refresh opportunities.

**How to do it**

1. Connect Revive or your analytics export and pull organic sessions per URL by month for the last 24 months.
2. Flag any URL whose current month sits below 60 percent of its own peak month.
3. Drop URLs that peaked on a one-off news spike, since those are not decay.
4. Sort the survivors by peak traffic, so you refresh the pages with the most to recover first.
5. For each, check the current SERP and note what the top three pages cover that yours does not.
6. Update figures, screenshots, product names and dates first; these are usually the whole problem.
7. Republish and re-check position after four weeks before deciding whether a structural rewrite is needed.
8. Only commission new articles once the top ten decaying pages have been refreshed.

**Tools:** Revive, Google Analytics, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Refreshing pages that never ranked. Decay work only pays on pages with a proven peak; a page that launched flat has a targeting problem, not a currency problem.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has a large back catalogue of blog and template pages. David should run a decay pass before each batch of new commissions, since a refreshed page with a ranking history returns faster than a new URL.

**Apply anywhere:** Before commissioning new articles, run a decay audit: find pages now below 60 percent of their own traffic peak, refresh the ten biggest, and check positions four weeks later.
