### 1. Mine HARO pitch requests daily for topic ideas  `33.7`
*core · best practices · source 33*

The guest deliberately blends reactive and active PR: every day he scans incoming reactive-PR requests (from HARO/Qwoted-style platforms) not just to respond for a citation, but as intelligence on what journalists are actively researching and about to publish. Because those requests reveal upcoming content before it's live elsewhere, he can build original content on the same or an adjacent angle and pitch it proactively while the topic is still current, deciding case by case whether to match the exact angle a journalist is fishing for or offer a related angle they haven't explicitly asked for. This is framed as an ongoing daily habit rather than a one-off exercise, since new requests (and therefore new topic intelligence) arrive constantly.

> "an idea as to what new content is going to come up"

**How to do it**

1. Every day, scan incoming reactive-PR pitch requests (HARO/Connectively, Qwoted, etc.) even for topics you won't personally respond to.
2. Read each request as a signal of what a journalist is actively researching and will likely publish soon, not only as a citation opportunity.
3. Cross-reference the upcoming topics against your own data/expertise to spot where you could create original content on the same or an adjacent angle.
4. Decide whether to match the exact angle the journalist is fishing for, or offer a related but different angle they haven't explicitly requested.
5. Build and pitch that original content proactively through your standard active-PR process while the topic is still current in the news cycle.
6. Repeat this daily rather than treating it as a one-time exercise, since new requests keep surfacing new topic intelligence.

**Tools:** HARO/Connectively, Qwoted

**Pitfall:** Treating reactive-PR requests only as citation opportunities and missing their value as free, forward-looking topic research for your own proactive campaigns.

### 2. Negotiate sponsorships for newsletter and social placement, not just a link  `23.7`
*core · best practices · source 23*

At the ranking-site stage, with a real marketing budget available, Edward reframes sponsorships as a link-building upgrade: instead of settling for a single dofollow backlink from whatever you sponsor, negotiate explicitly for placement in the sponsored entity's newsletter and social media posts as part of the deal. The goal he states directly is to make the sponsorship generate referral traffic, branded searches, and top-of-mind brand awareness, not just a link-equity signal, because at this stage Google is watching for whether a site has the real-world marketing signals an actual business would have rather than looking like a site optimized purely for links.

> "Don't just try to get the dofollow backlink from the event"

**How to do it**

1. Once you have a marketing budget, identify sponsorship opportunities at events, newsletters, or communities relevant to your niche.
2. Before finalizing any sponsorship deal, explicitly request placement in the sponsored entity's email newsletter, not only a website/backlink mention.
3. Also explicitly request a social media post or mention from the sponsored entity's own accounts as part of the sponsorship terms.
4. Treat a plain dofollow backlink as the minimum acceptable outcome of the negotiation, not the goal.
5. After the sponsorship runs, track branded search volume and direct/referral traffic in the following weeks to assess whether it drove real brand awareness, not just a link (inferred verification step).
6. Pursue repeat or expanded sponsorships with the same outlet if the awareness/traffic results were strong (inferred).

**Pitfall:** Settling for just the dofollow backlink from a sponsorship instead of negotiating for newsletter and social placement — Edward explicitly warns not to just get the dofollow backlink, since Google penalizes sites that only show link signals without real branded-traffic and business signals.

### 3. Newsjack into unsolidified SERPs, then stack the wins  `56.13`
*core · concrete actions · source 56*

Newsjacking is one of two things Cody says he is most interested in right now. The mechanic he describes: identify the topic relevant to your category that is happening right now, collect everything you can find about it, write a good article, and promote it socially as 'this is happening, here's everything I learned, full article here'. Because the SERPs for a breaking topic aren't solidified yet - no established page is already ranking - a good article can reach page one. Traffic spikes, then finds its equilibrium, but the pieces can be produced fast and stacked. He reports strong click-through rates on them. His most valuable framing is the combination: ride the news cycle for social traffic and also get the article ranking for the keyword, which he says is far more valuable than the SEO half alone - and the reason people don't do it is simply that it's more work. Edward's examples are a COVID stats page launched on Product Hunt with a work-from-home tool CTA at the bottom, and a submarine-disaster TikTok that pushed 700,000 views to a podcast episode.

> "what's the topic that's relevant"

**How to do it**

1. Monitor for breaking topics inside your category rather than general news - relevance to your product is what makes the traffic worth having.
2. Collect everything findable on the topic quickly; speed matters more than depth because the SERP is about to solidify.
3. Write a genuinely good article on it, targeting the keyword phrase people will search.
4. Publish and immediately promote it on social as a first-person 'here's everything I learned' post linking to the full article.
5. Add a relevant product CTA at the bottom of the page - Edward's COVID-stats page asked 'are you working from home? try our product'.
6. Expect a traffic spike that settles to a lower equilibrium, and judge the piece on the equilibrium plus the links it earned.
7. Repeat and stack - the value is in producing these fast and often, not in one hit.

**Pitfall:** Newsjacking a topic unrelated to your product buys you a traffic spike with no commercial value and content that then needs maintaining. Both hosts tie the value directly to category relevance.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau has an obvious newsjacking lane - regulatory changes, insurer and payer policy shifts, new treatment approvals - where the SERP is genuinely unsolidified and a fast, accurate article can take page one before the trade press consolidates.

**Apply anywhere:** Most categories have an obvious newsjacking lane - regulatory changes, policy shifts, new approvals - where the SERP is genuinely unsolidified and a fast, accurate article can take page one before the trade press consolidates.

### 4. Pick the influencer by asking customers who they trust for advice  `172.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 172*

Benji Hyam of Grow and Convert says step one of an influencer campaign is choosing the right target, and that choice comes from user research rather than follower counts. He recommends a phone or email survey of existing customers asking who they trust most for advice about the industry. The point is overlap: you want a person whose audience is made of your buyers, not the biggest account in the niche. In the case study, songwriter Evan Blum targeted pop star Camila Cabello because her fanbase is pop music lovers and his goal was to become a pop songwriter. Hyam is explicit that this works for B2B too, and that he has run it at multiple companies. Do the research before you create anything, because the content itself has to be built around the person you pick.

> "who do you trust most for advice about X industry?"

**Evidence:** Evan Blum's target, Camila Cabello, had 3.44M Twitter followers made up of pop music fans, the exact audience he needed as an aspiring pop songwriter.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the exact buyer you want more of, in one sentence.
2. Email or call 10 to 20 existing customers and ask: who do you trust most for advice about this industry?
3. Tally the names that come back more than once and drop anyone named only by a single customer.
4. For each repeat name, check whether their audience is your buyers rather than peers or press.
5. Cross-check the shortlist in SparkToro for audience overlap with your own site's visitors.
6. Pick one target per campaign, not a list, because the content has to be built around that person.
7. Record the target's recurring details, phrases and public interests to use later in the asset.

**Tools:** SparkToro

**Pitfall:** Choosing by follower count instead of audience match. A huge account whose followers are not your buyers sends a spike of traffic that converts at zero and never repeats.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau commissions any influencer or partner-led piece, David should survey a dozen clinic owners on who they follow for practice-management and aesthetics business advice, and build the shortlist from those names rather than from Instagram follower counts.

**Apply anywhere:** Choose influencer targets by surveying your own customers on who they trust for industry advice, then verify that the named accounts have an audience of buyers rather than peers. Pick one target per campaign and build the asset around that person.

### 5. Pitch a co-produced guide to every vendor in an adjacent category at once  `173.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 173*

After the conference party worked, Dane generalized it into co-produced content with non-competitive brands serving the same audience. His method was to pick a topic where an adjacent product category has an obvious stake, then approach the entire category rather than one company. For a guide on getting phone leads from PPC, he contacted every call tracking provider he could find, not expecting them all to agree. They all said yes, which left KlientBoost with the problem of turning companies down. They published their own phone leads guide first, then produced a retargeting guide with Invoca. The mechanism is that the partner distributes to their list, so a company with almost no traffic reaches a large qualified audience. Grow and Convert draw the wider lesson: when a new strategy shows signs of working, go all in rather than testing it once.

> "we reached to every single call tracking provider"

**Evidence:** Every call tracking provider approached said yes; the resulting Invoca retargeting guide became part of a full resources page of co-produced webinars and ebooks.

**How to do it**

1. Pick a topic where your service and an adjacent product category both benefit from the reader acting.
2. List every vendor in that adjacent category, not just the market leader.
3. Email all of them the same week with a one-paragraph pitch naming the guide topic and who it serves.
4. Offer to do the writing and the design so the partner's only cost is distribution and a review pass.
5. Agree in writing who emails the asset to which list, and on what date.
6. Pick the partner with the best-matched list, not the biggest brand, and decline the rest politely.
7. Publish your own version of the guide first so you own an ungated asset regardless of the partnership.
8. Repeat with webinars once the ebook has run, using the same partner relationships.

**Pitfall:** Approaching one partner at a time stretches a two-week play into six months, and a single 'no' reads as the tactic failing. Dane's surprise was that the entire category accepted.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's adjacent categories are aesthetic device makers, injectable distributors, payment processors and marketing agencies serving clinics. David should pitch a co-produced guide, on clinic patient retention for example, to every vendor in one of those categories in the same week, and publish Pabau's own version regardless of who signs.

**Apply anywhere:** Find an adjacent, non-competitive product category whose buyers are your buyers, pitch a co-produced guide to every vendor in it simultaneously, and let the best-matched list win. Publish your own edition first so you keep the asset either way.

### 6. Pitch an interview by leading with your audience makeup and traffic  `172.4`
*core · concrete actions · source 172*

Benji Hyam reached out to Chris Messina over Twitter to ask for an interview. He says the key part of the pitch was telling Messina who the audience was: product managers, startup founders and developers from the Valley. He also cited traffic numbers and previous interviews the site had run. Messina agreed and the conversation moved to email to coordinate logistics. The interview produced enough material for a two-part series, and Messina shared both parts with his Twitter following, then about 70,000 and later over 95,000. The stories picked up more traffic after being posted to a subreddit, and each article drove thousands of visitors. The mechanism is that an interview is a low-effort ask for the subject and gives them distribution, so the pitch only has to prove the audience is worth their hour.

> "letting him know about our audience"

**Evidence:** Chris Messina agreed after a Twitter pitch citing audience makeup and traffic, shared both parts with roughly 70,000 followers, and each article drove thousands of visitors.

**How to do it**

1. Pick a target whose followers match your buyers and who is publicly active on a channel you can reach, such as Twitter or LinkedIn.
2. Write the pitch with three elements: who your audience is in concrete job titles, your traffic numbers, and two previous interviews you have published.
3. Send it on the target's most active public channel rather than to a generic contact form.
4. Move to email as soon as they agree, to coordinate scheduling and questions.
5. Prepare questions from their recent work so the conversation produces material that does not already exist online.
6. Split a long interview into a two-part series so you get two publishing moments and two share prompts.
7. Send each published part to the subject on the day it goes live, ready to share.
8. Post the pieces to the subreddits where that audience already gathers to extend the traffic beyond the subject's own following.

**Pitfall:** Pitching with flattery and no numbers. Busy interviewees skip requests that do not say who will read it, because an hour of their time has to buy distribution they do not already have.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau pitches an interview to an aesthetics industry figure, the email should state the clinic-owner and practice-manager readership, the blog's monthly sessions, and two prior interviews, rather than opening with praise.

**Apply anywhere:** Pitch interviews by stating your audience in concrete job titles, your traffic numbers, and two previous interviews you have run. Move to email once they agree, split long interviews into a two-part series, and post each part where that audience already gathers.

### 7. Pitch every journalist who covered the topic before you publish  `123.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 123*

The promotion half of Hyam's ThinkApps launch was a single targeted list. Before the Apple Watch concept-art post went live, he reached out to every journalist who had previously covered the Apple Watch and pitched the piece to them. The morning after publication 9to5Mac picked it up, and from that one pickup the article was syndicated to over 200 tech and design publications. That produced over 200 high quality linking domains and over 20,000 unique visitors inside a week. The mechanism worth copying is the prospecting rule: prior coverage of the exact subject, not general beat relevance, is what qualifies a journalist. The pitch also went out before publication so the piece was news rather than an archive item.

> "we reached out to every journalist who had covered the Apple Watch previously"

**Evidence:** 9to5Mac picked up the piece the morning after publication; syndication to over 200 tech and design publications gave over 200 high quality backlinks and over 20,000 unique visitors within a week.

**How to do it**

1. Search news coverage of your exact topic or news event and build a list of every journalist who has already written about it.
2. Add the publication, the article they wrote and its date to each row so the pitch can reference their own piece.
3. Prioritize outlets with syndication reach, since one pickup by a hub like 9to5Mac cascaded into 200+ republications here.
4. Send the pitch before the post is published, offering the piece as news rather than as an existing URL.
5. Give each journalist the visual assets separately so they can publish without asking, since a design-led piece is what they will run.
6. Follow up the morning of publication with the live link to whoever did not reply.
7. Track referring domains for the week after launch, not just day one, because syndication arrives after the first pickup.

**Pitfall:** Pitching a general beat list instead of writers with prior coverage of the specific subject. Response rates collapse, and you learn it when a launch piece gets no pickup at all and the syndication cascade never starts.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau's next original-data piece, David should build the press list from journalists who have already covered aesthetics industry regulation or medspa business news, and pitch them the day before the post publishes rather than after.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your press list from journalists who have already covered your specific subject, not from a general beat list, and pitch them a day before you publish so the piece lands as news.

### 8. Pitch writers who already covered the exact topic, one email each  `172.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 172*

Benji Hyam created a piece about the Apple Watch for ThinkApps, then found industry writers who had previously written about the Apple Watch and sent each a personal email pitching the article. The story was picked up by 9to5Mac and, through syndication, around 200 other publications. That syndication drove 20,000 unique visitors in a week and is what launched the ThinkApps blog. The selection rule is the useful part: prior coverage of the same subject proves the beat, proves interest, and gives you a specific reference point for the email. Hyam pairs this with the same rule he gives Evan Blum, which is to tailor each pitch to what that publication's audience would care about rather than sending one generic note to a list.

> "I identified industry writers who had written about"

**Evidence:** Hyam's Apple Watch piece was picked up by 9to5Mac and around 200 other publications, driving 20,000 unique visitors in a week and launching the ThinkApps blog.

**How to do it**

1. Search Google News and the publication archives for articles on your exact topic from the last 12 months.
2. Record the writer's name, publication, article URL and the angle they took in a sheet.
3. Cut anyone who covered the topic once in passing and keep those with two or more pieces on it.
4. Write each email individually, opening with the specific article of theirs you read and why your piece follows from it.
5. State the angle you are offering in one sentence, framed for that publication's readers, not yours.
6. Attach or link the finished asset so there is nothing for them to commission.
7. Send from a real named person at your company, not a marketing alias.
8. Track opens and resend non-openers after three to four days with a different subject line.
9. Once one outlet runs it, pitch the remaining list again citing that pickup.

**Pitfall:** Blasting one identical pitch to a scraped media list. Writers on the wrong beat ignore it, and a generic note to the right writer reads as spam, which burns the contact for later.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, this means pitching aesthetics and healthcare trade writers who have already covered clinic technology or regulation, one tailored email each, rather than adding a generic press page and waiting.

**Apply anywhere:** Build your pitch list from writers who have already published on your exact topic at least twice, then send each an individual email that opens with their own article and offers an angle framed for their readers. Re-pitch the list once the first outlet runs it.

### 9. Point your paid links at the link magnet, not the money page  `57.6`
*core · concrete actions · source 57*

Cody describes the structural pattern his team uses: for a client, they build statistics pages related to the software - long-form content on low-difficulty but bottom-of-funnel terms - and then all of their niche edits and link building point at those statistics and link-magnet pages rather than at the money pages directly. The digital PR asset becomes the link target, and internal linking distributes the authority from there. Combined with journalist outreach, the same page absorbs both the paid links and the earned ones. Jackie's addition is that the amplification is cheap: you can cold email every journalist covering that geography or beat, and being able to say 'our article is trending on Reddit right now' makes them far more likely to pick it up, turning 10-15 links into 50.

> "all of our link building is basically to these statistics"

**How to do it**

1. Build a small set of statistics or data pages on genuinely relevant low-difficulty terms in your category.
2. Make them the destination for your paid placements and niche edits rather than pointing those at commercial pages.
3. Internally link from the statistics pages to the commercial pages they support.
4. Run journalist outreach at the same pages so earned and paid links land in the same place.
5. Use social traction as the hook in the outreach - a piece that is already trending is easier for a journalist to justify.
6. Scrape journalist contact details for the relevant beat, and use their social profiles as a secondary channel.

**Pitfall:** Paid placements and niche edits are outside Google's guidelines regardless of which page they point at - the structural insight (concentrate links on a small number of link-worthy assets and distribute internally) is what transfers.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should concentrate its earned links on a handful of genuinely citable statistics and research pages, then internally link from those to the feature and template pages that need the authority - the concentration is the transferable half of this.

**Apply anywhere:** Concentrate earned links on a handful of genuinely citable statistics and research pages, then internally link from those to the commercial pages that need the authority - the concentration is the transferable half of this.

### 12. Resend unopened outreach after 3-4 days with a new subject line  `162.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 162*

Peralta splits follow-up into two branches based on open tracking, and this is the no-open branch. If the expert never opened the email at all, tracked in Mixmax, he sends the same email again after 3-4 days under a different subject line. He alternates between two: 'Open for an interview?' and 'Putting together a story about THEIR COMPANY NAME'. His reasoning is that a busy inbox buries the first message and a second subject line is a second chance to be seen, not a nag. He notes this is exactly how he got the CEO of Buffer to open his email; the first one was missed and the second was opened. The branch only works if you have open tracking, since sending a reminder to someone who already read the email needs different wording.

> "I send it again after 3-4 days using a new subject line"

**Evidence:** Peralta credits this exact resend with getting the CEO of Buffer to open his email after missing the first send.

**How to do it**

1. Install open tracking, such as Mixmax, before sending the first outreach email.
2. Check open status 3-4 days after sending.
3. If the email was never opened, resend the same body under a new subject line.
4. Alternate between a short question subject like 'Open for an interview?' and a specific one like 'Putting together a story about [Company Name]'.
5. Do not add pressure or reference the previous email, since they never saw it.
6. If the second send is also unopened after 3-4 days, move to the insider step rather than a third resend.

**Tools:** Mixmax

**Pitfall:** Sending an 'as per my last email' reminder to someone who never opened the first one. It reads as pushy about a message they cannot remember and burns the prospect.

**Apply at Pabau:** Whoever runs Pabau's expert outreach needs open tracking on the mailbox, so the follow-up wording branches correctly between clinicians who never saw the email and those who read it and did not reply.

**Apply anywhere:** Track opens, and when an outreach email is never opened, resend the identical body after 3-4 days under a different subject line rather than writing a reminder.

### 13. Route the share through a fan or mutual rather than pitching cold  `172.5`
*core · concrete actions · source 172*

The pivotal moment in the case study was not a pitch. A fan found Evan Blum's song, shared it in a Portuguese Facebook group full of Camila Cabello fans, and one of those people tweeted it at her saying the song was named after her. She retweeted it to 3.4M followers. Benji Hyam's read is that the fan's framing did the work, and that a company can engineer this deliberately. His advice is to identify someone who already knows the influencer and have that person share it, so the introduction arrives warm rather than as another request in the inbox. This runs before or instead of a direct pitch, and it only works once the asset itself is built around the influencer, so the messenger has something genuine to say.

> "identify someone who knows the influencer and have them share it"

**Evidence:** A fan tweeted the song at Camila Cabello with the line that it was named after her, and she retweeted it to 3.4M followers, driving nearly 40,000 views in a day and a half.

**How to do it**

1. Finish the asset first so the messenger has something worth passing on.
2. List people who interact publicly with the target: replies, co-authors, podcast guests, employees, and active fan communities.
3. Check your own team's LinkedIn and Twitter connections for anyone one hop from the target.
4. Pick two or three warm intermediaries rather than one, so a non-response does not stall the campaign.
5. Send each a short personal note with the asset and a one-line reason the target specifically would like it.
6. Ask them to pass it on in their own words rather than supplying copy to paste.
7. If no warm route exists, seed the asset in the communities where the target's fans gather and let it travel from there.
8. Only cold-pitch the target directly after the warm routes have been tried.

**Pitfall:** Asking an intermediary before the asset exists, or handing them scripted copy. The message reads as marketing, the intermediary declines, and you have burned the one warm route you had.

**Apply at Pabau:** For Pabau, this means mapping which aesthetics trainers and clinic influencers already interact with a target account, and asking a Pabau customer who follows them to pass on the piece rather than sending a cold DM from the brand account.

**Apply anywhere:** Before cold-pitching an influencer, find someone who already interacts with them and ask that person to pass the finished asset on in their own words. Warm introductions get shared far more often than brand outreach.

### 14. Run a seven-step expert outreach sequence before giving up on a target  `162.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 162*

David Peralta, a content strategist at Grow and Convert, reports a 60% positive response rate from experts using a fixed seven-step sequence rather than one-off emails. The sequence is: define the audience's pain points to decide who to interview, find contact details, research a unique angle, tweet first, email or InMail second, follow up on non-opens with a new subject line, follow up on opens-without-replies, and finally ask an insider such as the company's content marketer to broker the interview. Each step has a wait built in, so a single prospect can run three weeks before it is dead. Peralta says he started landing interviews with founders and CEOs within three days of first trying, and that fear of rejection, not difficulty, was what had stopped him.

> "a simple, 7-step outreach process"

**Evidence:** Peralta reports a 60% positive response rate across experts contacted using this process, including CEOs of Hotjar, Buffer, Drift and Amplitude Analytics.

**How to do it**

1. Define your audience's specific pain points and name the kind of person who has already solved them.
2. Build a target list in a spreadsheet with name, company, Twitter handle and email columns.
3. Research a unique angle for each target before writing any message.
4. Send a tweet containing the angle if the target posts at least every few days.
5. Wait 3-4 days, then send an email or LinkedIn InMail leading with the same angle.
6. If the email is never opened, resend after 3-4 days with a different subject line.
7. If it was opened but ignored, wait 4-5 days and send a short polite reminder.
8. If still silent, email the company's content marketer or PR lead and ask them to set up the interview.
9. Log the outcome of every step in the spreadsheet so you know which stage each prospect sits at.

**Tools:** Mixmax, Voila Norbert, Email Hunter

**Pitfall:** Most people stop after the first unanswered email, which throws away the four steps that produce the interviews. The signal you have hit this is a response rate under 20% with no follow-up column in your tracker.
