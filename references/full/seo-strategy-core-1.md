### 1. "If it feels spammy, it probably is spammy" — skip the shortcut  `42.9`
*core · best practices · source 42*

The standing rule offered for every tactical decision in this playbook is to avoid any shortcut that either obviously violates Google's guidelines or simply "feels spammy" to the person doing it. The reasoning given is blunt: if it feels spammy, it probably is spammy, and taking the shortcut puts the entire compounding, long-term promise of SEO at risk. The framing is explicitly a long-game trade-off: a short-term gain from a spammy tactic is weighed against jeopardizing the years-long compounding authority and traffic being built through the foundational, referral-traffic, and satisfied-searcher approach described throughout the rest of the episode.

> "If it feels spammy, it probably is spammy"

**How to do it**

1. Before executing any new SEO or link-building tactic, explicitly ask whether it would obviously violate Google's published webmaster guidelines.
2. Separately ask a gut-check question: does this tactic feel spammy to me, regardless of whether I can point to a specific rule it breaks?
3. If the answer to either question is yes, do not execute the tactic, even if it appears to offer a fast short-term gain.
4. Document tactics that were rejected on this basis so the team has a consistent, shared bar for future decisions rather than re-litigating it case by case (inferred documentation practice).
5. Periodically revisit the list of avoided tactics as guidelines and norms evolve, since what "feels spammy" can shift over time (inferred review cadence).

**Pitfall:** Shortcuts that go against Google's rules or feel spammy risk the entire long-term compounding promise of SEO for a short-term gain — the whole strategy depends on playing the long game without cutting corners.

### 2. A new site's first 10 SEO hours: one audience, one path, one measurable gate  `29.e3`
*core · concrete actions · source 29 · universal-edition only*

Asked how to spend just 10 hours in a month on a brand-new site, Azarian rejects breadth entirely. The whole budget goes to narrowing to a single audience — his observation is that people arrive wanting to address three or four at once — and building one path that takes that person from a specific pain point the product addresses, through the solution, to a measurable value event: a sale, a sign-up, an email, something that functions as a gate. He specifies the scope as a one-to-two-page funnel, and says that if you can do that in 10 hours it is a good starting point. He advises starting on a template or platform that already handles basic sitemap and architecture plumbing so the hours go into the flow rather than the setup, then treating that first content flow as the anchor to measure everything against — using something like PickFu to test variations on which framing actually addresses the pain point, and only then expanding the keyword universe to a second pain point or solution.

> "one clean journey for one core user, and how your product solves their problem, in a one-to-two-page funnel"

**How to do it**

1. Force the audience list down to exactly one before writing anything.
2. Name the specific pain point of that audience which your product addresses.
3. Choose the measurable gate up front: a sale, sign-up, or email capture.
4. Build the path as a one-to-two-page funnel from pain point to solution to gate.
5. Start from a template or platform that already handles sitemaps and basic architecture, so the hours go into the flow.
6. Treat that flow as the measurement anchor for everything you add later.
7. Run variation tests on the framing to see which actually addresses the pain point.
8. Only after that, expand the keyword universe to a second pain point or solution.

**Tools:** PickFu, Google Analytics, Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Spreading the first 10 hours across three or four audiences — the entire value of the exercise is having one clean journey you can actually measure against.

### 3. Ask an SEO candidate what their strategy is optimized to achieve  `163.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 163*

Grow and Convert call this the single most important hiring question, because it decides whether the budget produces ROI. Most SEO professionals run traffic-focused keyword strategies on the assumption that conversions follow volume. Their experience across dozens of clients is that more search traffic does not mean more conversions. They allow one exception: large incumbents already ranking for most of their high-buying-intent keywords can reasonably chase traffic next. Everyone else, especially budget-constrained companies with revenue targets, should hire someone whose strategy is optimized for conversions first. The practical move is to ask the question plainly and listen for whether the answer is expressed in sessions and rankings or in leads and sales. A candidate who cannot state the goal metric of their own methodology has not thought about it.

> "more search traffic does not mean more conversions"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert cite their Pain-Point SEO and Geekbot scaling articles as the conversion-rate data behind the claim, drawn from years of agency client work.

**How to do it**

1. Ask every candidate directly: what is your SEO or content strategy optimized to achieve?
2. Listen for the unit of the answer: sessions and keyword counts, or leads, demos and sales.
3. Follow up by asking which keywords they would target first for your business and why those specific ones.
4. Reject the answer that traffic comes first and conversions follow later, unless you already rank for your buying-intent terms.
5. Ask them to show one past client where the reported outcome was leads or revenue, not pageviews.
6. Agree the goal metric in writing before the contract starts, and make the monthly report lead with it.

**Pitfall:** Both sides nod at conversions in the pitch and then report on traffic from month one. The signal is a first monthly report whose headline chart is sessions.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's SEO reporting should lead with demo requests attributed to organic landing pages, not sessions. Any vendor or contractor David briefs gets the goal metric stated in the scope before work starts.

**Apply anywhere:** Make your reporting lead with the commercial action, not sessions, and state that goal metric in any vendor scope before work starts.

### 4. Ask an agency for the time split between technical, links and content  `179.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 179*

Grow and Convert say most agencies put the majority of their effort into on-page work, technical SEO and link building, and much less into identifying high-value keywords and building pages to rank for them. They call this backwards, because technical health and white-hat backlinks generate no revenue on their own; ranking for valuable keywords is where SEO revenue comes from. Their vetting move is to force the agency to quantify the split rather than describe it. Ask how much weight they put on each of the three facets, what portion of time and effort each takes, and to walk through a typical client month. Heavy or equal emphasis on technical and links is a red flag. So is an agency that talks fluently about technical audits but cannot articulate a keyword and content strategy.

> "What portion of their time and effort do they spend on each?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report clients arriving frustrated after budgets went into cleaning up sites, fixing broken links, adjusting canonicals, schema and site speed, with no measurable increase in trials, demos or calls.

**How to do it**

1. Ask the agency to assign a percentage of monthly hours to technical SEO, link building, and keyword strategy plus content, totaling 100.
2. Ask them to walk through a typical month for a current client, naming the deliverables in each bucket.
3. Cross-check the percentages against the deliverable list; a 60% content claim with two articles a month does not reconcile.
4. Treat technical and links weighted heavily or as equal to content as a red flag.
5. Ask them to state their keyword and content strategy in two sentences without using the word audit.
6. Ask which technical fixes are one-time and which recur, since most are one-time and should not anchor an ongoing retainer.
7. Ask how technical work and link building will be pointed at the specific pages targeting your priority keywords.

**Prompt / template:**

```text
How much weight do you place on technical SEO versus link building versus keyword strategy and content? What portion of your time goes to each? Can you walk me through a typical month of work for a client?
```

**Pitfall:** Letting the agency answer that all three matter equally. Equal weighting is the failure mode itself, and it usually means the retainer will be filled with recurring technical maintenance because that work is easy to bill and easy to report.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau scopes any outside SEO work, the statement of work should name the share of hours going to keyword selection and page production, and cap recurring technical maintenance. Pabau's technical debt on pabau.com is largely one-time work.

**Apply anywhere:** Make any prospective agency put a percentage on how its monthly hours split between technical SEO, link building, and keyword strategy plus content, then reconcile those percentages against the actual deliverables in a typical client month. Heavy or equal weighting on technical and links is a warning: neither produces revenue without pages that rank for keywords worth ranking for.

### 5. Ask how many pre-breakeven months you can fund, and budget 6 to 12  `129.10`
*core · concrete actions · source 129*

Grow and Convert put the breakeven window at 6 to 12 months. That is how long it takes, in their experience, to amass enough top rankings for a client to reach the conversion volume needed to cover monthly spend. They convert that into a direct affordability question rather than a projection: can you fund the monthly cost for, say, ten months before positive ROI starts? If not, SEO may not be a reasonable channel to invest in right now, or you need a cheaper way to test it. If you can, the later upside is large. The important part is the framing. This is a cash-runway question, separate from the value question, and a business can pass the value test and still fail the runway test.

> "it takes between 6-12 months to amass enough top search rankings"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report 6 to 12 months to reach the ranking volume needed for clients to break even on monthly spend.

**How to do it**

1. Take your fixed monthly SEO cost from the cost estimate.
2. Multiply it by 10 to get the pre-breakeven cash requirement, using the middle of the 6 to 12 month window.
3. Check that amount against actual available marketing cash, not against a hoped-for budget.
4. If it does not fit, do not start the full program.
5. In that case, test the channel cheaply instead: five to ten articles on the highest-intent keywords.
6. If it does fit, commit the full window in the budget so the program is not cancelled at month four.
7. Set the breakeven review at month twelve and state up front that earlier reviews are progress checks, not verdicts.

**Tools:** Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel)

**Pitfall:** Funding the program month to month. A business that can afford $10,000 in month one but not in month eight will cancel during the deficit and lose the entire investment, since the rankings were the asset being paid for.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau commissions a new page family, commit the twelve-month production budget at the start, including visuals and refreshes. A family half-built and abandoned at month five produces neither rankings nor leads.

**Apply anywhere:** Treat SEO funding as a runway question. Breakeven typically lands between months 6 and 12, so multiply your fixed monthly cost by about ten and check that against real available cash. If it does not fit, test the channel with five to ten high-intent articles instead of launching a full program you will cancel mid-deficit.

### 6. Assess click value, citation value and business value independently, then check they align  `66.2`
*core · best practices · source 66*

The framework's central process rule is that the three kinds of value a page can return must be judged separately before they are judged together. Every content type has a different primary role and doesn't need to generate clicks, citations and conversions at the same level: a live status page earns clicks but few citations, an original research study earns citations and brand mentions but converts little, a checkout page converts but is never cited. Collapsing them into one 'performance' number is what makes teams kill a page that was doing its actual job. The check is not that every page scores well on all three, but that across the site the three are aligned to provide the expected value to the brand — so that the citation-earning pages, the click-earning pages and the converting pages exist in deliberate proportion rather than by accident.

> "fundamental to assess click/traffic value, citation/mention value and business value independently"

**How to do it**

1. Tag every content pattern on the site with its one primary role: earn clicks, earn citations and mentions, or drive a business outcome. Force a single choice per pattern.
2. Measure each role with its own metric: clicks and impressions from Search Console for the click role, AI-answer and third-party citations for the citation role, and conversions or support resolutions for the business role.
3. Judge each pattern only against its own role's metric. Do not fault a research study for low conversions or a checkout page for zero citations.
4. Then step back to the site level and check the proportions: whether enough patterns carry the citation role to build entity association, and enough carry the business role to convert the demand that association creates.
5. Where a role is thin across the whole site, commission for that role specifically rather than commissioning more of whatever already ranks.
6. Flag any pattern that scores low on all three roles at once — that is the genuine deprioritization candidate, and the only case where a single verdict is safe.

**Tools:** Google Search Console, Google Analytics

**Pitfall:** Holding every page to the same KPI. A page judged on the wrong role gets cut for failing a job it was never built to do — and citation-earning pages, whose whole return is indirect, are the first casualties of a clicks-only review.

**Apply at Pabau:** Give each Pabau content pattern one role and one metric: template pages and feature docs are business-value pages, original research and industry benchmarks are citation pages, and the blog's how-to library is a click page. Review each against its own role, then check the mix is deliberate rather than all-blog.

**Apply anywhere:** Give each content pattern one role and one metric: product and template pages are business-value pages, original research and industry benchmarks are citation pages, and the how-to library is a click page. Review each against its own role, then check the mix is deliberate rather than all-blog.

### 7. Audit every marketing subscription against three process questions, then cancel  `175.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 175*

Grow and Convert closes the piece with a specific audit anyone can run this quarter. List every piece of software you pay for, then put three questions to each one: does it improve a process, does it improve the speed at which you can do something, and does it organize information better than you could manually. A tool that cannot answer yes to at least one is not moving the needle and should be cancelled. The point is not frugality for its own sake. The money freed up is redirected into improving the craft, which is where the author says results actually come from. The trigger for the audit is any tool bought to replace or improve a function rather than to speed one up.

> "How many of them improve a process?"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert states plainly that there are a lot of tools people pay for that are not moving the needle for them or their business.

**How to do it**

1. Export the last 12 months of card and invoice charges and list every marketing or SEO subscription with its monthly cost.
2. For each tool write down the exact process it was bought to serve, in one sentence.
3. Ask question one: does it improve that process, or was it bought to create one that does not exist yet.
4. Ask question two: does it make an existing task measurably faster, and note the hours saved per month.
5. Ask question three: does it organize information better than a spreadsheet you would otherwise maintain.
6. Mark any tool that fails all three as a cancellation candidate.
7. Check seat counts and last-login dates on the survivors and cut unused seats.
8. Cancel the failures, and reassign the recovered budget to training, a course, a mentor or paid practice.
9. Re-run the audit each renewal quarter so lapsed tools do not accumulate again.

**Tools:** Zapier, IFTTT, HubSpot, Marketo, Pardot

**Pitfall:** Teams keep tools because cancelling feels like admitting the purchase was a mistake. The signal you have hit it is a tool nobody has logged into for a month that still renews.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should run this audit across Pabau's own marketing stack once a quarter and redirect the savings into writer coaching and original research, which is what moves the blog. Note the irony to handle carefully: Pabau sells software, so its blog should never imply tools alone fix a practice's problems.

**Apply anywhere:** Run this audit across your marketing stack each renewal quarter. Cancel anything that fails all three questions and put the savings into skills, research or better writers rather than another subscription.

### 8. Audit messaging against positioning with four direct questions  `159.12`
*core · concrete actions · source 159*

Grow and Convert's final step separates two things people conflate. Positioning is how you want the product perceived in the market. Messaging is what you actually communicate to make people believe it. They say the mismatch is common in client work: clients tell them they want super specific leads while their messaging is entirely generic, which on the wide-narrow chart means the positioning sits narrow and the messaging sits wide. Their example is a development agency that wants consumer mobile app design projects and runs a website that effectively says 'we'll develop anything'. They note people laugh at that example and it is extremely common. The audit is four questions run over the whole site after the positioning work is done, not before.

> "Positioning and messaging are _not_ the same"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report seeing this mismatch repeatedly across clients, describing the wanted-specific-leads and generic-messaging combination as extremely common.

**How to do it**

1. Finish the positioning work first: target customer, chosen pain point, uniqueness, proof.
2. Write the four conclusions on one page as the reference document.
3. Comb through every page of live messaging: homepage, product pages, ads, sales decks, email sequences.
4. Ask of each: do we want these specific customers, and does this page's language actually address them?
5. Ask: we help solve this critical pain point, do we say that anywhere on this page?
6. Ask: we are unique in this way, is that obvious from this page or only to someone who already knows us?
7. Ask: we have proof to back it up, can a prospect find and state that proof without asking us?
8. Fix every page that fails a question, starting with the pages carrying the most traffic.
9. Repeat the audit whenever the positioning changes, since old messaging survives long after a repositioning.

**Pitfall:** Repositioning in a strategy document while the site keeps the old generic messaging. The result is expensive leads that do not match the segment the company decided to serve.

**Apply at Pabau:** After any Pabau positioning decision, David should audit the blog, template pages and product pages against these four questions rather than only the homepage, since organic entry points carry most first impressions.

**Apply anywhere:** After deciding positioning, audit every page against four questions: do we address these customers, do we name this pain point, is the uniqueness obvious, and can a prospect find the proof.

### 9. Bottom and middle-funnel content survives AI extraction; top doesn't  `28.2`
*core · content insights · source 28*

The single lever that reversed the traffic decline was shifting investment away from top-of-funnel/informational content (e.g., 'how to sign hello') toward middle- and bottom-of-funnel content (e.g., 'Does Duolingo have ASL?'), because top-of-funnel content is, in Endre's words, the easiest thing for AI to just regurgitate — a short factual answer that AI Overviews can lift wholesale, after which the user has no reason to click through. Bottom-of-funnel content, by contrast, poses comparison and evaluation questions an AI answer can't fully resolve without the user visiting the actual page, so it kept driving both traffic and app-download conversions even as the informational layer was cannibalized.

> "the easiest thing for AI to just regurgitate"

**Evidence:** Endre's article 'Does Duolingo Teach ASL?' became the highest-performing article in the US specifically because it and similar bottom-of-funnel pieces, such as an alphabet-in-sign-language article, were the actual measured drivers of app-download recovery, contrasted directly against the informational content that got stolen by AI Overviews.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should audit its funnel distribution and deliberately weight new content investment toward comparison, evaluation, and decision-stage topics, such as integration or versus-competitor questions, rather than pure how-to or definitional content, since the latter is the content type most exposed to full AI-answer extraction with zero click-through.

**Apply anywhere:** You should audit its funnel distribution and deliberately weight new content investment toward comparison, evaluation, and decision-stage topics, such as integration or versus-competitor questions, rather than pure how-to or definitional content, since the latter is the content type most exposed to full AI-answer extraction with zero click-through.

### 10. Brand and traffic beat technical SEO on Get Your Tips Out  `18.1`
*core · content insights · source 18*

The 'Get Your Tips Out' horse-racing/betting tips site had an unnatural links penalty flagged in Google Search Console, roughly 4,500 404s and stray 301 redirects when crawled in Screaming Frog, daily 'here are Monday's tips' content with no topical authority and no internal linking — by the classic four-pillars standard (technical SEO, content, topical authority, backlinks) it failed on every count. Yet one article on the site kept appearing in Google News and jumped to position 2 for 'best horse racing betting sites in the UK' (a 20,000-searches/month keyword), driven by branded search and Twitter referral traffic rather than any of the four pillars. The speaker (James Dooley) says this case is what convinced him to invest in the business and reshaped his view that brand and traffic recognition can outweigh conventional technical/content/authority signals. For a site chasing competitive terms, this suggests newsworthy content plus brand-search volume can be a faster path to a strong ranking than incremental technical or on-page fixes.

> "it jumped to position number two for"

**Evidence:** Get Your Tips Out ranked #2 for 'best horse racing betting sites in the UK' (a 20,000/month keyword) despite an unnatural links penalty in GSC and roughly 4,500 404s found in a Screaming Frog crawl, because the site had Google News pickup and Twitter referral traffic.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau's own content underperforms despite solid on-page quality, check whether competitors are winning through brand/PR/News-style visibility and branded search rather than through superior content or technical execution — and weigh PR/brand investment, not just more content or technical fixes, as a lever for competitive terms.

**Apply anywhere:** When your own content underperforms despite solid on-page quality, check whether competitors are winning through brand/PR/News-style visibility and branded search rather than through superior content or technical execution — and weigh PR/brand investment, not just more content or technical fixes, as a lever for competitive terms.

### 11. Brand-first companies beat keyword-first companies long-term  `43.14`
*core · content insights · source 43*

Rand contrasts two founder mentalities and states a clear pattern from his own observation. Companies that start from 'we need to build a brand and product that people know, like, and trust, then figure out which channels earn that trust' consistently outperform companies that start from 'there's a big keyword opportunity, how do I rank for these keywords and fill content/information gaps.' He walks through the keyword-first mentality's typical internal logic (find the keyword opportunity, identify information voids, publish enough content, build links to it, hope visitors develop know/like/trust after arriving from the search result) and contrasts it with brand-first thinking that treats know/like/trust as the starting design goal rather than a hoped-for byproduct of ranking. His stated conclusion is that the brand-focus mentality works and the SEO-first mentality tended not to.

> "this SEO-first mentality tended to not work"

**Evidence:** Rand's direct, repeated observation contrasting brand-first vs. keyword-first starting mentalities across companies/founders he's advised or watched, concluding one 'works' and the other 'tended to not work'; connected earlier in the conversation to the Super Bowl-ad anecdote where companies that invested in branding survived long-term better than companies that dominated 5,000 keywords in Google.

**Apply at Pabau:** When planning Pabau's content roadmap, start from what builds know/like/trust with clinic owners and makes them recommend Pabau, rather than starting purely from keyword-gap analysis — use keyword research to find where to deliver the brand message, not as the sole input for what to publish.

**Apply anywhere:** When planning your content roadmap, start from what builds know/like/trust with your actual buyers and makes them recommend you, rather than starting purely from keyword-gap analysis — use keyword research to find where to deliver the brand message, not as the sole input for what to publish.

### 12. Budget for a traffic dip and a six-month conversion lag after switching to BOFU  `136.6`
*core · content insights · source 136*

Grow and Convert are specific about the shape of the transition at Leadfeeder, and it is not flattering. Overall blog traffic dipped after they stopped publishing story content, because pain point SEO posts are not promotable in communities and they had no replacement promotion channel yet. They started publishing pain point posts in spring and summer 2018, went almost exclusively to that format in July and August, and attributed signups only began rising in December 2018. They name the cause plainly: it takes time for the posts to rank and gather enough traffic to move the monthly signup number. After December, conversions rose linearly month over month for over a year, which they call extremely rare in the conversion data they have seen. Anyone planning this switch should expect roughly four to six months of worse-looking dashboards first.

> "we did see an initial dip in overall traffic"

**Evidence:** Leadfeeder: pain point posts began spring/summer 2018, almost exclusive from July-August 2018, attributed signups started climbing December 2018 and rose linearly for over a year to 215-225+ a month.

**Pitfall:** Reporting on pageviews during the transition makes the new strategy look like a failure exactly when it is working. Teams reverse course in month three, right before the ranking curve turns.

**Apply at Pabau:** If David moves pabau.com's calendar toward comparison and alternatives pages, he should tell stakeholders up front that sessions may fall for a quarter and that signup lift lands around month four to six. Switch the reporting headline to demo bookings per article before the switch, not after the dip.

**Apply anywhere:** Expect blog traffic to fall and conversions to stay flat for roughly four to six months after moving from top-funnel to bottom-funnel content. Agree the lag and the conversion-based reporting metric with stakeholders before you start, so the dip does not end the experiment.

### 13. Budget just over a year before a post earns its first-page ranking  `153.1`
*core · best practices · source 153*

Grow and Convert analyzed over 40 clients and plotted first-page rankings divided by number of posts published. The curve crosses roughly 1.00 around month 15, meaning each published post averages about one top-10 ranking a little over a year after publishing began. They also report an average of more than 100 first-page rankings after three years of publishing, and about 20 first-page rankings by month 10. Their cadence is three articles a month, each targeting a single keyword. The number to plan against is the ratio, not the raw count, because raw rankings rise simply because more posts exist. They call time the single most crucial factor, ahead of domain strength.

> "around month 15, each post averages close to one first-page ranking"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert data across 40+ clients: ~20 first-page rankings by month 10, rankings-per-post crossing 1.00 around month 15, and 100+ first-page rankings after three years.

**How to do it**

1. Log every published post with its URL, publish date and single target keyword in one sheet.
2. Track top-10 rankings monthly with a rank tracker, counting the top 10 results rather than a literal page one.
3. Each month divide total first-page rankings by number of posts published to date to get rankings per post.
4. Chart that ratio against months of publishing, not against calendar quarters.
5. Set the internal expectation at roughly 1.00 rankings per post by month 15, not month 3.
6. Allow that the ratio can exceed 1.00 because some posts rank for several target keywords.
7. Review any post still outside the top 20 at 12 months as an individual case rather than treating the whole program as failing.
8. Report the ratio to stakeholders every quarter so early flat months are read as normal.

**Tools:** Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Judging a content program at month three or six. The ratio is near zero early by design, so an honest program looks like a failure exactly when leadership first asks for numbers.

**Apply at Pabau:** David should set the Pabau blog's ranking expectation in rankings-per-post and hold new articles for at least 12 months before judging them. Build a sheet of every /blog/ and /templates/ URL with publish date and target keyword, and report the ratio quarterly rather than raw traffic.

**Apply anywhere:** Set your content program's expectation in first-page rankings per published post, and give each post at least 12 months. Track publish date and target keyword for every URL, then chart the ratio monthly so early flat results are read as normal rather than as failure.

### 14. Build content because CPCs always inflate: the law of shitty clickthroughs  `68.20`
*core · general insights · source 68*

Cody's justification for content marketing is a cost argument, not a traffic one. Ad costs in any category rise as the market gets sophisticated, and he points at any space taking VC money as the clearest example: CPCs spike overnight. His numbers, current at the time of recording, are $16 CPCs around AI SEO software and $11 for 'AI business intelligence software', which he says he cannot compete on for Graphed and which were nowhere near that level before the money arrived. The mechanism he cites is Andrew Chen's law of shitty clickthroughs from The Cold Start Problem: someone finds arbitrage in a channel, others see it, more money enters, ad inventory is fixed, so the price rises. Content is the hedge. More content surface area means more top of funnel, more branded search, more word of mouth and more direct arrivals, which keeps blended acquisition cost flat while paid cost climbs. The strategic point is that you build the content before you need it.

> "the law of shitty clickthroughs"

**Evidence:** CPCs of about $16 for AI SEO software and $11 for AI business intelligence software, both far below those levels before VC money entered the categories.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the current CPC for your two or three most commercially valuable keywords and record it as a baseline.
2. Re-check those CPCs quarterly and chart the trend rather than the absolute number.
3. Check whether competitors in your category have recently taken funding, since that predicts the spike.
4. Size content investment against the projected paid cost 12 to 24 months out, not today's.
5. Shift targeting from generic category keywords toward your own brand terms, which nobody else can bid up meaningfully.
6. Track branded search impressions and clicks in Search Console as the measure that the hedge is working.
7. Read The Cold Start Problem by Andrew Chen for the underlying model.

**Tools:** Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Starting content only once paid has become unaffordable means paying the inflated CPC for the 12 months it takes the content to compound. The hedge has to be built before you need it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Practice management software is a funded category, so Pabau should expect CPCs on generic terms to keep rising and should chart them quarterly. The blog and template pages are the hedge, and branded search is the metric that shows it working.

**Apply anywhere:** Ad costs rise in every category as money enters and inventory stays fixed. Build content surface area and brand demand ahead of that curve, and measure success as rising branded search rather than as content traffic.

### 15. Buy a tool only to speed a process that already produces results  `175.2`
*core · best practices · source 175*

The central rule in the piece is that tools optimize processes, they do not solve problems. The author's own worked example: if content marketing is already driving traffic and leads, and lead routing to sales is the bottleneck, then marketing automation, or even just Zapier or IFTTT to push data between systems, is a sensible purchase. If no inbound traffic or leads exist and no strategy exists, buying HubSpot, Marketo or Pardot will not improve the program. The test is directional. The process must already work at some small scale before a tool is layered on it. Applied to SEO, this means do not buy a rank tracker, a content optimizer or an AI writing suite to create a content operation that does not exist yet.

> "tools don't solve problems. They optimize processes"

**Evidence:** The author's example contrasts buying marketing automation on top of a working content program against buying HubSpot, Marketo or Pardot with no inbound traffic or strategy.

**How to do it**

1. Before any purchase, write down the process the tool will speed up and confirm it is already running manually.
2. Show a result from that manual process: leads, rankings, published pieces, whatever the tool would multiply.
3. If there is no manual version and no result, stop and build the process by hand first.
4. Where the need is only moving data between systems, price Zapier or IFTTT before a full platform.
5. Size the purchase to the bottleneck, not the whole job function.
6. Set a review date and a metric the tool must improve, such as hours saved or leads routed per week.
7. Cancel at the review if the metric did not move.

**Tools:** Zapier, IFTTT, HubSpot, Marketo, Pardot

**Pitfall:** Buying an enterprise platform as a substitute for a strategy. The failure signal is a tool that sits configured but idle because there is no traffic, no leads and no content flowing through it.

**Apply at Pabau:** Before Pabau's marketing team adds another SEO or AI tool, David should require a one-line note naming the process it accelerates and the result that process already produces. Anything bought to start a process rather than speed one is deferred.

**Apply anywhere:** Require a one-line note before any tool purchase naming the process it accelerates and the result that process already produces. Defer anything bought to start a process rather than speed one.

### 16. Buy an exact-match .com and rank it with ten exact-anchor links in 30 days  `68.19`
*core · concrete actions · source 68*

> "buy those exact match domains is because they're extremely easy to rank"

### 17. Buy every brand exact-match domain before announcing the brand name  `67.1`
*core · concrete actions · source 67*

Dirk Schembri treats exact-match domain buying as brand protection first, ranking tactic second. > "before making even your new name public, I would I would always emphasize make sure you buy the domains"

**How to do it**

### 18. Chase volume only if pageviews are the product you sell  `138.9`
*core · general insights · source 138*

Nat Eliason's first recap lesson draws a clean line. Chasing search volume is a fine SEO strategy for media sites, which monetize pageviews directly through display ads. It does not make sense as a content strategy for a business with a separate product or service, because a pageview only has value if it moves toward that product. This is the reasoning behind everything else in the case study: the 60,000-visitor post with $0 in sales is a success under a media-site model and a failure under a product model. The test is what your revenue line is denominated in. If it is impressions, optimize traffic. If it is orders or subscriptions, optimize the path to them and accept smaller numbers on the traffic chart.

> "That's a fine SEO strategy for media sites"

**Evidence:** Cup & Leaf grew SEO sales more than 300% in two months while traffic grew about 175%, so revenue outran traffic by a wide margin once topics were chosen for intent.

**Pitfall:** Content teams inherit media-site KPIs because traffic is the easiest number to report. The team then optimizes correctly against the wrong objective, and the traffic chart looks healthy while revenue does not move.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog reporting should lead with demo requests per article, with sessions as a secondary line. Pabau does not sell pageviews, so a traffic-led dashboard rewards the wrong articles.

### 19. Check both ROI assumptions hold before trusting any SEO model  `129.16`
*core · best practices · source 129*

Grow and Convert attach two explicit preconditions to their whole spend-versus-value curve, and say the ideal long-term shape only holds if both are true. First, there is good keyword search volume and conversion potential in your space, meaning the potential value exceeds the likely costs. Second, the SEO strategy targets buying-intent keywords that drive actual revenue, not brand awareness, traffic-chasing or low-intent terms. The second one is the trap, because a program can pass the value model and then fail in execution by spending the budget on high-volume informational keywords. They treat the first assumption as the subject of the ROI article and the second as their foundational strategy work, but the point for anyone building a business case is that the model is void if either assumption is unchecked.

> "There's good keyword search volume and conversion potential in your space"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert state the ROI curve holds under two assumptions: real search volume and conversion potential in the space, and a strategy focused on buying-intent keywords rather than awareness or traffic.

**How to do it**

1. Write both assumptions at the top of the ROI model as a checklist, not as background.
2. Verify assumption one with the keyword count and volume floors for your business model.
3. Verify assumption two by listing the actual keywords the budget will target and marking each as buying intent or not.
4. Reject the model if more than a small share of the target list is brand-awareness or traffic-chasing keywords.
5. Rewrite the target list to lead with buying intent before signing off the spend.
6. Re-audit the list every quarter, because keyword lists drift toward volume over time.
7. If results miss the model, check which assumption broke before changing the forecast.

**Tools:** Spreadsheet (Sheets/Excel), Ahrefs

**Pitfall:** Passing the value test and then failing on execution. The signal is a content calendar full of high-volume informational topics while the modeled ROI assumed high-intent conversion rates.

**Apply at Pabau:** When Pabau signs off a page family, list the keywords the pages will target and mark each as buying intent or not. A family that drifts toward general aesthetic-industry topics should be re-scoped before production starts, not after the results come in flat.

**Apply anywhere:** Put both ROI assumptions on the model as a checklist: real search volume and conversion potential in the space, and a keyword list actually aimed at buying intent. Verify the second by marking every target keyword, and re-scope the list before signing off spend if awareness terms dominate.

### 20. Choose bottom-funnel or top-funnel by the client's existing domain authority  `141.3`
*core · concrete actions · source 141*

Grow and Convert started their agency on the hypothesis that a new account opens with top-of-funnel content that earns links, then circles back to bottom-of-funnel once authority exists. They now split that by starting authority. For a genuinely brand-new blog or domain with no authority, they still call the link-earning top-funnel opening a good strategy. But most of their clients sit at domain authorities between 25 and 60 or above, and for those they say starting on bottom-of-funnel long-tail high-conversion-intent content gets results faster. They add a second test on top of the authority band: whether the category already has demand. For the subscription client they skipped top-funnel entirely because the client sold a known service with existing search demand and was not creating a new category. Top-funnel would only make sense if heavy category education were needed and the bottom and middle funnel terms were already exhausted.

> "domain authorities ranging between 25-60+, we believe starting on bottom of the funnel"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's clients typically sit at DA 25-60+, and on that basis they moved their standard opening from top-funnel link bait to bottom-funnel long-tail content.

**How to do it**

1. Pull the site's domain authority or domain rating before planning any content.
2. If it sits at roughly 25 or above, plan the first batch entirely as bottom-funnel long-tail high-intent terms.
3. If it is a brand-new domain near zero, open instead with link-earning top-funnel pieces and revisit bottom-funnel once referring domains start arriving.
4. Apply the second test regardless of authority: check whether the category already has search demand for high-intent terms.
5. If demand exists and you are not creating a new category, skip top-funnel content entirely for now.
6. Only schedule top-funnel work once the bottom and middle funnel term list is exhausted, or the category genuinely needs education before anyone buys.
7. Re-run both tests at each quarterly plan rather than locking the split for the year.

**Tools:** Ahrefs, Moz

**Pitfall:** Copying the link-first top-funnel playbook onto a site that already has authority wastes the fastest months of an engagement; the tell is high pageviews with no signups by month three.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's domain is well past the 25 threshold, so David should keep the plan weighted to bottom-funnel practice-management terms and comparison pages. Aesthetic and healthcare practice software has established demand, so category-education articles are not the priority.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide the funnel split from your starting authority and your category's existing demand. Above roughly DA 25, open with bottom-funnel long-tail terms. On a brand-new domain, open with link-earning top-funnel content instead. Skip category education entirely if buyers already search for what you sell.

### 21. Commit content to one goal: traffic and awareness, or leads and sales  `102.1`
*core · best practices · source 102*

Grow and Convert open their strategy framework by forcing a choice between two goals: traffic and brand awareness growth, or leads and sales growth. They say the two require very different strategies, and that most companies default to the first while assuming it will eventually produce the second. Their experience across dozens of clients over five years is that it usually does not: traffic-first content drives very few actual customers. They allow that traffic-first is a reasonable investment for large brands with existing market dominance, but say small businesses and anyone expected to show measurable ROI should design for leads and sales from the start. The goal choice then determines topic selection, which they call the most important part of the strategy.

> "these two content marketing goals require very different strategies"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert report five-plus years of agency work across dozens of businesses where traffic-focused content drove very few customers.

**How to do it**

1. Write down which single goal the content program is accountable for: traffic and brand awareness, or leads and sales.
2. Ask who will judge the program and on what number; if the answer is pipeline or revenue, pick the leads-and-sales goal.
3. Reject the assumption that traffic will convert later unless you can name the mechanism (email list, retargeting) and show it has worked before.
4. Set the reporting metric to match the goal before any topic is chosen, so measurement cannot drift to pageviews.
5. Only pursue the traffic goal deliberately, when the brand already dominates its market and can fund unattributed awareness.
6. Re-run this choice each planning cycle and re-plan the topic list if the goal changes.

**Pitfall:** Picking the traffic goal by default, then being asked for lead numbers a year later. The signal is a large content library that, when measured, produces almost no demos, trials or sales.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's blog should be split explicitly into a leads-and-sales set (software comparison, pricing, workflow and compliance topics tied to what the platform does) and a smaller awareness set. Report demo requests per article, not sessions.

**Apply anywhere:** Decide up front whether your content is accountable for traffic or for leads, and let that decision pick your topics and your reporting metric. Most sites should choose leads and report conversions per article rather than sessions.

### 22. Compare two content bets on conversions, not traffic, before doubling down  `176.2`
*core · concrete actions · source 176*

Grow and Convert describe the decision that produced Pain Point SEO. Their first bet was interview-based stories shared on social, which grew client traffic a lot and very quickly. Their second, almost accidental, bet was a handful of SEO articles targeting what they would later call bottom-of-funnel terms. When they stepped back and compared the two on conversions rather than traffic, the stories produced barely any conversions while the SEO pieces drove a ton, and kept driving traffic because they ranked. That comparison, not a new idea, is what let them commit. The procedure generalizes: run two approaches long enough to measure, then judge them on the business outcome you started the company or the channel to produce.

> "many of these stories produced barely any conversions"

**Evidence:** Grow and Convert's interview stories grew traffic fast but produced almost no conversions; the few bottom-of-funnel SEO pieces converted heavily and kept ranking.

**How to do it**

1. Write down the single business outcome the content channel exists to produce, such as demo requests.
2. Tag every published piece with which approach produced it.
3. Wait until the SEO pieces have ranked; short-lived social spikes and ranked pages are not comparable before then.
4. Pull traffic and the business outcome per piece for each approach side by side.
5. Rank the approaches on the business outcome only, and treat the traffic column as context.
6. Kill the losing approach outright rather than running both at half budget.
7. Give the winner a name and a documented process so the whole team executes it the same way.

**Tools:** Google Analytics, Google Search Console

**Pitfall:** Judging too early. Social-driven stories peak in days while SEO pieces need months to rank, so an early read hands the win to the approach that will die out.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau should compare its blog formats on demo requests per article, not sessions. Split the report by page type — /templates/, code-reference pages, and standard blog posts — and fund the type that actually books demos.

**Apply anywhere:** Compare your content formats on the business outcome per piece rather than on sessions, and only after the SEO pieces have had time to rank. Then defund the loser entirely.

### 23. Decide article versus landing page by reading each keyword's SERP first  `131.7`
*core · concrete actions · source 131*

Before writing anything for Smartlook, Grow and Convert analyzed the SERP for each target keyword and decided per keyword what format searchers would be best served by. For the competitor and alternatives terms they concluded an in-depth blog post beat a landing page, because it gave room to dig into the nuances between Smartlook's features and each competitor's while showing genuine expertise in the product analytics space. For 'tracking user activity on website', reading the SERP told them searchers wanted tools that track specific interactions such as button clicks and text inputs, and explain why users behaved that way, which mapped directly onto Smartlook's advanced features. The SERP read is doing two jobs at once: choosing the format and defining what the piece must contain.

> "we determined that searchers would be best served by an in-depth blog post"

**Evidence:** Smartlook's SERP-led format choice moved competitor keywords from outside the top 100 on landing pages to first-page rankings on posts.

**How to do it**

1. Search each target keyword and record the format of the top 10 results, splitting them into articles, roundups, landing pages and docs.
2. If articles dominate, plan an article; if product pages dominate, plan a landing page.
3. Open the top three results and list the specific questions each one answers.
4. Write down the concrete thing the searcher wants, in one sentence, such as tracking button clicks and text inputs and explaining why users behaved that way.
5. Map that sentence onto the exact features of your product that satisfy it.
6. Brief the piece so the mapped features are demonstrated with screenshots, not just claimed.
7. Check the ranking pages for the comparison depth they include and set that as the minimum for your draft.
8. Re-check the SERP before any refresh, since the dominant format can change.

**Pitfall:** Choosing the format from house preference rather than the SERP. Publishing a landing page into an article SERP is the specific failure that kept Smartlook's competitor pages out of the top 100.

**Apply at Pabau:** Pabau's /SEO flow should record the format split of the top 10 for every target keyword in the brief, and let that decide whether the answer is a /blog/ article, a /templates/ page or a product page.

**Apply anywhere:** Read the SERP for each target keyword before briefing. Let the dominant result format choose article or landing page, and let the top three results define the specific questions the piece must answer.
